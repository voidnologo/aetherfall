# Chapter 2

The foundry hunched against the sky like something that had died standing up.

Kael had walked past it a hundred times in his Greycoat years and never really looked at it. It was three storeys of soot-black brick and riveted iron under a roof of cracked glass, with two great chimneys that hadn't breathed in two years. Ashworth had built it in the first boom after the Tear, when every month brought a new kind of engine. When the work moved further west into newer halls, the foundry closed. Now it stood on the edge of the Factory Quarter with its windows dark, waiting for a demolition crew that never came.

They approached it the next morning from the south, along the canal path, in ones and twos. Kael went first and alone, a working man with his hands in his pockets.

The hum was strong here. He'd felt it as a constable on this end of his beat: a steady vibration in the back teeth and the bones of the jaw, like standing too close to a tram rail. It didn't bother him. If anything it steadied him. His revolver sat on his hip under the jacket, solid and heavy and ready. A good machine in a place that liked machines.

Sera was having a worse time of it. As they came off the canal path she'd gone pale and quiet. She kept swallowing.

"You all right?" he'd asked.

"It's like being in a room with the windows painted shut," she'd said. "I'll be fine. I just can't hear anything."

Aldric had touched his satchel, where the spellbook lived, and frowned. "Heavy," he'd murmured. "Everything's heavier here."

Mira, of course, looked delighted. Her arc pistol was holstered under her arm. Twice he'd watched her check its little brass charge needle. Both times it sat fat and happy at the top of the dial.

---

The loading bay faced the street behind a high fence of iron palings. The gates were shut. A chain hung on the great doors behind them, bright new steel of the sort you bought by the yard at a chandler's, threaded through hasps rusted orange by two years of rain.

A sign on the fence read ASHWORTH INDUSTRIAL — DECOMMISSIONED — NO TRESPASSING — BY ORDER OF THE GREYCOAT AUTHORITY. Underneath, someone had chalked NOT EMPTY in letters a foot high.

"Friendly," said Mira, coming up beside him.

Inside the fence, two men in work clothes and flat caps sat on crates by the loading bay door, eating lunch out of paper: bread, cheese, an onion each. At a glance they were demolition men. Kael looked longer. Their boots were too clean for demolition, and so were their hands. When the bigger one leaned to reach his tea, his jacket pulled under the left arm in a shape Kael had seen on a thousand men in a thousand alleys.

"Revolvers," he said. "Both of them, left side. The little one has a knife in his boot."

"Thank you, Kael." Mira took a folded paper from her coat. It was a Greycoat inspection docket she'd filled in the night before, on a blank she'd kept in a drawer for longer than Kael wanted to know about. Her face changed to a clerk's, brisk and bored, with a quota to meet. "Wait here. Look like a surveyor."

He didn't know how to look like a surveyor. He looked at the chimneys instead.

She went through the gate as if it wasn't locked. It wasn't; the chain was on the inside doors. She walked up to the men on the crates with her docket held out and started talking. He couldn't hear the words, but he could read the big one's shoulders. They were high and tight at first. Little by little they came down. The small one laughed at something. Then the big one got up and fished in his pocket for the key to the chain.

*She's good*, he thought. *God, she's good.*

Aldric had been standing beside him with Harker's journal under his arm, doing his best impression of a surveyor. Now he looked past the open door into the dim cavern of the main hall, and his eyes went straight to the thing he most wanted to see: the far end, the east end, where an iron service stair went down through the floor.

The big man saw him look.

Kael saw the big man's face change. The bread dropped out of his hand, and the hand went under the jacket.

Kael was already moving.

---

Fights always went fast and then very slow.

Afterwards Sera asked him about it. He tried to explain and found he couldn't put it in order, because it hadn't happened in order. It had happened all at once, in layers. Eighteen years had taught him one trick: let the others commit first. Watch who's going where. Then choose.

The small man moved first. He was the jumpy one, as Kael had known he would be. He came off his crate with the boot knife already out and went for Mira, because she was closest. At that range a knife was quicker than any gun. It was the first thing they taught you at Coldwater. Mira went sideways like a cat off a wall, and the blade cut air where her ribs had been.

The big one had his revolver half out. He was going for Aldric, who stood in the open gateway with a book under his arm.

Behind Kael, Sera threw out her hand.

He felt her do it, a pressure in the air by his ear. Then he felt it fail. It went out of her in the right direction and ran out of road, water spilling across a hot pavement. The big man's coat flapped in what might have been a gust. Sera made a sound of pure fury.

Then Mira's arc pistol spoke.

Kael had heard it fired in practice on the waste ground behind the gasworks. It still made him flinch. There was no bang, only a vicious tearing *crack* like a whip made of glass. A line of blue-white light joined muzzle and target, so bright it left a purple stripe across his vision. The small man went backwards off his feet into a stack of salvage with his shirt smoking. He lay there twitching and swearing. In this air the weapon was a monster; the hum fed it. Mira looked at it in her own hand with something close to alarm.

The big man fired at Aldric.

Kael fired at the big man.

He fired a fraction later, because he'd waited to see, and a fraction later was enough. The revolver bucked in his hand, clean and sure as it had been on every range and every street for seven years. The big man's shot went into the brickwork a foot from Aldric's head and threw red dust over him. The big man spun half round, clutching his right arm above the elbow. His gun clattered on the cobbles.

"Down," said Kael. "Down on the ground. Now."

The big man didn't go down. He went for the gun with his left hand.

Kael would remember what happened next long after he'd forgotten the shot.

The man bent for his revolver and he was slow. He didn't mean to be. He was big and strong and capable, and a minute ago he'd have scooped that gun off the ground in one motion. Now he fumbled it. His right arm hung. Blood ran down into his sleeve and dripped off his fingers, and his face had gone a strange yellowish white. When he got the gun into his left hand it shook too badly to line up. He tried to stand and his knees wobbled. He tried to aim and the muzzle wandered across the yard like a drunk looking for a door. Every second he got worse, and the fright of it made him worse again.

Kael had seen it before and never got used to it: a man coming apart one piece at a time, from one hole in his arm.

"Put it down," he said again, softer. "You're done. Look at yourself. You're done."

The big man looked at the blood dripping off his fingers. He looked at Kael. He put the gun down.

---

Aldric tied off the arm with his own necktie and a stick. He knelt in the yard with brick dust in his hair. His hands were not entirely steady.

"You could—" Sera began, and stopped.

"Not here." Aldric didn't look up. "I could try. In this air it would be like threading a needle in mittens. I'd spend everything I have for half a result, and I think I'm going to need everything I have later." He pulled the knot tight. The big man hissed. "This will hold. It's not a bad wound. He'll keep the arm."

"Thanks," said the big man through his teeth. It surprised everyone, himself included.

The small man had stopped twitching and started groaning, which Aldric said was a good sign. His shirt was scorched in a fist-sized patch over red, blistered skin. Every hair on his arms stood straight up and wouldn't lie down.

Mira crouched in front of the big one. She'd put the arc pistol away. Her face was friendly again, warmer than the clerk's and more dangerous: the face of somebody who'd be on your side if you'd only let her.

"You're Syndicate," she said.

He looked at the cobbles.

"It's all right. We're not Greycoats. Nobody here wants to see you in front of a magistrate. We want the Scholar." She waited. "Thin man. Round spectacles. Terrible at cards."

The big man laughed despite himself, a short, pained sound. "Took six marks off him the first night," he said. "Bloody awful player." The laugh went out of him. "He's downstairs. He's all right. Nobody's hurt him. Boss said hold him till the shipment moves. After that I don't know. After that it's not my business."

"When does the shipment move?"

"Tonight. Last load." He shifted his arm and winced. "Crates. Heavy ones. They come up out of the tunnels on a handcart and go out the storm drain on the west side to the canal. There's something in 'em, I don't know what. You stand near 'em too long, your teeth itch. Your fillings go warm."

"Who's down there?"

"Three. Two lads like us." He paused. "And her. Corva." He lowered his voice on the name, as men do with a foreman they fear and respect. "You don't want to make a mess with her. She doesn't like mess."

"Tunnels," said Aldric suddenly. He'd been listening with his head on one side. "You said tunnels. Not a cellar."

"Old drains. Under the whole quarter. They go east, under the border, into the old town. Over that side they're..." The big man shook his head. "Over that side they're wrong. You'll see."

---

They tied the two of them to the iron palings with their own belts, not too tight, and left them their lunch. Kael put the big man's revolver in his pocket and the small man's knife in his boot. Mira tucked a half-mark coin into the big man's top pocket. When he looked at her, she shrugged.

"For the tie," she said. "Aldric liked that tie."

Then they went into the dark of the main hall and along it, between the dead shapes of the machines, to the east end where the iron stair went down into the floor.

Kael went first. After this he would always go first, though he didn't know that yet.

He stopped at the top step. The stairwell spiralled down into blackness, and the air that came up out of it was not the foundry's air. It was warm and thick. It smelled of rain on hot stone and old pennies, with something sweet underneath, like cut grass in a room where no grass had ever grown.

Behind him Sera drew a long, deep breath, like a swimmer coming up.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, there it is."
