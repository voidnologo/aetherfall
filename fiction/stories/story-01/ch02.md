# Chapter 2

The foundry hunched against the sky like something that had died standing up.

Kael had walked past it a hundred times in his Greycoat years and never really looked at it. Three storeys of soot-black brick and riveted iron, a roof of cracked glass panes in iron frames, two great chimneys that hadn't breathed in two years. Ashworth had built it in the first boom after the Tear, when every month brought a new kind of engine, and shut it down when the work moved further west into newer halls. Now it just stood there on the edge of the Factory Quarter with its windows dark, waiting to be pulled down by somebody who never came.

They came at it the next morning from the south, along the canal path, in ones and twos. Kael went first, alone, a working man with his hands in his pockets.

The hum was strong here. He'd always felt it as a constable, walking this end of his beat — a steady vibration in the back teeth, in the bones of the jaw, like standing too close to a tram rail. It didn't bother him. If anything it steadied him. His revolver sat on his hip under the jacket, and here, in this air, it felt the way it always felt: solid, heavy, ready. A good machine in a place that liked machines.

Behind him, Sera was having a worse time of it. He'd seen her face as they came off the canal path. She'd gone pale and quiet and kept swallowing, as if she were trying to breathe through a wet cloth.

"You all right?" he'd asked.

"It's like being in a room with the windows painted shut," she'd said. "I'll be fine. I just can't hear anything."

Aldric had touched his satchel, where his spellbook lived, and frowned at it. "Heavy," he'd murmured. "Everything's heavier here."

Mira, of course, looked delighted. Her arc pistol was holstered under her arm, and he'd watched her check its little brass charge needle twice, and both times it had been sitting fat and happy right up at the top of its dial.

---

The loading bay faced the street behind a high fence of iron palings. The gates were shut. On the great doors behind them hung a chain — and the chain was bright steel, new, the kind you bought by the yard at a chandler's, threaded through hasps rusted orange with two years of rain.

A sign on the fence read ASHWORTH INDUSTRIAL — DECOMMISSIONED — NO TRESPASSING — BY ORDER OF THE GREYCOAT AUTHORITY. Underneath, someone had scrawled in chalk, in letters a foot high: NOT EMPTY.

"Friendly," said Mira, coming up beside him.

Inside the fence, two men in work clothes and flat caps sat on a pair of crates by the loading bay door, eating their lunch out of paper. Bread, cheese, an onion each. They had the look of demolition men, if you didn't look long. Kael looked long. Their boots were too clean for demolition. Their hands were too clean. And under the left arm of the bigger one, when he leaned to reach his tea, the jacket pulled in a shape Kael had seen on a thousand men in a thousand alleys.

"Revolvers," he said quietly. "Both. Left side, both of them. The little one has a knife in his boot."

"Thank you, Kael." Mira took a folded paper out of her coat — the Greycoat inspection docket she'd drawn up the night before on a blank she'd had in a drawer for longer than Kael wanted to know about — and put on a different face, brisk and bored, a clerk with a clipboard and a quota. "Wait here. Look like a surveyor."

He didn't know how to look like a surveyor. He looked at the chimneys instead.

He watched her go through the gate as if it wasn't locked — it wasn't; the chain was on the inside doors, not the gate — and up to the men on the crates with her docket held out in front of her, and start talking. He couldn't hear the words. He didn't need to. He watched the big one's shoulders, and they were high and tight at first, and then, little by little, they came down. He watched the small one laugh at something. He watched the big one get up and fish in his pocket for the key to the chain.

*She's good*, he thought, not for the first time. *God, she's good.*

Then Aldric, who'd been standing beside him with Harker's journal under his arm doing his best impression of a surveyor, looked past the open door into the dim cavern of the main hall — and his eyes went, the way a scholar's eyes go, straight to the place he was most interested in. The far end. The east end. Where, in the gloom, an iron service stair went down through the floor.

The big man saw him look.

Kael saw the big man see it. He saw his face change. He saw the bread drop out of his hand, and the hand go under the jacket.

Kael was already moving.

---

It went the way fights always went, which was fast and then very slow.

Afterwards, he would try to explain it to Sera, who asked, and found he couldn't make it make sense in order, because it hadn't happened in order. It had happened all at once in layers. The trick — the thing eighteen years had taught him — was to let the others commit first. Watch. See who's going where. Then choose.

The small man went first. He was the jumpy one; Kael had known he would be. He came off his crate with the boot knife already in his hand and went for Mira, because Mira was closest, and the knife was quick, quicker than any gun — a knife was always quicker than a gun, at that range; that was the first thing they taught you at Coldwater. Mira saw him coming and went sideways like a cat off a wall, and the blade cut air where her ribs had been.

The big one had his revolver half out. He was going for Aldric, who was standing in the gateway with a book under his arm like a target in a shooting gallery.

Sera, behind Kael, threw out her hand.

He felt her do it — a pressure in the air by his ear, like the moment before a door slams in a draught — and then he felt it fail. It went out of her in the right direction and simply ran out of road, the way water runs out across a hot pavement. The big man's coat flapped as though a gust had caught it. That was all. Sera made a sound of pure fury.

Then Mira's arc pistol spoke.

Kael had heard it fired before, in practice, out on the waste ground behind the gasworks, and it still made him flinch: not a bang but a vicious, tearing *crack*, like a whip made of glass, and a line of blue-white light between the muzzle and the target so bright it left a purple stripe across his vision. The small man went backwards off his feet into a stack of salvage with his shirt smoking, and lay there twitching and swearing. In here, in this air, the thing was a monster. The hum fed it. Mira looked at it in her own hand with something like alarm.

The big man fired at Aldric.

Kael fired at the big man.

He fired a fraction later, because he'd waited to see, and a fraction later was enough. The revolver bucked in his hand, clean and sure, the way it had on every range and every street for seven years, and the big man's shot went into the brickwork a foot from Aldric's head and threw red dust over him, and the big man spun half round, clutching his right arm above the elbow, and his gun clattered on the cobbles.

"Down," said Kael. "Down on the ground. Now."

The big man didn't go down. He went for the gun with his left hand.

That was the part Kael would remember. Not the shot. The after.

The man bent for his revolver and he was slow. Not because he wanted to be — Kael could see he didn't. He was a big, strong, capable man, and a minute ago he'd have scooped that gun up off the ground in one smooth motion. Now he fumbled it. His right arm hung, and the blood ran down it into his sleeve and dripped off his fingers, and his face had gone a strange yellowish white, and when he got the gun in his left hand it shook so badly he couldn't line it up. He tried to stand and his knees wobbled. He tried to aim and the muzzle wandered across the yard like a drunk looking for a door. Every second he got worse. Every second he was less of what he'd been, and more frightened about it, and the fright made him worse again.

Kael had seen it before. He'd never got used to it. A man coming apart, one piece at a time, from one hole in his arm.

"Put it down," he said again, softer. "You're done. Look at yourself. You're done."

The big man looked at the blood dripping off his fingers. He looked at Kael. He put the gun down.

---

Aldric tied off the arm with his own necktie and a stick, kneeling in the yard with brick dust in his hair and his hands not entirely steady.

"You could—" Sera began, and stopped.

"Not here." Aldric didn't look up. "I could try. In this air it would be like — threading a needle in mittens. I'd spend everything I have for half a result. And I think I'm going to need everything I have, later." He pulled the knot tight. The big man hissed. "This will hold. It's not a bad wound. He'll keep the arm."

"Thanks," said the big man, through his teeth, surprising everyone, himself included.

The small man had stopped twitching and started groaning, which Aldric said was a good sign. His shirt was scorched in a fist-sized patch and the skin under it was blistered and red, and every hair on his arms was standing straight up and wouldn't lie down.

Mira crouched in front of the big one. She'd put the arc pistol away. Her face was friendly again — not the clerk now, something warmer and more dangerous, the face of somebody who was on your side if you'd only let her be.

"You're Syndicate," she said. Not a question.

He looked at the cobbles.

"It's all right. We're not Greycoats. Nobody here wants to see you in front of a magistrate. We want the Scholar." She waited. "Thin man. Round spectacles. Terrible at cards."

The big man laughed, despite himself — a short, pained sound. "Took six marks off him the first night," he said. "Bloody awful player." Then the laugh went out of him. "He's downstairs. He's all right. Nobody's hurt him. Boss said hold him till the shipment moves, then — I don't know. Then it's not my business."

"When does the shipment move?"

"Tonight. Last load." He shifted his arm and winced. "Crates. Heavy ones. They come up out of the tunnels on a handcart and go out the storm drain on the west side to the canal. There's something in 'em. I don't know what. You stand near 'em too long, your teeth itch. Your fillings go warm."

"Who's down there?"

"Three. Two lads like us." A pause. "And her. Corva." He said the name the way men say the names of foremen they're afraid of and respect. "She's — you don't want to make a mess with her. She doesn't like mess."

"Tunnels," said Aldric suddenly. He'd been listening with his head on one side. "You said tunnels. Not a cellar."

"Old drains. Under the whole quarter. They go east, under the border, into the old town. Over that side they're—" the big man shook his head. "Over that side they're wrong. You'll see."

---

They tied the two of them to the iron palings with their own belts, not too tight, and left them their lunch. Kael put the big man's revolver in his pocket and the small man's knife in his boot. Mira tucked a half-mark coin into the big man's top pocket, and when he looked at her, she shrugged.

"For the tie," she said. "Aldric liked that tie."

Then they went into the dark of the main hall, and along it, between the dead shapes of the machines, to the east end, where the iron service stair went down into the floor.

Kael went first. He'd always go first, after this. He didn't know that yet.

He stopped at the top step. The stairwell spiralled down into blackness. And up out of it, against his face, came air — and it was not the air of the foundry. It was warm. It was thick. It smelled of rain on hot stone and of old pennies and of something sweet underneath, like cut grass in a room where no grass had ever grown.

Behind him he heard Sera draw in a long, deep, astonished breath, like a swimmer coming up.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, there it is."
