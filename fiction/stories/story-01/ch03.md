# Chapter 3

Aldric counted the steps going down. He counted things when he was nervous, and someone ought to.

There were forty-one, an iron spiral bolted into a brick shaft so narrow his shoulders brushed both sides. At the twelfth step the hum began to fade. By the twentieth, the headache he'd carried since the canal path had loosened its grip. It was the dull, grinding pressure behind the eyes that every caster paid for standing in a Galvanic district. At the thirtieth it was gone.

At the thirty-fifth, his satchel went light.

He stopped with one hand on the rail and put the other on the satchel's flap. Up in the foundry the spellbook had felt like a book made of lead. Reading it would have been like reading in a dream. Now it was just a book. Less than that, even: it was eager. The pages seemed to stir under his palm like a dog that has heard its name.

"You feel that?" Sera whispered from the step above. There was a lift in her voice he'd never heard before.

"Yes," said Aldric. "Yes, I do."

At the bottom, Mira had her arc pistol out and was frowning at it. The little brass charge needle that had sat so fat and full in the yard was trembling. As he watched, it dropped a quarter of the dial, climbed back, and dropped again.

"That's not good," she said.

"No," Aldric agreed. "It's not built for this air. Nothing Galvanic is." He looked at Kael. "Your revolver ought to be all right. It's only springs and powder."

Kael didn't answer. He had drawn the revolver and was holding it out in front of him. He turned it over once, tapped the cylinder twice with his thumb, and frowned at it. A man might frown like that at a watch that had started to tick a fraction too loud.

Then he put it away and went on. Aldric forgot about it until much later.

---

The tunnels began at the bottom of the stair.

They had been drains once; it showed. The arched brick corridor was tall enough to walk without stooping. A thin stream of water still ran in a channel down the middle. Iron rungs were set into the walls at intervals, where workmen had once climbed up to manholes long since paved over. It was pre-Tear work, solid and municipal. Somebody had laid every one of these bricks with a trowel, had a cup of tea afterwards, and gone home.

About twenty paces in, the bricks began to glow.

Only some of them, and only faintly. The condensation running down the walls carried a weak blue light, as if the water had been mixed with moonlight. In the corners where the arches met the walls, something was growing: clusters of thin, pale crystals, finger-long and sharp as glass, sprouting from the mortar like frost on a window pane. They gave off a soft, cold light of their own. As Aldric watched, it brightened and dimmed and brightened, slow and steady.

Like breathing, he thought. Or a pulse.

He had read about this. Every scholar of the gradient had. *Aetheric condensation in enclosed spaces: crystalline precipitation of ambient field energy under sustained exposure.* He'd seen drawings. Once, in a glass case at the Scholars, he'd seen a single crystal the size of a thumbnail. An expedition had brought it back from the Wilds and lost two men getting it.

There were thousands here. Hundreds of thousands, growing out of the walls of a drain under a factory.

"Aldric," said Mira. "You've stopped."

"Sorry. Yes." He had his notebook out, though he didn't remember taking it out. He wrote as he walked: *crystals from c. 20 paces past stair, clustering at arch springing points, luminous, rhythmic, period approx. 4 seconds*. The pencil moved by itself while the rest of him looked.

This was Harker's anomaly, made real. Every number in those calibration notes that climbed where it ought to fall was here, growing out of the brickwork. *Something is actively pulling the boundary.* It was no error and no drift. The Aether was coming through here, under the Factory Quarter, where every textbook he'd ever owned said it couldn't.

His heart was going faster. He honestly couldn't say whether it was fear or joy.

---

They found the first chalk mark at the first junction.

The tunnel split. The left branch sloped downward. The right ran level, wider, its floor scuffed. On the brick at the fork somebody had chalked a small sign: a circle with a short line through it, and three dots beneath.

"Syndicate," said Mira. "That's their road-mark. I've seen it on gradient doors. It means *ours*." She tilted her head. "I don't know the dots."

"Distance, maybe," Aldric said. "Or direction. Or a count of something." He copied it into the notebook. A few yards along the right-hand branch there was another, a circle and a line with two dots. "Counting down," he said, pleased. "Two. Then one. Then whatever's at the end." He copied that too. By the end of the night he would fill four pages with marks, and he would spend weeks afterwards trying to read them. He would manage perhaps half.

Nobody decided the marching order. It made itself. Kael went first, because Kael always went first into dark places. No one thought to argue. Mira came second, close behind him, where she could see past his shoulder and talk to whoever they met. Sera walked in the middle, because whatever came at them, she could answer it from anywhere. Aldric came last. The one who mends things should be the one nobody can reach first.

Later he wrote that down too and underlined it. They walked into every dark place that way for a year.

---

"I'm going to look ahead," Aldric said at the next fork. "Before we walk into something."

Mira nodded. Kael put his back to the wall. Sera turned to watch the way they'd come.

He took out the spellbook but didn't open it. He knew this working as well as his own name. It was the first his first teacher had ever made him learn: a seeing, a reaching-out of the mind's eye along the lines of a place. In the foundry it would have been like pushing a cart through mud. Down here...

He laid the frame. Three lines of attention, set at angles like tent poles, with a shape between them. Then he opened it a crack for the Aether to come in and fill it.

It came in like a tide through a sluice.

He'd asked for a cupful and been given a bucket. He set his teeth and held the shape steady while it filled and strained at the edges. Then the seeing went out from him down the right-hand tunnel. It swung round a bend, along a straight, past a fall of fresh rubble where a side passage had collapsed, and into a room.

It was a big room, a vault thirty feet across under an arched brick ceiling. Every surface was furred with crystal, so that the whole place glowed blue-white like the inside of an iceberg. He saw a stack of wooden crates and a handcart. Three figures were lifting and stacking: two big and slow, one smaller and quick, giving directions. Against the far wall sat a fourth, very still, his hands held together in front of him the way only tied hands sit.

He let it go. The seeing snapped back into him like a stretched band. He swayed, and Mira's hand was under his elbow.

"He's there," Aldric said. His voice sounded odd in his own ears. "Harker. He's alive. Sitting up. Three others. Crates, a cart. A big room. A cistern, I think. Round the next bend and along."

"Good," said Mira. "Well done." She was watching him closely. "You're bleeding."

He touched his upper lip and his fingers came away red. A nosebleed, only a little one. In fifteen years he had never had one from a seeing.

"It came in too fast," he said. "There's a great deal of it down here. More than I asked for. I'll be more careful." He wiped his lip and folded the handkerchief away, then wrote that down too: *seeing, standard frame, delivered far beyond intent. Nosebleed. NB: the air here GIVES. Be careful what you ask for.*

---

There was one more thing. Afterwards it was the thing he thought about most.

At the fork before the vault, where the right-hand tunnel ran on towards the voices, there was a third way. It wasn't on any map. He'd swear it wasn't on any drainage plan the city had ever filed. A narrow passage opened on the left, its mouth half hidden behind a pillar, and led down. Steeply down. Its walls were different. They might have been older or newer; he couldn't tell. They were smoother, as though water had worn them for a thousand years, or as though they had never been brick at all. The crystals grew thicker here than anywhere else. And the air that came up out of it wasn't warm like the rest of the tunnels.

It was cold and very still.

He stood at the mouth longer than he should have. The crystals here pulsed slower and deeper. He had the absurd feeling that if he stayed long enough, and was quiet enough, he would hear something breathing at the bottom.

"Aldric," said Kael from ahead. It was a summons.

He wrote *third passage at last fork, descending, not on plans, cold, still, crystals dense, period slower*. He underlined it twice, closed the notebook, and went.

Round the bend, the voices in the vault had been talking low and steady over the scrape of crates on stone. As Aldric came up behind the others, they stopped. All at once, mid-sentence.

The tunnel was very quiet. The crystals breathed.
