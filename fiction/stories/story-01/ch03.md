# Chapter 3

Aldric counted the steps going down, because he counted things when he was nervous, and because someone ought to.

Forty-one. An iron spiral, bolted into the brick of a shaft so narrow his shoulders brushed both sides. At the twelfth step the hum began to fade. At the twentieth he realised that the headache he'd been carrying since they came off the canal path — a dull, grinding pressure behind his eyes, the price every caster paid for standing in a Galvanic district — had loosened its grip. At the thirtieth it was gone.

At the thirty-fifth, his satchel went light.

He actually stopped, one hand on the rail, and put the other on the flap of the satchel, where the spellbook sat. Up in the foundry it had felt like a book made of lead. Like trying to read in a dream. Now it was just a book. No — less than that. It was eager. The pages seemed almost to stir under his palm, the way a dog stirs when it hears its name.

"You feel that?" Sera said behind him, very quietly. She was on the step above. Her voice had a lift in it he'd never heard before.

"Yes," said Aldric. "Yes, I do."

Ahead of him, at the bottom, Mira had her arc pistol out and was looking at it with a frown. The little brass charge needle that had been sitting so fat and full in the yard was trembling. As he watched, it dropped a quarter of the dial, and climbed back, and dropped again, like the hand of a man with a fever.

"That's not good," she said.

"No," Aldric agreed. "It's not built for this air. Nothing Galvanic is." He looked at Kael. "Your revolver ought to be all right. It's only springs and powder."

Kael didn't answer. He had drawn the revolver and was holding it in front of him, and he turned it over in his hand, once, and tapped the cylinder twice with his thumb, and frowned at it, the way a man frowns at a watch that has started to tick a fraction too loud.

Then he put it away and went on, and Aldric forgot about it. He'd remember, much later.

---

The tunnels began at the bottom of the stair.

They had been drains, once. You could see that. An arched brick corridor, tall enough to walk in without stooping, with a channel down the middle where a thin stream of water still ran, and iron rungs set into the walls at intervals where workmen had once climbed up to manholes long since paved over. Pre-Tear. Solid, sensible, municipal. Somebody had laid every one of these bricks with a trowel, and had a cup of tea afterwards, and gone home.

And then, about twenty paces in, the bricks began to glow.

Not all of them. Not brightly. The condensation running down the walls had a faint blue light in it, as if the water had been mixed with moonlight. And where the arches met the walls, in the corners, something was growing. Clusters of thin, pale crystals, finger-long, sharp as glass, sprouting out of the mortar in little bursts like frost on a window pane. They gave off a soft, cold light of their own. As Aldric watched, it brightened very slightly, and dimmed, and brightened, slow and steady.

Like breathing. Or a pulse.

He had read about this. Every scholar of the gradient had. *Aetheric condensation in enclosed spaces: crystalline precipitation of ambient field energy under sustained exposure.* He'd seen drawings. He'd seen, once, in a glass case at the Scholars, a single crystal the size of a thumbnail, brought back from the Wilds by an expedition that had lost two men getting it.

There were thousands here. Hundreds of thousands. Growing out of the walls of a drain under a factory.

"Aldric," said Mira, gently. "You've stopped."

"Sorry. Yes." He had his notebook out. He didn't remember taking it out. He began to write as he walked — *crystals from c. 20 paces past stair, clustering at arch springing points, luminous, rhythmic, period approx. 4 seconds* — and the pencil moved by itself while the rest of him looked.

It was Harker's anomaly, made real. He could see it. Everything in those calibration notes, every number climbing where it ought to fall — here it was, growing out of the brickwork. *Something is actively pulling the boundary.* Not an error. Not drift. The Aether was coming through here. Down here, under the Factory Quarter, in a place where every textbook he'd ever owned said it couldn't.

He felt his heart go faster, and could not honestly say whether it was fear or joy.

---

They found the first chalk mark at the first junction.

The tunnel split in two — left, sloping downward, and right, running level, wider, the floor scuffed. On the brick at the fork somebody had drawn in chalk a small sign: a circle with a short line through it, and three dots beneath.

"Syndicate," said Mira. "That's their road-mark. I've seen it on gradient doors. It means *ours*." She tilted her head. "I don't know the dots."

"Distance, maybe," Aldric said. "Or direction. Or numbers of something." He copied it into the notebook. A few yards on, on the right-hand branch, there was another: a circle, a line, and two dots. "Counting down," he said, pleased. "Two. Then one. Then — whatever's at the end." He copied that too. He would fill four pages with them by the end of the night, and spend weeks afterwards trying to read the rest. He would get perhaps half.

The marching order made itself without anyone deciding it. Kael went first, because Kael always went first into dark places, and it simply didn't occur to any of them to argue. Mira second, close behind him, where she could see past his shoulder and talk to whoever they met. Sera third, in the middle, because whatever came at them, she was the one who could answer it from anywhere. And Aldric last, because the one who mends things should be the one nobody can reach first.

Later, he'd write that down too, and underline it, and they would walk that way into every dark place they went for a year.

---

"I'm going to look ahead," Aldric said at the next fork. "Before we walk into something."

Mira nodded. Kael stopped and put his back to the wall. Sera turned to watch the way they'd come.

He took out the spellbook and didn't open it. He didn't need to. He knew this one the way he knew his own name — the first working his first teacher had ever made him learn, a seeing, a reaching-out of the mind's eye along the lines of a place. Up in the foundry, it would have been like pushing a cart through mud. Down here —

He laid the frame. Three lines of attention, set at angles, like the poles of a tent. A shape between them. And then the opening, just a crack, for the Aether to come in and fill it.

It came in like a tide through a sluice.

He hadn't asked for that much. He'd asked for a cupful and been given a bucket, and he had to set his teeth and hold the shape steady as it filled and filled and strained at the edges. Then the seeing went out from him down the right-hand tunnel like a lamp swung on a long chain — round a bend, along a straight, past a fall of fresh rubble where a side passage had come down — and into a room.

A big room. A vault. Thirty feet across, the ceiling arched in old brick, and every surface furred with crystal, so that the whole place glowed blue-white like the inside of an iceberg. A stack of wooden crates. A handcart. Three figures moving, lifting, stacking — two big and slow, one smaller and quick, giving directions. And against the far wall, sitting very still, a fourth, with his hands in front of him in a way that hands don't sit unless they're tied.

He let it go. The seeing snapped back into him like a stretched band, and he swayed, and Mira's hand was under his elbow.

"He's there," Aldric said. His voice sounded odd in his own ears. "Harker. He's alive. Sitting up. Three others. Crates, a cart. A big room — a cistern, I think. Round the next bend and along."

"Good," said Mira. "Good. Well done." She was looking at him closely. "You're bleeding."

He put his hand to his upper lip, and it came away red. A nosebleed. Just a little. He'd never had one from a seeing before. Not once, in fifteen years.

"It came in too fast," he said. "There's — there's a great deal of it down here. More than I asked for. I'll be more careful." He wiped his lip with his handkerchief and folded it away and wrote that down too: *seeing, standard frame, delivered far beyond intent. Nosebleed. NB: the air here GIVES. Be careful what you ask for.*

---

There was one more thing, and afterwards it was the thing he thought about most.

At the fork before the vault, where the right-hand tunnel ran on towards the voices, there was a third way. Not on any map. Not, he'd swear, on any drainage plan the city had ever filed. A narrow passage on the left, its mouth half hidden behind a pillar, leading down. Steeply down. The brick of its walls was different — older, or newer, he couldn't tell; it was smoother, somehow, as if it had been worn by water for a thousand years, or had never been brick at all. The crystals in it were thicker than anywhere else. And the air that came up out of it was not warm, like the rest of the tunnels.

It was cold. And very, very still.

He stood at the mouth of it for a moment longer than he should have. The pulse of the crystals here was slower. Deeper. He had the absurd feeling that if he stood there long enough, and was quiet enough, he would hear something breathing at the bottom.

"Aldric," said Kael softly, from ahead. Not a question. A summons.

He wrote down *third passage at last fork, descending, not on plans, cold, still, crystals dense, period slower*, and underlined it twice, and closed the notebook, and went.

Ahead of them, round the bend, the voices in the vault had been talking, low and steady, the scrape of crates on stone underneath. As Aldric came up behind the others, they stopped.

All at once. Mid-sentence.

The tunnel was very quiet. The crystals breathed.
