# Chapter 1

The charter was seven days old. The ink still smudged if you rubbed it. Mira had rubbed it a good deal.

She didn't mean to. It lived in the inside pocket of her coat, folded in three, and every so often her hand went in to touch it like a tongue finding a new tooth. *Ash & Veil Recovery. Chartered Adventuring Society, Asset Recovery, City of Ashwick.* Four names at the bottom in four different hands. Hers was the smallest and fastest. Aldric's was the neatest. Kael's pressed so hard it had nearly gone through the paper. Sera's sloped uphill.

Seven days. Zero jobs. One hundred and forty marks in the kitty would cover rent on the flat over the cobbler's until the end of the month, and not one day after.

"She's late," said Kael.

"She's a Greycoat inspector," said Mira. "They're always late. It's how you know they're busy."

They had the corner table at the Wet Ember, with its back to the wall and a clear view of the door. Kael would not sit anywhere else. Mira had stopped arguing about it the first week she knew him.

It was late afternoon. The fire was lit, the gas lamps were up, and the four o'clock crowd was coming in: factory hands from the west side still grey with shift dust, clerks from Central, a pair of old women from the Quarter whose copper bangles chimed when they lifted their glasses. Nobody bothered anybody. The Wet Ember sat exactly on the gradient, where the hum from the factories met the sweetness from the old town and the two cancelled out. Behind the bar, Hesper Brack worked with one arm and a towel and made sure that whatever you were outside, in here you were a customer.

Aldric was reading. Sera watched the fire with her chin on her hands. When she turned her head, the light caught the faint pale ring at the edge of her irises, so faint you had to know it was there. Mira knew. She'd noticed it in the spring. She'd also noticed Sera noticing her notice. Neither of them had mentioned it since.

The door opened, and a woman in a grey coat came in out of the drizzle.

It was a Greycoat's grey: long and belted, a brass badge pinned at the collar, the hem dark with wet. Mira put her in her mid-forties, with sharp eyes and a mouth that had been told a lot of lies and remembered all of them. She looked round the room once, found them, and crossed without hurrying. She sat down without being asked and put a thin card folder on the table between the glasses.

"You're the new Society," she said. "I'm Inspector Laine. Let's skip the part where you try to impress me."

Mira smiled. It was her best smile, the one that said *I am delighted, I am competent, I am not in the least intimidated*. Laine gave it the look a jeweller gives paste.

"Of course," Mira said. "What do you need?"

---

What Laine needed was a man called Fen Harker.

She laid the folder out like a hand of cards. There was a pencil sketch of a thin man with round spectacles, who looked faintly startled, as if someone had just told him the time. There was a hand-drawn map of the Ashwick border district with the zone lines inked in amber for the Galvanic side and blue for the Aetheric, and a shaded grey band between them where the two bled together. And there was a note in cramped handwriting on Gradient Scholars letterhead.

"Gradient Scholar," Laine said. "Forty-three. Nearsighted. Terrible card player. He's been in Ashwick for two months measuring zone drift, which means where the boundary moves and how fast. Dull work. Someone has to do it." She tapped the note. "That's his last dead-drop to his people at the Scholars. Three days ago."

Mira turned it so the others could read.

*Anomalous readings near the decommissioned Ashworth foundry. Drift pattern inconsistent with natural decay — something is actively pulling the boundary. Investigating tomorrow. Will report findings at next check-in.*

"He didn't report," said Laine. "His room's paid through the month. His instruments are gone. Either he found something interesting and lost track of time, which academics do, I'm told, or he found something that didn't want to be found." She looked round the table. "Two hundred marks. Split it how you like. Plus expenses, within reason, and I decide what's reason. Find him. Bring him back. And try not to start a war with anyone."

Aldric had picked up the note. He held it close to his spectacles, then further away, then close again.

"The hand's steady at the start," he said. "Then it isn't. Look at the last line. The letters lean further right as they go, so he was writing faster. And here." He touched the corner. "The ink's smudged. He folded it before it was dry."

Laine looked at him properly. "So?"

"So he was frightened when he wrote it," Aldric said. "Or excited. In my experience it's hard to tell the difference with Scholars."

The corner of Laine's mouth moved.

Mira waited a moment before she spoke. "You don't have the people to go out there yourself."

"No."

"And you didn't come to a week-old Society because you thought we were the best in Ashwick."

"I came to you," Laine said, "because the best in Ashwick cost four hundred marks and don't return Greycoat letters." She paused. "And because a Society that wants its first job badly enough tends to try harder."

"That's fair," said Mira. She leaned in, though not much. "Is there anything else we should know? Anything about the border district this month? Anyone busy who isn't usually?"

Laine studied her as if she were deciding what the information was worth, and whether she'd get change.

"The Lamplighters have been busier than usual round the Factory Quarter edge," she said. "Carts at night. It's not my department and I don't have proof. I'm only telling you so that if you find yourselves somewhere you shouldn't be, you'll know whose somewhere it probably is." She stood. "His boarding house is on Tallow Row. The landlady's name is Olt. Report to me at the Central station when you've got him, or when you've got something worth my time. Not before."

She left. The door swung shut on the rain. At the bar, Hesper polished a glass that didn't need it.

"Well," said Kael.

"Two hundred marks," said Sera, sitting up. Her eyes were bright. "Two hundred."

"First job," said Mira. Her hand had gone to the charter again. This time she took it out and left it on the table.

---

Tallow Row was three streets east of the Wet Ember, on the neutral side. Its terraced houses were grey stone with brass knockers and net curtains, and people there swept their own steps. Two blocks to the west the factories hummed, low and steady, like a cat purring in another room. A brass plaque by one door read *OLT'S — ROOMS BY THE WEEK*.

Mrs. Olt opened on the second knock. She was sixty or so, with flour on her apron and reading glasses pushed up into grey hair. When Mira said Harker's name her whole face changed.

"Oh, thank goodness. Come in, come in. I haven't touched his room. It didn't seem right, with him not back."

She talked all the way up the stairs. Mr. Harker was a lovely tenant: quiet, polite, paid on the nail. Three mornings ago he'd gone out with his bag, heavy and clanking with all his instruments, and said he'd be back for supper. He'd been going out to the foundry every day for a fortnight. And yesterday...

She stopped on the landing.

"There was a man," she said. "Across the road, by the lamppost, smoking. Long coat. Rough-looking. He stood there an hour, near enough, looking at the house. I didn't like it. I went to the window and stared at him. He looked straight back at me. Then he just strolled off." She pressed a hand flat to her apron. "I've never seen him before. Is Mr. Harker in trouble?"

"We're going to find out," Mira said. "You've been a great help."

Harker's room was small and very tidy. He was a man with few things who knew exactly where each of them was. There was a narrow bed and a washstand. A desk under the window had a locked drawer. A shelf held books on zone theory, most of them heavily annotated. On the floor sat an empty instrument case lined in green baize, with cut-outs for things that weren't there.

Nobody had to be told where to look.

Kael went to the window. He stood to one side of it; Mira had noticed he never stood in front of one. He looked down at the street, then at the floor, and crouched.

"Someone's been in," he said. "Before us. Through here." On the boards under the sill, in the faint film of dust, he pointed out the edge of a boot and the scuff of a knee. "Climbed the drainpipe, came in, had a look round. Careful about it. Not careful enough."

Sera had gone straight to the instrument case. She didn't touch it. She stood over it with her head tilted, listening for something only she could hear.

"It's warm," she said.

"It's baize," said Aldric. "Baize holds—"

"Not that sort of warm." She held her hand an inch above the empty cut-outs and moved it slowly along them. "The things that were in here have been somewhere thick. They brought a bit of it back, and it's in the cloth." She frowned. "Like when you come in from the Quarter and your coat smells of copper for an hour."

Mira went to the desk. The drawer had the cheap brass lock that comes with cheap desks. Aldric got as far as "Mira, should we really—" and stopped. It was already open.

Inside were a bundle of letters from a sister in Millbridge and a pocket notebook. Under them lay a thick black journal with a cracked spine.

"They missed it," Kael said from the window. "Whoever came in had the room to themselves, and they missed it."

"They weren't looking in a locked drawer," said Mira. "If they'd known about it they'd have taken it, lock or no lock. So they didn't know it was here." She handed the journal to Aldric.

He sat on the edge of the bed and turned the pages. For a minute the only sounds were the paper and the faint hum through the wall. His face went still and young, as it always did when he was reading something that mattered.

"Calibration notes," he said. "Two weeks of them. He's been walking the boundary every day with his instruments." He turned another page. "Here. And here. Look at the numbers. The Aetheric reading should fall as he walks west into the Factory Quarter, and it does, everywhere except one place. There it rises. Steeply. Inside a Galvanic district." He looked up. "That's not possible. It's like finding a warm spring under a glacier."

"The foundry," said Mira.

"The foundry." He found the last page with writing on it. "Last entry. *Sub-basement access — the old service stair, east end of the main hall. Readings strongest below ground. Whatever is pulling the boundary is beneath the building. Going in tomorrow to confirm.*" He closed the journal gently. "Tomorrow was three days ago."

Mira went to the window and stood beside Kael, off to the side as he did. Down in the street the drizzle had thinned to nothing. The lamplighter had been round, and the lamps were coming on one by one along Tallow Row, each in its own cone of yellow.

A man in a long coat stood smoking by the lamppost across the road.

He wasn't looking at the house. He was looking at the window, at them.

He let her see him see him. Then he took the cigarette from his mouth, looked at it, and flicked it into the gutter, where it hissed out. He turned up his collar and strolled off down Tallow Row towards the factories, unhurried. He walked like a man who knew where they'd be going next.

"Kael," said Mira.

"I saw."

She put her hand in her pocket and touched the charter. This time she didn't take it out.
