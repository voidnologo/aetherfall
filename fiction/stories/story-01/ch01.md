# Chapter 1

The charter was seven days old and the ink still smudged if you rubbed it, and Mira had rubbed it a good deal.

She didn't mean to. It lived in the inside pocket of her coat, folded in three, and every so often her hand went in and touched it, the way your tongue goes to a new tooth. *Ash & Veil Recovery. Chartered Adventuring Society, Asset Recovery, City of Ashwick.* Four names at the bottom in four different hands. Hers was the smallest and fastest. Aldric's was the neatest. Kael's pressed so hard it had nearly gone through the paper. Sera's sloped uphill.

Seven days. Zero jobs. One hundred and forty marks in the kitty, which would cover rent on the flat over the cobbler's until the end of the month, and not one day after.

"She's late," said Kael.

"She's a Greycoat inspector," said Mira. "They're always late. It's how you know they're busy."

They had the corner table at the Wet Ember, the one with its back to the wall and a clear view of the door, because Kael would not sit anywhere else and Mira had stopped arguing about it the first week she knew him. Late afternoon. The fire was lit, the lamps were gas, and the room was filling with the four o'clock crowd — factory hands from the west side still grey with shift dust, clerks from Central, a pair of old women from the Quarter with copper bangles that chimed when they lifted their glasses. Nobody bothered anybody. That was the Wet Ember's whole religion. It sat exactly on the gradient, where the hum from the factories and the sweetness from the old town met and cancelled out, and Hesper Brack, behind the bar with her one arm and her towel, made sure that whatever you were outside, in here you were a customer.

Aldric was reading. Sera was watching the fire with her chin on her hands. The faint pale ring around the edge of her irises caught the light when she turned her head — so faint you had to know it was there. Mira knew. She'd noticed it in the spring, and she'd noticed Sera noticing her notice, and neither of them had mentioned it since.

The door opened, and a woman in a grey coat came in out of the drizzle.

Not grey like a colour. Grey like a uniform. Long, belted, the brass badge pinned at the collar, the hem dark with wet. She was mid-forties, Mira thought, with sharp eyes and a mouth that had been told a lot of lies and remembered all of them. She looked round the room once, found them, and crossed to their table without hurrying. She sat down without being asked. She put a thin card folder on the table between the glasses.

"You're the new Society," she said. "I'm Inspector Laine. Let's skip the part where you try to impress me."

Mira smiled. It was her best smile, the one that said *I am delighted, I am competent, I am not in the least intimidated*, and Laine looked at it the way a jeweller looks at paste.

"Of course," Mira said. "What do you need?"

---

What Laine needed was a man called Fen Harker.

She opened the folder and laid it out for them like a hand of cards. A pencil sketch of a thin man with round spectacles and the faintly startled expression of someone who has just been told the time. A map of the Ashwick border district, hand-drawn, with the zone lines inked in three colours — amber for the Galvanic side, blue for the Aetheric, and a shaded band of grey between them where the two bled together. And a note, on Gradient Scholars letterhead, in cramped handwriting.

"Gradient Scholar," Laine said. "Forty-three. Nearsighted. Terrible card player. He's been in Ashwick for two months measuring zone drift — where the boundary moves, and how fast. Dull work. Someone has to do it." She tapped the note. "That's his last dead-drop to his people at the Scholars. Three days ago."

Mira turned it so the others could read.

*Anomalous readings near the decommissioned Ashworth foundry. Drift pattern inconsistent with natural decay — something is actively pulling the boundary. Investigating tomorrow. Will report findings at next check-in.*

"He didn't report," said Laine. "His room's paid through the month. His instruments are gone. Either he found something interesting and lost track of time — academics do that, I'm told — or he found something that didn't want to be found." She looked round the table. "Two hundred marks. Split it how you like. Plus expenses, within reason, and I decide what's reason. Find him. Bring him back. And try not to start a war with anyone."

Aldric had picked up the note. He held it close to his spectacles, then a little further away, then close again.

"The hand's steady at the start," he said. "And then it isn't. Look at the last line. The letters lean further right as they go. He was writing faster. And here —" he touched the corner — "the ink's smudged. He folded it before it was dry."

Laine looked at him properly for the first time. "So?"

"So he was frightened," Aldric said simply, "when he wrote it. Or excited. In my experience it's hard to tell the difference with Scholars."

Something at the corner of Laine's mouth moved, very slightly.

Mira let the silence sit for a beat. Then she said, mildly, "You don't have the people to go out there yourself."

"No."

"And you didn't come to a week-old Society because you thought we were the best in Ashwick."

"I came to you," Laine said, "because the best in Ashwick cost four hundred marks and don't return Greycoat letters." She paused. "And because a Society that wants its first job badly enough tends to try harder."

"That's fair," said Mira. She leaned forward a little. Not much. "Is there anything else we should know? Anything about the border district this month? Anyone busy who isn't usually?"

Laine looked at her for a long moment. It was the look of someone deciding how much a thing was worth and whether she'd get change.

"The Lamplighters," she said at last, "have been busier than usual round the Factory Quarter edge. Carts at night. Not my department and I don't have proof. I'm only telling you so that if you find yourselves somewhere you shouldn't be, you'll know whose somewhere it probably is." She stood. "His boarding house is on Tallow Row. The landlady's name is Olt. Report to me at the Central station when you've got him, or when you've got something worth my time. Not before."

She left. The door swung shut on the rain. Hesper, at the bar, polished a glass that didn't need it.

"Well," said Kael.

"Two hundred marks," said Sera, sitting up. Her eyes were bright. "Two hundred."

"First job," said Mira, and found her hand had gone to the charter in her pocket again. She took it out, and left it out.

---

Tallow Row was three streets east of the Wet Ember, on the neutral side — terraced houses of grey stone with brass knockers and net curtains, the kind of street where people swept their own steps. You could hear the factories from here, a low steady hum two blocks to the west, like a cat purring in another room. A brass plaque by one door read *OLT'S — ROOMS BY THE WEEK*.

Mrs. Olt opened on the second knock. She was sixty or so, with flour on her apron and reading glasses pushed up into grey hair, and when Mira said Harker's name her whole face changed.

"Oh, thank goodness. Come in, come in. I haven't touched his room — it seemed wrong, somehow, with him not back."

She talked all the way up the stairs. Mr. Harker was a lovely tenant, quiet, polite, paid on the nail. He'd gone out three mornings ago with his bag — heavy, it clanked, all his instruments — and said he'd be back for supper. He'd been going out to the foundry area every day for a fortnight. And yesterday —

She stopped on the landing.

"There was a man," she said. "Across the road. Standing by the lamppost, smoking. A long coat. Rough-looking. He stood there an hour, near enough, looking at the house. I didn't like it. I went to the window and stared at him, and he looked straight back at me, and then he just — strolled off." She pressed a hand flat to her apron. "I've never seen him before. Is Mr. Harker in trouble?"

"We're going to find out," Mira said gently. "You've been a great help."

Harker's room was small and very tidy, in the way of a man who had nothing much and knew exactly where all of it was. A narrow bed. A washstand. A desk under the window with a locked drawer. A shelf of books about zone theory, most of them heavily annotated. An empty instrument case lined in green baize, with cut-outs for things that weren't there.

They did what they did best, which was to spread out without being told to.

Kael went to the window. He stood to one side of it — never in front, Mira noticed; he never stood in front of a window — and looked down at the street, and then down at the floor. Then he crouched.

"Someone's been in," he said. "Before us. Through here." He pointed. On the boards under the sill, in the faint film of dust, there were marks: the edge of a boot, the scuff of a knee. "Climbed up the drainpipe, came in, had a look round. Careful about it. Not careful enough."

Sera had gone straight to the instrument case. She didn't touch it. She stood over it with her head tilted, as though listening to something a long way off.

"It's warm," she said.

"It's baize," said Aldric. "Baize holds—"

"Not that sort of warm." She held her hand an inch above the empty cut-outs and moved it slowly along them. "The things that were in here have been somewhere. Somewhere thick. They've brought a bit of it back with them. It's in the cloth." She frowned. "It's like when you come in from the Quarter and your coat smells of copper for an hour."

Mira went to the desk. The drawer had a cheap brass lock, the kind that came with cheap desks, and she had it open in the time it took Aldric to say "Mira, should we really—" and stop, because it was already done.

Inside: a bundle of letters from a sister in Millbridge. A pocket notebook. And underneath them, a thick black journal, its spine cracked with use.

"They missed it," Kael said, from the window. "Whoever came in. They had the room to themselves and they missed it."

"They weren't looking in a locked drawer," said Mira, "because they didn't need to pick it. They'd have just taken it. Which means they didn't know it was here." She handed the journal to Aldric. That was his.

He sat down on the edge of the bed with it, and turned pages, and for a minute the only sound in the room was the paper and the faint hum through the wall. His face did the thing it always did when he was reading something that mattered — went very still, and very young.

"Calibration notes," he said. "Two weeks of them. He's been walking the boundary every day with his instruments, taking readings." He turned a page, and another. "Here. And here. Look at the numbers going up. The Aetheric reading should fall as he walks west into the Factory Quarter. It does. Everywhere except one place, where it — rises. Steeply. Inside a Galvanic district." He looked up. "That's not possible. That's like finding a warm spring under a glacier."

"The foundry," said Mira.

"The foundry." He turned to the last page with writing on it. "Last entry. *Sub-basement access — the old service stair, east end of the main hall. Readings strongest below ground. Whatever is pulling the boundary is beneath the building. Going in tomorrow to confirm.*" He closed the journal very gently. "Tomorrow was three days ago."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Mira went to the window and stood beside Kael — to one side of it, the way he did. Down in the street the drizzle had thinned to nothing. The lamplighter had been round; the lamps were coming on one by one along Tallow Row, each in its little cone of yellow.

By the lamppost across the road, there was a man in a long coat, smoking.

He wasn't looking at the house. He was looking at the window. At them.

He let her see him see him. Then he took the cigarette from his mouth, looked at it, and flicked it into the gutter, where it went out with a small hiss. He turned up his collar. He walked away down Tallow Row towards the west and the factories, unhurried, as if he had all the time in the world and knew precisely where they'd be going next.

"Kael," said Mira quietly.

"I saw."

She put her hand in her pocket and touched the charter, and this time she didn't take it out.
