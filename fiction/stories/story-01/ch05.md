# Chapter 5

Kael kept his revolver on the man with the shotgun and let Mira do the talking. That was the arrangement. It had been the arrangement since Harrier Street, years before there was a charter, when she was a skinny girl running parcels across the gradient and he was the constable who let her.

"Corva," Mira said pleasantly. "I assume."

The woman in the coat inclined her head a fraction, as if acknowledging a minor point in a debate. "And you'll be the new Society. Ash and something. Your charter was in the Gazette last week. Very small print." She glanced at the hole in the wall, still breathing dust. "I'll confess I'd pictured you as less — emphatic."

"We're here for Mr. Harker."

"I imagined you might be." Corva looked over at the thin man by the wall, who gave a little wave with his tied hands, and then seemed embarrassed about it. "He's quite well. I want you to know that. Nobody's touched him. He's been fed. He's beaten my men at cards twice, which I'm told is unusual." She turned back. "I don't kill Scholars, Miss —?"

"Cade."

"Miss Cade. Dead Scholars bring Greycoats, and Greycoats are bad for business. I was only keeping him somewhere quiet until tonight, when the last of this goes out. After that I'd have let him go."

"Would you," said Mira.

"Probably." Corva smiled, very slightly. "Let's not find out. Here's what I propose. You take Mr. Harker. You walk out the way you came — well, perhaps not *exactly* the way you came. We carry on with our evening. In the morning there's nothing down here but some pretty crystals and an empty room, and you've done the job you were paid for and come home with your client in one piece. And for your trouble," she put her hand into her coat, slowly, so Kael could watch every inch of it, and brought out a small leather purse, "a hundred marks. On top of whatever Laine's paying you. Which can't be much."

Kael didn't take his eyes off the shotgun. But he felt the room shift a little, all the same. A hundred marks. On top of two hundred. Rent till midwinter. He felt Sera, at his shoulder, feel it too.

Mira didn't move.

"That's generous," she said. "I'd want one more thing."

"Of course you would."

"Your manifest." Mira nodded at the crates. "What's in those, where it's going, who's buying. Not the money — I don't care about your money. Just the paper. So that when I tell the inspector what we found, I'm telling her something."

Corva looked at her for a long time. Kael had seen that look on the faces of a great many people across a great many tables, and he knew what it meant. *You're better at this than I expected. I'm not sure I like it.*

"No," said Corva. "I don't think so. You can have the Scholar and the money and your lives. That's three more things than most people get from me in a week."

And it might have ended there. Kael would think about it afterwards — how close it came to ending there, with a purse changing hands and four people walking back up forty-one steps into the evening with a nearsighted scholar between them, and the crates going out on the canal, and nobody ever the wiser. He thought Mira would have taken it. He thought she was only pushing to see where the edges were.

But the man with the shotgun was looking at Sera.

Kael saw him do it. Saw his eyes go to her face, and widen, and stay there. Sera's eyes were still faintly lit from what she'd done to the rubble — a pale blue-white glow at the rims, like the crystals, like the walls. In the blue room it made her look like something that had grown there.

"She's one of them," the man said. His voice cracked. "Corva. Look at her eyes. She's one of the—"

"Benn," said Corva sharply. "Be quiet."

"She's *wild*. Look at her. She brought the wall down, she'll bring the roof—"

"Benn—"

He fired.

---

He didn't fire at anyone. Kael would always believe that. He fired the way frightened men fire, at the thing frightening them, without aiming — and the thing frightening him was the whole blue breathing room, and the shotgun went off with a roar like the end of the world and the charge went up into the vaulted ceiling.

Into the crystal.

The ceiling exploded. Shards of crystal came down in a glittering rain, a great branching spray of it breaking off and falling end over end and shattering on the floor between them in a burst of blue-white light. Something hot and sharp went across Kael's cheek like a cat's claw.

And Sera — he heard her cry out, and felt the air go tight beside him, and then she did the thing she'd done to the rubble, but sideways, without meaning to, the way you throw up your arms when something falls on you.

The stack of crates went over.

All of them. They flew apart from each other as if a bomb had gone off in the middle of the stack, and smashed against the walls, and burst open. Straw everywhere. And in the straw, rolling across the floor — Kael saw it in flashes, like lightning — pieces of crystal, big ones, cut and packed, glowing fiercely. Small carved stones, dark, with lines on them that glowed too. A glass jar, cracking, with something inside it that looked like light poured into water.

Somebody fired a revolver. The other Syndicate man, firing at nobody, at everything. Kael put his own revolver on him.

And it was — slow.

He felt it. He'd remember it all his life, though at the time he barely noticed. His thumb on the hammer, the hammer going back — and there was a half-heartbeat where it didn't feel like his gun. Where it felt like holding something wrapped in wet cloth. Heavy, and far away, and reluctant. As if the machine in his hand had stopped, just for an instant, to wonder where it was.

Then it fired. Clean. A little late. The man's revolver spun out of his hand and skittered away across the crystal-strewn floor, and he grabbed his fingers and sat down and howled.

Kael looked at his own gun for a moment. Then the moment was over, and there were other things to look at.

Mira had drawn her arc pistol on Benn. He saw her squeeze the trigger. He saw the brass needle on its dial jump and drop. Instead of that glass-whip crack and the blue-white line, there was a feeble fizz, like a match struck on a wet box, and a little puff of sparks from the muzzle, and nothing else. Mira said a word Kael hadn't known she knew. The arc pistol went back in its holster, and the derringer came out of her boot in the same movement, and she pointed it at Benn's head from four feet away, and Benn — who had fired his one barrel at the ceiling and had nothing else — dropped the shotgun and put his hands up.

"I'm sorry," he was saying. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I didn't—"

"I know," said Mira. "Sit down."

And Corva was gone.

---

Kael saw the last of her. A flick of dark coat at the far side of the vault, where a second tunnel opened, running west — the way the crates were meant to go. The way the handcart's wheel-ruts ran, worn into the floor. She didn't run. She walked quickly. At the mouth of the tunnel she stopped, just for a moment, and looked back over her shoulder at them across the wreckage of her evening, and her face in the blue light was perfectly calm.

He could have gone after her. He'd have caught her. He knew it, and she knew it, and he saw her see him know it.

He looked at the thin man tied against the wall, with crystal shards in his hair and his broken spectacles, who was in the middle of everything and had no way to get out of it.

He stayed where he was.

Corva nodded to him — a small, precise nod, like a woman settling a bill — and turned, and the dark of the tunnel took her.

---

"Hold still," Aldric said.

Kael held still. He was sitting on an upturned crate with his head tilted back, and Aldric's long, cool fingers were on his cheek, and there was blood running down his neck into his collar that he hadn't noticed until Sera had made a noise and pointed at it.

"It's only a scratch," Kael said.

"It's a gash. Down to the bone. A piece of crystal." Aldric's voice was very calm. "If I leave it, it'll scar, and it'll take a fortnight, and it may go bad in this air. If I don't leave it — hold still — it'll itch. I'm told that's the worst part."

He laid his fingers along the cut.

Kael had never been mended before. He'd seen it done, once or twice, in the Greycoat infirmary, by the one Scholar the Authority could afford to keep on staff. He'd never felt it. There was a warmth, first, spreading out from Aldric's fingertips, like the heat off a mug of tea held to the face. Then a pulling. Not painful. Strange. As if the two sides of the cut had suddenly remembered they used to be together and were very anxious to be together again. Then the itch, exactly as promised, maddening, all along the line of it — and then nothing.

Aldric took his hand away. Kael touched his cheek. Smooth. A little tender. A thin raised line, like an old scratch from a bramble.

"Huh," he said.

Aldric was looking at his own fingers. There was a curious expression on his face — pleasure and alarm together.

"It came easily," he said. "Too easily. I could have done a great deal more with it than I meant to." He shook his hand out, as though it had pins and needles. "This place is very generous. I don't entirely trust it."

---

They cut Fen Harker loose.

He flexed his hands, and winced, and pushed his broken spectacles up his nose with one finger, and looked round at the vault — the shattered crystal, the burst crates, the glowing stones in the straw — with an expression of pure scholarly joy.

"Thank you," he said. "Genuinely. I was beginning to worry they'd—" He stopped, distracted, and bent over to peer at one of the carved stones lying in the straw. "Do you know what these are? Charged artifacts. Aetheric condensate, harvested and *packed*. Someone has been taking the crystal out of these tunnels and shipping it. Which is why my readings were wrong! The boundary isn't drifting. It's being pulled. They're *mining* the Aether through here." He straightened up, and swayed, and Aldric caught him. "This is the most important discovery of my career," said Harker, "and I made it by being kidnapped. The Scholars are going to have so many questions."

"I expect," said Mira, "that we'll be answering most of them."

They loaded three of the least broken crates onto the handcart — evidence, Mira said, and nobody argued. They left Benn and the other man tied back to back in the middle of the vault with their hands free enough to work the knots loose in an hour or two; Mira said it was a kindness, and Kael thought it was also a message. And they went out the way the crates were meant to go — west, along Corva's tunnel, following the wheel-ruts, until the crystals thinned and the hum came back and the air went cold and ordinary and smelled of canal water.

The tunnel ended at a storm drain. An iron grating, its padlock hanging open, opening onto a slope of broken brick and old machinery on the west side of the foundry, behind a heap of industrial rubbish where nobody would ever look.

They came out into the dusk with a scholar and a handcart and three crates of stolen light. The foundry chimneys stood up black against an orange sky. Somewhere down the canal a barge horn sounded.

Kael stood at the grating for a moment, looking back into the dark.

He took out his revolver and turned it over in his hand. Out here, in the hum, it felt like itself again. Solid. Heavy. His.

He put it away. He didn't say anything about it. There didn't seem to be anything to say.
