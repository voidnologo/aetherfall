# Chapter 5

Kael kept his revolver on the man with the shotgun and let Mira do the talking. That had been the arrangement since Harrier Street, years before there was a charter, when she was a skinny girl running parcels across the gradient and he was the constable who let her.

"Corva," Mira said pleasantly. "I assume."

The woman in the coat inclined her head a fraction, conceding a minor point in a debate. "And you'll be the new Society. Ash and something. Your charter was in the Gazette last week, in very small print." She glanced at the hole in the wall, which was still breathing dust. "I'll confess I'd pictured you as less... emphatic."

"We're here for Mr. Harker."

"I imagined you might be." Corva looked over at the thin man by the wall. He gave a little wave with his tied hands and then seemed embarrassed about it. "He's quite well. I want you to know that. Nobody's touched him. He's been fed. He's beaten my men at cards twice, which I'm told is unusual." She turned back. "I don't kill Scholars, Miss...?"

"Cade."

"Miss Cade. Dead Scholars bring Greycoats, and Greycoats are bad for business. I was only keeping him somewhere quiet until tonight, when the last of this goes out. After that I'd have let him go."

"Would you," said Mira.

"Probably." Corva smiled. "Let's not find out. Here's what I propose. You take Mr. Harker. You walk out the way you came, or perhaps not *exactly* the way you came. We carry on with our evening. In the morning there's nothing down here but some pretty crystals and an empty room. You've done the job you were paid for and brought your client home in one piece. And for your trouble..." She put her hand into her coat slowly, so Kael could watch every inch of it, and brought out a small leather purse. "A hundred marks. On top of whatever Laine's paying you, which can't be much."

Kael kept his eyes on the shotgun. All the same, he felt the room shift. A hundred marks on top of two hundred was rent till midwinter. At his shoulder, he felt Sera feel it too.

Mira didn't move.

"That's generous," she said. "I'd want one more thing."

"Of course you would."

"Your manifest." Mira nodded at the crates. "What's in those, where it's going, who's buying. I don't care about your money. Just the paper. So that when I tell the inspector what we found, I'm telling her something."

Corva held her eyes a long time. Kael had seen that look across a great many tables. It meant: *You're better at this than I expected, and I'm not sure I like it.*

"No," said Corva. "I don't think so. You can have the Scholar and the money and your lives. That's three more things than most people get from me in a week."

It might have ended there. Kael thought about that afterwards: a purse changing hands, four people walking back up forty-one steps into the evening with a nearsighted scholar between them, the crates going out on the canal, and nobody ever the wiser. He thought Mira would have taken it. She was only pushing to find the edges.

But the man with the shotgun was looking at Sera.

Kael watched his eyes go to her face and widen and stay there. Sera's eyes were still faintly lit from what she'd done to the rubble, a pale blue-white glow at the rims, the colour of the crystals and the walls. In the blue room she looked like something that had grown there.

"She's one of them," the man said. His voice cracked. "Corva. Look at her eyes. She's one of the—"

"Benn," said Corva sharply. "Be quiet."

"She's *wild*. Look at her. She brought the wall down, she'll bring the roof—"

"Benn—"

He fired.

---

He didn't fire at anyone. Kael would always believe that. Frightened men fire at whatever frightens them, without aiming, and what frightened Benn was the whole blue breathing room. The shotgun went off with a roar that filled the vault. The charge went up into the vaulted ceiling, into the crystal.

The ceiling exploded. Shards came down in a glittering rain. A great branching spray broke off, fell end over end and shattered on the floor between them in a burst of blue-white light. Something hot and sharp went across Kael's cheek like a cat's claw.

He heard Sera cry out and felt the air go tight beside him. She did what she'd done to the rubble, but sideways and without meaning to, as anyone throws up their arms when something falls on them.

The stack of crates went over. They flew apart as if a bomb had gone off in the middle of the stack, smashed against the walls and burst open. There was straw everywhere. Kael saw what rolled out of it in flashes: pieces of crystal, big ones, cut and packed and glowing fiercely. Small dark stones carved with lines that glowed too. A cracking glass jar with something inside that looked like light poured into water.

A revolver went off. The other Syndicate man was firing at nobody and everything. Kael put his own revolver on him.

And it was slow.

He felt it, though at the time he barely noticed. He'd remember it all his life. His thumb drew the hammer back, and for half a heartbeat it didn't feel like his gun. It felt like something wrapped in wet cloth, heavy and far away and reluctant, as if the machine in his hand had stopped for an instant to wonder where it was.

Then it fired, clean and a little late. The man's revolver spun out of his hand and skittered across the crystal-strewn floor. He grabbed his fingers, sat down and howled.

Kael looked at his own gun. Then there were other things to look at.

Mira had drawn her arc pistol on Benn. Kael saw her squeeze the trigger and saw the brass needle jump and drop. There was no glass-whip crack and no blue-white line, only a feeble fizz like a match struck on a wet box and a little puff of sparks from the muzzle. Mira said a word Kael hadn't known she knew. The arc pistol went back in its holster and the derringer came out of her boot in the same movement. She pointed it at Benn's head from four feet away. Benn had fired his one barrel at the ceiling and had nothing else. He dropped the shotgun and put his hands up.

"I'm sorry," he was saying. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I didn't—"

"I know," said Mira. "Sit down."

Corva was gone.

---

Kael saw the last of her: a flick of dark coat on the far side of the vault, where a second tunnel ran west. The handcart's wheel-ruts were worn into the floor towards it; that was where the crates were meant to go. She didn't run. She walked quickly. At the mouth of the tunnel she stopped and looked back over her shoulder across the wreckage of her evening. In the blue light her face was perfectly calm.

He could have gone after her. He'd have caught her. He knew it, she knew it, and he saw her see him know it.

He looked at the thin man tied against the wall, with crystal shards in his hair and broken spectacles, who was in the middle of everything with no way out of it.

He stayed where he was.

Corva gave him a small, precise nod, like a woman settling a bill. Then she turned and the dark of the tunnel took her.

---

"Hold still," Aldric said.

Kael held still. He sat on an upturned crate with his head tilted back and Aldric's long, cool fingers on his cheek. Blood was running down his neck into his collar. He hadn't noticed it until Sera made a noise and pointed.

"It's only a scratch," Kael said.

"It's a gash. Down to the bone. A piece of crystal." Aldric's voice was calm. "If I leave it, it'll scar, it'll take a fortnight, and it may go bad in this air. If I don't leave it... hold still... it'll itch. I'm told that's the worst part."

He laid his fingers along the cut.

Kael had never been mended. He'd seen it done once or twice in the Greycoat infirmary, by the one Scholar the Authority could afford to keep on staff, but he'd never felt it. First warmth spread out from Aldric's fingertips. Then came a pulling. It didn't hurt. It was strange, as though the two sides of the cut had remembered they used to be together and were anxious to be together again. Then the itch arrived exactly as promised, maddening, all along the line. Then nothing.

Aldric took his hand away. Kael touched his cheek. It was smooth and a little tender, with a thin raised line like an old bramble scratch.

"Huh," he said.

Aldric was looking at his own fingers with an odd mix of pleasure and alarm.

"It came easily," he said. "Too easily. I could have done a great deal more with it than I meant to." He shook out his hand. "This place is very generous. I don't entirely trust it."

---

They cut Fen Harker loose.

He flexed his hands and winced. He pushed his broken spectacles up his nose with one finger and looked round at the shattered crystal, the burst crates and the glowing stones in the straw with an expression of pure scholarly joy.

"Thank you," he said. "Genuinely. I was beginning to worry they'd—" He broke off and bent to peer at one of the carved stones. "Do you know what these are? Charged artifacts. Aetheric condensate, harvested and *packed*. Someone has been taking the crystal out of these tunnels and shipping it. That's why my readings were wrong! The boundary isn't drifting. It's being pulled. They're *mining* the Aether through here." He straightened up and swayed. Aldric caught him. "This is the most important discovery of my career," said Harker, "and I made it by being kidnapped. The Scholars are going to have so many questions."

"I expect," said Mira, "that we'll be answering most of them."

They loaded three of the least broken crates onto the handcart as evidence. Nobody argued. They left Benn and the other man tied back to back in the middle of the vault, with enough slack to work the knots loose in an hour or two. Mira called it a kindness. Kael thought it was also a message. Then they went out the way the crates were meant to go, west along Corva's tunnel, following the wheel-ruts. The crystals thinned. The hum came back. The air turned cold and ordinary and smelled of canal water.

The tunnel ended at a storm drain. Its iron grating hung open, padlock and all, onto a slope of broken brick and old machinery on the west side of the foundry, behind a heap of industrial rubbish where nobody would ever look.

They came out into the dusk with a scholar, a handcart and three crates of stolen light. The foundry chimneys stood black against an orange sky. Somewhere down the canal a barge horn sounded.

Kael stood at the grating, looking back into the dark.

He took out his revolver and turned it over in his hand. Out here, in the hum, it felt like itself again: solid, heavy, his.

He put it away and said nothing about it. There didn't seem to be anything to say.
