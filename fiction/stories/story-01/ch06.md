# Chapter 6

Inspector Laine's office at the Central station was a cupboard with a window, and the window looked at a wall.

She sat behind a desk buried in paper and listened to Mira's account with her chin on her fist and her eyes half-closed, the way a cat listens to a mouse it hasn't decided whether to bother with. Harker had been taken off to the Scholars' infirmary in a Greycoat cab, protesting that he was perfectly well and needed his notebooks. The three crates stood in the corridor outside under a tarpaulin, with a very young constable guarding them who kept looking at the tarpaulin as though it might bite.

Mira told it well. She'd been telling stories to people behind desks since she was fourteen, and she knew how to make one sit up straight and walk in a line.

The Scholar's journal. The foundry, the chain, the two men in the yard, who were tied to the railings and might still be there if anyone wanted them. The Syndicate. A smuggling operation running stolen goods out of the old drains under the Factory Quarter and down the canal. A woman called Corva, who'd got away. The Scholar alive, unhurt, fed, and four marks up at cards. Three crates of evidence in the corridor.

All of it true.

What she didn't mention — and she noticed herself not mentioning it, the way you notice your own hand reaching for something before you've decided to reach — was the rest. The crystals growing out of the brick in their hundreds of thousands, pulsing together like one slow heart. The way Sera's eyes had lit up blue when she pushed, and stayed lit. The way Aldric's seeing had poured into him until his nose bled, and his mending had come so easily it frightened him. The third passage at the last fork, the one that went down and down, cold and still, that was on no plan the city had ever filed.

She didn't decide to leave it out. She just — didn't put it in. Some quick, practised part of her had looked at Laine, overworked, under-staffed, a woman with a hundred problems and no constables, and had looked at the thing in the tunnels, which was very large and very strange and not yet understood by anyone, and had made a judgement. *Not her problem yet. Not yet. Not until we know what it is.*

It was the kind of judgement Mira had been making all her life. It had kept her alive on the gradient. She didn't think twice about it.

Kael, standing by the door with his hat in his hands and a thin pink line across his cheek, didn't say anything either. But she felt him look at her once, about halfway through. Just once. Then he looked at the wall.

"Well," said Laine, when she'd finished. She opened her eyes. "Corva Sable. I know the name. Never had a face to put to it." She made a note. "The Syndicate will have that route shut by midnight and another one open by Thursday. You know that."

"I know that."

"But they've lost the shipment, and the drain, and four men's worth of nerve, and they'll be careful down there for a while. That's something." She looked up at them — at Mira, and Kael, and past them through the glass in the door to the corridor, where Aldric and Sera were sitting on a bench by the crates, Aldric writing in his notebook, Sera with her head back against the wall and her eyes closed. "And you brought the Scholar back alive, which is what I paid you for. And nobody's dead, which is what I asked for."

She opened a drawer and took out an envelope and counted two hundred marks out of it onto the desk, in notes, slowly. Then she held out her hand.

"Charter," she said.

Mira took it out of her pocket — creased now, and soft at the folds, and smudged where she'd rubbed it all week — and gave it to her. Laine unfolded it, and read it, and took a rubber stamp out of the same drawer, and inked it on a pad, and brought it down on the bottom corner with a thump that rattled the pens.

*GREYCOAT AUTHORITY — CONTRACT DISCHARGED — SATISFACTORY.*

She handed it back.

"Congratulations," said Laine. "You're no longer the new Society." And then, as they turned to go, without looking up from her paperwork: "The Lamplighter doesn't forget faces, Miss Cade. You've made somebody unhappy tonight. Make sure you're worth the trouble."

---

The Wet Ember, again. Same corner table. Nearly midnight.

The fire had burned down to a red glow and the room was emptying as the late shift went off to the factories. Hesper was wiping the bar in long slow sweeps. Fen Harker had escaped from the infirmary, as he'd promised he would, and was perched on a stool at the far end of the bar with a glass of something much stronger than tea and a brand-new notebook, writing so fast the pencil kept breaking. Every so often he looked up at the ceiling, as if he could see through it and through the city to the drains, and smiled to himself.

The four of them sat round the corner table with the two hundred marks in the middle, in a neat stack, and looked at it.

"I've never seen that much money on one table," said Sera.

"You've seen it," said Kael. "It just wasn't ours."

Aldric laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him. He'd been quiet since the station. Mira had watched him writing on the bench, page after page, and she knew without looking what he'd been writing about. The third passage. The cold. The slow breathing at the bottom of the dark.

Sera had taken her compass off its cord and was turning it over on the table. It was an old brass thing with a cracked lid, nothing special. As they watched, the needle swung — not to north, but round, slowly, all the way, as if something under the floor had turned over in its sleep. Then it wavered, and settled, and pointed north like any honest compass.

Sera looked at it for a long moment. Then she put it back on its cord without saying anything, and Mira didn't ask.

---

"Excuse me."

The man was standing at the end of their table. None of them had seen him come over — not even Kael, which Mira had never once known to happen — and Kael's hand went under his jacket and stopped there.

He was in his late forties, perhaps. Handsome, in a well-kept way. Silver coming into dark hair. A good charcoal overcoat over a waistcoat and a silk tie, the kind of clothes that said *I could buy this tavern, and I've chosen to drink in it*. In two fingers he held a slim cigarette that smelled of cloves.

"Please," he said to Kael, quite kindly. "There's no need for that. I'm not here on business. Or rather — I am, but not that sort." He looked round the table at each of them, the way Corva had in the vault, measuring, filing. "Ash and Veil. I've been hearing your name all evening. You've had a busy day."

"Have we," said Mira.

"You cost me a route tonight." He said it without heat, as if remarking on the weather. "A good one. Years to build. And a shipment I'd already been paid for, which is the part that stings, if I'm honest — I'll have to give the money back, and I so dislike giving money back." He drew on the cigarette. "Corva sends her regards, by the way. She was rather impressed. She isn't often."

Nobody said anything.

"No hard feelings," the man went on. "That's what I came to say. It was business. You were hired; you did the job. I respect that more than you'd think." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm and easy, and his eyes above it did not change at all. "It's a small city, Miss Cade. Everyone ends up owing everyone something sooner or later. I expect we'll do business, you and I. One day."

He reached into his coat and laid a coin on the table beside their stack of notes — a heavy silver five-mark piece, more than the drinks had cost, more than a week's worth of drinks.

"For the round," he said. "Corrigan Fels. You'll hear the name."

He nodded to them — to all of them, but last and longest, Mira noticed, to Kael — and turned and walked away between the empty tables, unhurried, and out through the door into the night. The smell of cloves stayed behind him.

Hesper, at the bar, stopped wiping, and looked at the door, and then at them, and shook her head very slightly, and went back to wiping.

---

After a while Mira took the Ledger out of her bag.

It was brand new. She'd bought it on the day of the charter — a fat black book with a marbled edge and ruled columns, the kind clerks used in counting-houses. It was going to hold everything: what came in, what went out, who owed the Society and whom the Society owed. Until tonight it had held nothing at all.

She uncapped her pen and wrote the first line in her small fast hand.

*The Ashwick Job. Insp. R. Laine, Greycoat Authority. Recovery of F. Harker, Gradient Scholars. Discharged satisfactory. Paid: 200 m.*

She looked at it for a moment. Then, beneath it, in the column headed *Owed*, she wrote one more line.

*C. Fels, Lamplighter Syndicate — ?*

She left the amount blank.

The fire settled in the grate. Harker's pencil broke again, and he swore happily and sharpened it with a penknife. Sera leaned her head on Aldric's shoulder, and Aldric, who was not used to that, held very still and went pink and kept writing. Kael sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the door, the way he always would, and touched the thin pink line on his cheek, and then the revolver on his hip, and frowned, very slightly, and let it go.

Outside, the Galvanic hum from the west and the copper sweetness from the east met in the street in front of the Wet Ember and argued quietly about the shape of the world, the way they always had.

And beneath the street, beneath the drains, at the bottom of a passage that was on no map, something cold and patient breathed in the dark, and went on growing.
