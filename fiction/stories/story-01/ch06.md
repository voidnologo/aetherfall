# Chapter 6

Inspector Laine's office at the Central station was a cupboard with a window, and the window looked at a wall.

She sat behind a desk buried in paper and listened to Mira's account with her chin on her fist and her eyes half-closed. She had the air of a cat that hasn't decided whether a mouse is worth the bother. Harker had gone off to the Scholars' infirmary in a Greycoat cab, protesting that he was perfectly well and needed his notebooks. The three crates stood in the corridor under a tarpaulin. A very young constable guarded them and kept glancing at the tarpaulin in case it bit.

Mira told it well. She'd been telling stories to people behind desks since she was fourteen, and she knew how to make one sit up straight and walk in a line.

She took Laine through the Scholar's journal, the foundry and its chain, and the two men in the yard, who were tied to the railings and might still be there if anyone wanted them. Then came the Syndicate, running stolen goods out of the old drains under the Factory Quarter and down the canal, and a woman called Corva, who'd got away. The Scholar was alive, unhurt, fed and four marks up at cards. The evidence was in the corridor.

All of it was true.

She left out the rest, and caught herself doing it, the way you catch your hand reaching for something before you've decided to reach. The crystals growing out of the brick in their hundreds of thousands, pulsing together like one slow heart, went unmentioned. So did Sera's eyes, which had lit blue when she pushed and stayed lit. Aldric's nosebleed never came up, nor the mending that had come so easily it frightened him. Least of all did she mention the third passage at the last fork, cold and still, going down and down, on no plan the city had ever filed.

She didn't decide to leave it out. She just didn't put it in. Some quick, practised part of her had weighed Laine (overworked, understaffed, a hundred problems and no constables) against the thing in the tunnels, which was very large and very strange and not yet understood by anyone. It had made a judgement. *Not her problem yet. Not until we know what it is.*

Mira had been making judgements like that all her life. They had kept her alive on the gradient. She didn't think twice about it.

Kael stood by the door with his hat in his hands and a thin pink line across his cheek. He didn't say anything either. About halfway through she felt him look at her, just once. Then he looked at the wall.

"Well," said Laine when she'd finished. She opened her eyes. "Corva Sable. I know the name. Never had a face to put to it." She made a note. "The Syndicate will have that route shut by midnight and another one open by Thursday. You know that."

"I know that."

"But they've lost the shipment, the drain and four men's worth of nerve. They'll be careful down there for a while. That's something." She looked past Mira and Kael, through the glass in the door, to the corridor. Aldric and Sera sat on a bench by the crates. Aldric was writing in his notebook; Sera had her head back against the wall and her eyes closed. "And you brought the Scholar back alive, which is what I paid you for. Nobody's dead, which is what I asked for."

She took an envelope from a drawer and slowly counted two hundred marks in notes onto the desk. Then she held out her hand.

"Charter," she said.

Mira took it out of her pocket. It was creased now, soft at the folds and smudged where she'd rubbed it all week. Laine unfolded it and read it. She took a rubber stamp out of the same drawer, inked it on a pad and brought it down on the bottom corner with a thump that rattled the pens.

*GREYCOAT AUTHORITY — CONTRACT DISCHARGED — SATISFACTORY.*

She handed it back.

"Congratulations," said Laine. "You're no longer the new Society." As they turned to go, she added without looking up from her paperwork: "The Lamplighter doesn't forget faces, Miss Cade. You've made somebody unhappy tonight. Make sure you're worth the trouble."

---

It was nearly midnight at the Wet Ember, at the same corner table.

The fire had burned down to a red glow. The room was emptying as the late shift went off to the factories. Hesper was wiping the bar in long slow sweeps. Fen Harker had escaped from the infirmary, as promised. He was perched on a stool at the far end of the bar with a glass of something much stronger than tea and a brand-new notebook, writing so fast the pencil kept breaking. Now and then he looked up at the ceiling and smiled to himself, as if he could see through it and through the city to the drains.

The two hundred marks sat in a neat stack in the middle of the corner table. The four of them looked at it.

"I've never seen that much money on one table," said Sera.

"You've seen it," said Kael. "It just wasn't ours."

Aldric laughed. It was a real laugh, surprised out of him. He'd been quiet since the station. Mira had watched him writing on the bench, page after page. She knew without looking what it was about: the third passage, the cold, the slow breathing at the bottom of the dark.

Sera had taken her compass off its cord and was turning it over on the table. It was an old brass thing with a cracked lid, nothing special. As they watched, the needle swung away from north. It went slowly round, all the way, as if something under the floor had turned over in its sleep. Then it wavered and settled on north like any honest compass.

Sera looked at it a while. She put it back on its cord without a word. Mira didn't ask.

---

"Excuse me."

A man was standing at the end of their table. None of them had seen him come over. Mira had never known that to happen to Kael. Kael's hand went under his jacket and stopped there.

He was perhaps in his late forties, handsome in a well-kept way, with silver coming into dark hair. He wore a good charcoal overcoat over a waistcoat and silk tie. His clothes said *I could buy this tavern, and I've chosen to drink in it*. Between two fingers he held a slim cigarette that smelled of cloves.

"Please," he said to Kael, quite kindly. "There's no need for that. I'm not here on business. Or rather I am, but not that sort." His eyes went round the table, measuring and filing, as Corva's had in the vault. "Ash and Veil. I've been hearing your name all evening. You've had a busy day."

"Have we," said Mira.

"You cost me a route tonight." He said it without heat, as if remarking on the weather. "A good one. Years to build. And a shipment I'd already been paid for, which is the part that stings, if I'm honest. I'll have to give the money back, and I so dislike giving money back." He drew on the cigarette. "Corva sends her regards, by the way. She was rather impressed. She isn't often."

Nobody answered.

"No hard feelings," the man went on. "That's what I came to say. It was business. You were hired; you did the job. I respect that more than you'd think." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm and easy, and his eyes above it did not change at all. "It's a small city, Miss Cade. Everyone ends up owing everyone something sooner or later. I expect we'll do business, you and I. One day."

He reached into his coat and laid a heavy silver five-mark piece on the table beside their stack of notes. It was more than the drinks had cost, more than a week of drinks.

"For the round," he said. "Corrigan Fels. You'll hear the name."

He nodded to all of them. Mira noticed that he nodded last and longest to Kael. Then he walked away between the empty tables, unhurried, and out into the night. The smell of cloves stayed behind him.

At the bar, Hesper stopped wiping. She looked at the door, then at them, and shook her head. Then she went back to wiping.

---

After a while Mira took the Ledger out of her bag.

It was brand new. She'd bought it on the day of the charter: a fat black book with a marbled edge and ruled columns, the sort clerks used in counting-houses. It would hold everything that came in and went out, who owed the Society and whom the Society owed. Until tonight it had held nothing at all.

She uncapped her pen and wrote the first line in her small fast hand.

*The Ashwick Job. Insp. R. Laine, Greycoat Authority. Recovery of F. Harker, Gradient Scholars. Discharged satisfactory. Paid: 200 m.*

She looked at it. Then, beneath it, in the column headed *Owed*, she wrote one more line.

*C. Fels, Lamplighter Syndicate — ?*

She left the amount blank.

The fire settled in the grate. Harker's pencil broke again; he swore happily and sharpened it with a penknife. Sera leaned her head on Aldric's shoulder. Aldric, who was not used to that, held still, went pink and kept writing. Kael sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the door, as he always would. He touched the thin pink line on his cheek, then the revolver on his hip. He frowned and let it go.

Outside, in the street in front of the Wet Ember, the Galvanic hum from the west met the copper sweetness from the east. They argued quietly about the shape of the world, as they always had.

Beneath the street and beneath the drains, at the bottom of a passage that was on no map, something cold and patient breathed in the dark and went on growing.
