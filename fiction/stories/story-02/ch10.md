# Chapter 10

The Sootborn didn't ask what had happened. They could see it.

Pell met them at the edge of the settlement with neither surprise nor sympathy, only the flat recognition of a woman who had predicted exactly this and wished she'd been wrong. She looked at Aldric's grey face and the dried blood at his temple. She looked at Kael's revolver, holstered now and three rounds lighter. She looked at Sera's eyes, which still held a glow that had no business showing in daylight.

"Sit," Pell said. "Eat. Then leave."

They sat. The Sootborn brought food unasked, the same stew and dense bread, and well water that tasted sweet and did something for the headache that had been building behind Mira's temples since the clearing. It was kindness with a boundary, hospitality with a perimeter.

"You found it," Pell said. She stood with her arms folded, watching them eat.

"We found it." Mira kept her voice level. "We couldn't retrieve anything. The structure has defences we can't breach."

"Good."

Mira looked at her.

"My grandfather built that mill," Pell said. "That's the truth of the shell of it. My family ran it for two generations before the Wilds came in and took the whole of this country. What's inside it now isn't his work. It isn't anyone's work that we've named. It's been remaking itself in there for fifty years, inside a building my family put up, and it doesn't need your patrons digging at it. It doesn't need the Collective's people prying at it. The mill was ours, in its day. What's living inside it now isn't anyone's to take. It needs to be left alone."

"The people in the forest behind us might disagree."

Pell's face tightened. "The ones who shot at you."

"Ironwork Solutions, working for the Ashworth Collective. They followed us in, and they didn't come to negotiate." Mira held Pell's gaze. "They'll come back. Once we're gone and our confirmation reaches the city, every faction in Ashwick will know the site is real. The Collective will send more people. So will the Covenant. Others will follow. Your settlement is about to become a staging ground whether you want it or not."

The silence afterwards was heavy with what Mira had just done. She had told the whole truth, because Pell deserved it and because the Sootborn needed to prepare for what was coming. Mira was a social operator. She handled information as other people handled money, carefully and strategically, releasing it in measured amounts for particular effects. She didn't give it away for free.

This was free. Pell had earned it by being right about everything, and by feeding them when she didn't have to.

Pell was quiet a long time. Then she said, "We'll manage. We always have." She unfolded her arms. "Finish your food. The road south is clear. No one's passed through since you left. Your friends from Ironwork came in from the northeast, a different approach. They won't beat you back to the road."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Just go."

---

They left within the hour. The walk from the settlement to the road was easier than the walk in. The Wilds ended where they ended, and beyond the tree line the world snapped back to normal so suddenly it felt like surfacing from deep water. The air was thin and clean, the sky grey and ordinary, the trees only trees.

Aldric slept on his feet for the first mile, stumbling in a half-conscious state. Kael kept him on course with an occasional hand at his elbow, as you might steer a boat in a gentle current. By the second mile he'd woken enough to walk straight, though his face still had the translucent look of a man who'd spent everything and hadn't begun to earn it back.

Sera's eyes had dimmed, but not fully. In the fading forest light the glow was barely visible. If you didn't know what you were looking at, you'd tell yourself it was a trick of the evening. By the time they reached the village with the yard, nobody who wasn't checking would see it.

Mira was checking.

The village was where they'd left it, and so was the saloon, sitting in the yard of abandoned cars with a fine film of grey pollen on its bonnet, watched as promised. The man in the leather apron came out of his shed, took another mark for his trouble, and watched with mild interest as Kael coaxed the engine into grudging cooperation. It caught on the third try and ran rough for the first half mile, then smoother, as if it remembered itself now that it was pointed away from the place that had confused it.

Kael drove. Aldric slept in the front passenger seat with his satchel in his lap. Mira watched the road in the wing mirror. Sera rested her forehead against the window glass and said nothing.

When they passed the coaching inn at the crossroads, the one with the hare in flight on its sign, it was lit and busy and smelled of bread and coal smoke. They didn't stop. Speed mattered more than comfort, and a coaching inn's comfort would have dissolved the urgency that was keeping them going.

They drove through the evening into the night. The countryside was dark and quiet: farmland asleep, an occasional light in a cottage window, a dog barking far off. It was a normal, ordinary world that had nothing to do with sealed structures and impossible containers and rifle fire in forests that broke the rules.

At some point Aldric had moved into the back, where he slumped against the door with his head on the window, and Mira sat beside Kael in the front. For a long time she said nothing. Silence was their oldest language. They had spoken it on Harrier Street for years before the Society existed: two people working through the same information, who would compare notes when they were done. The headlamps cut two yellow cones into the dark road ahead. The engine hummed, ordinary and obedient, as a machine does when the world round it behaves.

"They tried to kill us," Mira said finally.

"Yes."

"Not scare us. Not intimidate. Kill."

"Yes."

"That changes things."

Kael was quiet for a long stretch of road. "That changes everything. The Collective authorized lethal force against a chartered Society on a legitimate commission. If that becomes public, it's an act of war. If it doesn't..." He let the sentence hang.

"If it doesn't, they got away with it and they'll do it again."

"So it becomes public."

"Carefully," Mira said. "Through the right channels, at the right time, to the right ears. Not a broadsheet story. A quiet conversation with Inspector Laine, another with Thessaly, and a very careful note in the Society's records that can be produced if needed."

Kael glanced sideways at her. "You're already three moves ahead."

"I'm always three moves ahead. It's the only advantage I have."

---

Dace Rennick was standing at the second crossroads, eight miles south of the Wild edge, in the middle of the road with one hand raised like a man flagging a carriage in a less motorized decade. Kael braked the saloon to a stop twenty yards short of him, because twenty yards short of a man standing in a road at night was the only sensible place to stop.

Dace was smoking the thin cigar he never seemed to finish, its ember a small orange point in the dark. A dark horse stood placidly on the verge behind him, tethered to a fencepost. He strolled to the driver's side with the relaxed air of a man with nowhere pressing to be. That was always a lie. Dace Rennick's casualness was as constructed as Mira's mirroring, and twice as deliberate.

"Evening," he said through the open window. "You look like hell."

"Dace." Mira leaned across Kael's shoulder. Kael's hand drifted to his revolver out of reflex, not threat. "How did you know we'd be on this road?"

"I didn't. I knew you went northeast four days ago on a Covenant commission. I knew Ironwork went northeast a day ahead of you on a Collective commission. I know what's northeast of here, and I know what kind of car the Covenant quartermaster hands out at short notice." He tapped ash from the cigar. "I made a short ride of it and picked a crossroads. When two Societies go into the Wilds on competing commissions and one of them comes out looking like it lost a fight, I find that interesting."

"The Margin's interest is noted."

"The Margin's interest is for sale, as always." Dace stepped back from the window and let Kael kill the engine. Mira got out. The others stayed where they were. Dace was a big man, broad rather than tall, carrying weight that looked soft until you noticed how he moved. "What did you find?"

Mira calculated. Dace Rennick was an information broker. Whatever she told him would spread, controlled and priced, but it would spread. That could be useful or dangerous, depending on what she let go.

She gave him the shape and kept the details.

"The site is real. A pre-Tear structure in the Wilds, roughly two days from the settlement on foot, though the spatial distortion makes that unreliable. The structure holds an artifact. The artifact is out of reach, defended by mechanisms we don't understand and couldn't get past. Nobody is taking it home."

Dace's eyebrows rose. "Out of reach."

"Completely. The site itself is the find, not the artifact. And Ironwork Solutions tried to stop us bringing that confirmation back. Lethal force, in the Wilds."

She let that sit. Dace was clever enough to hear what it meant: the Collective had gone beyond professional rivalry into something that would cost it badly if it became known. That was worth money. It was worth more than money. It was leverage.

Dace drew on his cigar and studied her through the smoke. "Lethal force against a chartered Society. The Collective's getting ambitious."

"Or desperate."

"Same thing, in my experience." He paused. "I'll keep this for now. The site confirmation is sellable. The Ironwork business is delicate. I'll hold it until the price is right, or until holding it costs more than selling it."

"That's your business."

"It is." He smiled the easy, charming smile that made you forget for a moment that he'd built a career on knowing what other people wanted kept quiet. "Safe travels, Mira. You've had an interesting week."

He walked back to his horse, swung up with more ease than a man of his build should have managed, and rode off the way they'd come. The cigar's ember shrank to a point and vanished. Mira stood by the open driver's door and thought about information: who held it, and what it was worth when the world was watching.

---

Ashwick appeared on the horizon at midmorning: chimney smoke first, then rooftops, then the grey sprawl of a city that had never been beautiful and wasn't about to start. The factories were running. The Galvanic hum reached them before the city's outline resolved, a vibration in the road that Mira felt through the floorboards. The station clock tower was visible from two miles out, too far to read but reassuring, as familiar things are after days of the unfamiliar.

Kael eased the car down through the outer districts. The engine was happy now, the ignition firing as it should in air that made sense again. They stopped at the Covenant's depot long enough for Mira to hand in the keys and a damage report. There was nothing to report. The saloon had come back intact, which surprised the quartermaster more than it should have. Then they walked the last mile to Harrier Street, as journeys in Ashwick always ended, on their own feet, carrying what they could. They passed the coaching stops where the city's ordinary commerce carried on, and people working and shopping and arguing about prices who had no idea that four people were walking in from the Wilds with knowledge that would reshape the city's politics.

The flat on Harrier Street was as they'd left it: two rooms above the cobbler's shop, the stairs groaning under their weight, the door sticking in its frame as it always did in damp weather. Inside it smelled of leather and lamp oil. There was the kitchen table where Kael cleaned his revolver, the corner chair where Aldric read, the window where Sera sat and watched the Quarter.

Home, such as it was.

Aldric went straight to the corner chair and lowered himself into it as carefully as a man lowering himself into water. He opened his notebook and began transcribing in the small, precise hand that needed a magnifying lens to read. Whatever conclusions he was building about the chamber, the container, the agency remaking the interior and the categories that didn't hold, he was committing them to his records before exhaustion wore their edges away.

Kael sat at the kitchen table and took his revolver apart. He cleaned each piece with methodical care, removing what the Wilds had left behind: a faint stiffness in the spring, a barely visible corrosion on the cylinder pin that shouldn't have been there after four days. The gun had partly failed him. The knife hadn't. He cleaned the knife too.

Sera sat by the window. Her eyes looked normal again, the glow finally faded to nothing in daylight. She was writing in her journal, and Mira couldn't read her face. Whatever had happened to her in the Wilds, the amplification, the power, the eyes that had glowed like lanterns, she was working through it alone in the only record she kept, in handwriting nobody else was allowed to see.

Mira stood at the kitchen table and opened the Ledger.

The Covenant's fee was set out in the terms Thessaly had given: full payment for confirming the site, a bonus for recovering the artifact. They'd earned the first and missed the second. The numbers were good, two months of running costs, maybe three. It was enough to keep the flat, keep the team equipped and keep the Society going until the next job.

She noted the payment and the expenses: supplies, provisions, the food bought from the Sootborn. She noted the date, the job and the outcome. She did not note what lay underneath: that they had been sent as expendable assets on thin intelligence and had come back with confirmation that would make them indispensable.

The Covenant would want them again. The Collective would want them watched. The Margin would want them informed. Inspector Laine would want a quiet briefing, and the Gradient Scholars would want Aldric's notes. Every faction in Ashwick was about to learn that a pre-Tear structure stood in the Wilds, that it held an artifact nobody could reach, and that Ash & Veil Recovery was the Society that had found it.

They had been sent because they were available and unimportant. They were coming back as the people who knew where the most significant discovery in fifty years was, what it looked like inside, and what had happened when they tried to reach it.

The Ledger balanced. The next job would be harder, and the one after that harder still. Somewhere in the Wilds, in a clearing the forest was probably already rearranging, the shell of a mill that people had built long ago sat on warm ground. Inside it, in a chamber no people had built, a container rested on a pedestal behind a lock nobody could open, patient and sealed and waiting.

Mira closed the Ledger and went to make tea. There would be letters to write, reports to file, and careful conversations to have with exactly the right people at exactly the right time.

Tomorrow.

Tonight they were home, the job was done, and that was enough.
