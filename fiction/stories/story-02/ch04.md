# Chapter 4

Aldric catalogued the settlement as he'd catalogued everything since he was sixteen: systematically, completely, and with a persistent anxiety that he was missing the detail that mattered most.

The buildings were well made. No architect had laid them out and no surveyor had lined the foundations, but they had the practical competence of people who'd done hard work before and knew a wall had to be plumb and a roof had to shed rain. The timber was local and the stone gathered from a nearby streambed. The thatch was a bracken fern Aldric didn't recognize from any botanical text, though it clearly thrived in the Aetheric saturation. The gardens were extraordinary. Every text on Aetheric botany predicted accelerated growth in sustained Aetheric environments. Seeing it in practice was something else entirely: tomatoes fruiting in early spring, herbs waist-high, flowers in colours that seemed slightly too saturated to be natural.

He noted all of this. He also noted what the settlement lacked. There was no Galvanic equipment of any kind: no electric lights, no tool more complex than a hand saw or a hammer, nothing that might push back against the Aetheric environment. These people had committed fully. They'd walked away from everything that hummed with the Engine's resonance and built with their hands and whatever the forest gave them.

The evening meal was communal, at a long table between two buildings lit by candles that burned with a steady, too-bright flame. The Sootborn ate together and didn't invite the Society to join them. Pell brought their food separately: bowls of stew, thick bread, and well water that tasted faintly sweet. It was professional hospitality, without warmth.

Aldric ate slowly and watched. The research instinct was hard to suppress, and tonight he didn't try.

The child's drawing was the first piece. He'd noticed it on the wall of the second building while Mira was talking with Pell: two charcoal drawings layered on the same patch of rough timber, almost certainly by the same hand. The lower one was a building, a tall narrow box at one end with round cylinders attached, a pitched roof and a chimney like a pencil. Aldric knew the silhouette before he'd finished looking. It was a grain elevator and mill complex. He'd driven past three on the road out of Ashwick. The child had either seen the real thing somewhere or been told what one looked like by someone who had.

The upper drawing, layered over the lower like a correction, was different. It had corners, and what might have been corridors, drawn with the confident imprecision of a child drawing something described rather than seen. The proportions were wrong in a way that nagged at him: too many right angles, no curves at all. The lower drawing was a familiar shape drawn clumsily. The upper was an alien shape drawn carefully. They sat together on the plank as if the child had been trying to show how one had become the other.

The second piece came from the communal table. Two women were talking quietly, but sound carried in the clear air, and Aldric had spent twelve years in academic settings where the most important conversations happened just below speaking volume. One of them mentioned "the clearing past the second ridge." The other made a flattening gesture, palm down, as if pressing something shut. Then she noticed Aldric watching, and the conversation stopped.

The third piece was the well.

He'd gone to refill his water flask after the meal and found the old man sitting on the stone lip, smoking a pipe that smelled of something herbal and sharp. Aldric had heard someone call him Thom. He regarded Aldric with the quiet patience of someone who expected questions and had already decided which to answer.

"You're the scholar," Thom said.

"I was. I'm with the Society now."

"Same thing with a different coat." Thom drew on his pipe. "You want to know about the place they found."

"If you're willing to tell me."

"I'm willing to tell you what I know, which is less than you want and more than Pell would like." The smoke he breathed out curled upward and hung too long in the heavy air. "Five of them went in. Explorers, the younger ones, the ones who wanted to see how far the forest went. They were mapping, or trying to. Pell's idea, originally. Know your territory. Good sense."

"How far did they get?"

Thom's face changed. Aldric knew the look: someone trying to convey an experience that resisted language. "That's the question, isn't it. They said two days. My count said three sunrises, but the sun does things in there. Rises in the wrong place. Sets too fast. They packed for a week and came back with most of their food uneaten, which means either they weren't gone as long as they thought or they weren't hungry." He scratched his chin. "Adi, the one with the strongest sense, the young woman, she said the distances were wrong. You could see a ridge and walk towards it for an hour and it wouldn't get closer. Then you'd turn round and it'd be right behind you."

"Spatial distortion," Aldric said. "Consistent with deep Aetheric saturation. The literature describes..."

"I don't care what the literature describes." Thom said it without heat. "I care that five people I know went into the forest healthy and came back strange. Not hurt. Strange. Quieter. Like they'd seen something they were still deciding how to talk about."

Aldric closed his mouth and listened. He'd found it was the most productive research technique available.

"They found a clearing," Thom went on. "Past the second ridge, which doesn't mean what it sounds like, because the ridges move, or the paths do, or something does. The clearing was real. Solid ground, flat, like something had been laid out by people once." He paused. "We knew about that part, mind. Before the Wilds came in, that whole stretch was farmed. My father was a boy when the Tear happened. He grew up on this land before it turned. He remembered a processing mill out that way. Pell's grandfather owned it, as it happens. The old man, not our Pell. Grain elevator and a roller mill, employed maybe thirty hands. You could hear the shift bell from here on a still morning. When the Wilds came in, the mill went with everything else."

"And it's still standing?"

"The outside is. Adi said so. The silos, the loading dock, part of the mill house. Weathered, but there. She recognized it from photographs her mother kept. Pell's family kept the pictures, out of stubbornness or for evidence, I don't know which." Thom drew on his pipe. "But she said when they went inside, it wasn't the mill any more. The inside was something else. Made of something smooth. No seams, no joints. Like it was poured or grown, not built. She called them hallways, where the grain chutes and the machinery should have been. Openings that led into rooms that weren't mill rooms. They went in."

"How far?"

"Far enough to find something they couldn't take. A room with an object in it. Sealed, she said, behind something she couldn't touch or push through or cast through. She said the building changed round them while they were inside. Not fast. Slow, like the walls were breathing. They came out when the way back started looking different from the way in."

Aldric's hand was shaking. It took him a moment to realize he'd been gripping his pencil so hard his knuckles had gone white. He relaxed his fingers and wrote in small, precise letters, filling the margins of a page that already held notes on the settlement's garden rotation.

"One more thing," he said. "Can you see the structure from here?"

Thom pointed east, to a low rise at the settlement's edge where the tree line climbed. "Eastern ridge. On a clear morning, before the canopy mist comes in. Adi showed me once through a spyglass. It's a shape in the trees, lighter than the forest round it. Looks close." He drew on his pipe. "It's not."

---

The eastern ridge was a ten-minute walk from the settlement. Aldric made it in the grey light before dawn, and Kael came with him, carrying the binoculars without needing to be asked.

The ridge was a modest hump of bare stone above the tree line, with a view east over the canopy. The forest stretched to the horizon, a sea of green touched with the faint glow that seemed to live in the deeper foliage. Mist hung in the hollows between the ridges. There were three or four ridges visible from here, rising like waves in the forest floor.

Kael raised the binoculars and scanned east. He was quiet a long time.

"There," he said, and handed them over.

Aldric looked. Past the first ridge and the second, or what might have been the second, since at that distance they blurred together, shapes broke the canopy. They were paler than the forest round them and caught the early light. He had to work a moment to read the silhouette, and then he had it. Two tall cylinders stood together beside a lower rectangular block. There was the stub of what had once been a smokestack, broken near the top, and the outline of a loading bay the canopy had half eaten. It was a pre-Tear processing mill, weathered by fifty years of forest and saturation but unmistakable if you knew what you were looking at. He had driven past a dozen like it on working farms between Ashwick and here. The bones were the same.

But the bones were wrong.

The silos weren't the concrete grey he expected. Something had bleached or replaced them. They caught the light too pale and too smooth, and the angles where roof met wall didn't quite align, as though the structure underneath had been softened and reset into the shape it used to hold.

He estimated the distance at two miles, perhaps three. An easy walk on flat ground in normal conditions.

*Looks close*, Thom had said. *It's not.*

"How far?" Kael asked.

"By eye, two to three miles. The Sootborn explorers took what they described as two days to reach it, though their sense of elapsed time is unreliable." Aldric lowered the binoculars. "The spatial distortion in deep Aetheric zones doesn't follow consistent rules. The distance might be ten miles in practice. It might be fifty. It might be two miles that take two days to cross because the space between here and there isn't behaving."

"Can we do it?"

Aldric thought about softening the uncertainty. He could present a probability that made the enterprise sound calculated rather than a guess. He decided against it. Kael had asked a straight question and deserved a straight answer.

"I don't know," he said. "Sera can navigate by Aetheric sense, which is the most reliable method in these conditions. We have landmarks that may or may not hold true. We have a general direction. We don't have a route, a timeline, or any certainty about what we'll find when we get there."

"But you want to go."

"I've spent twelve years studying zones from the outside. Measuring drift patterns. Analysing data other people collected." Aldric looked east at the shape in the trees that shouldn't exist, and felt something he wasn't used to feeling: a desire that had nothing to do with method. "Yes. I want to go."

Kael nodded. He took the binoculars back and looked east once more. Aldric could see him doing what he always did: reading the terrain, weighing the risks, deciding whether the people behind him would survive what lay ahead.

"Then we go," Kael said. "Let's tell the others."
