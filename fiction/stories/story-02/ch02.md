# Chapter 2

The Society car was a boxy grey saloon. The Covenant's quartermaster had handed it to Mira at first light along with a fuel chit and a shrugging assurance that it would probably get them as far as they needed. Kael drove the first stretch out of the city. When the group was nervous Kael drove most things; he preferred a wheel in his hand to a seat he couldn't steer.

The road out of Ashwick ran northeast through a mile of residential sprawl before the buildings gave up and let the land breathe.

Kael marked the change as he'd marked a thousand others walking beats, by what changed around him. Row houses thinned to cottages, and cottages to farmsteads. The cobbles ran out into macadam, and the macadam into packed earth oiled against dust. The factory hum faded first, then the Aetheric sweetness that drifted from the Quarter on a morning wind. After that there was only the road, the fields, and the grey sky of early spring pressing down on all of it.

Aldric rode beside him with a map flat on his knees, though the map grew less useful by the mile. Mira sat in the back with one boot on the running board and the window half down, watching the road behind them in the wing mirror. Sera had the seat behind Kael. She was quiet, one hand on the door frame, her head tilted towards the open window like a dog reading the wind.

At first the engine was a friendly animal. It chewed up the miles with the steady four-cylinder insistence that had made these cars so common in the last decade, a machine that didn't ask much and did what was asked of it. Kael had never owned one and couldn't have afforded to, but he'd driven patrol wagons on the same frame for years. The pedals fell where his feet expected them.

At midmorning they stopped at a coaching inn, a two-storey stone building at a crossroads. In the last decade it had added a filling pump and a pair of oil drums to its original business without bothering to repaint its sign, a hare in flight weathered into a suggestion. The innkeeper was a broad, red-faced man. He sold them water, bread, petrol and directions they didn't need, and didn't ask where they were headed. That was coaching-inn etiquette. People passing through had their own business. The innkeeper's business was selling water and bread and, now, fuel.

Kael ate standing, watching the road behind them while the others rested. Mira caught his eye.

"Expecting company?"

"Just looking." He finished the bread and dusted his hands. "Fresh tracks on the road this morning. Cart ruts laid over yesterday's rain, and tyre tracks over the cart. Wide tread. Moving fast, same direction we're going."

"Could be anyone."

"Could be." He didn't elaborate. Mira was clever enough to get from *the Collective dispatched Ironwork* to *fresh tyre tracks heading northeast*. She was also clever enough not to say it out loud at a filling pump where the walls were thin and the innkeeper had a professional interest in gossip.

They moved on. Past the crossroads the road narrowed into a track used more by local farmers than by through-travellers. The macadam gave out first. After it came old paving stones laid a generation ago and never replaced when the frost pulled them apart, and the saloon's tyres thumped over the gaps. The land rose gently into low hills, the countryside gathering itself into folds. A village came and went: a church, a general store, a cluster of houses round a well. An old woman on her porch watched them pass with the idle interest of someone with nothing better to look at. A dog ran beside the wheel for a quarter of a mile before losing interest.

Past the village they met the first sign.

It was bolted to an iron post rusted to the colour of the soil. Its enamel face was bubbled and its blue field faded almost to grey, but the black letters were still legible if you knew what to look for:

**AETHERIC INFLUENCE AHEAD**
**LEAVE VEHICLES — TURN BACK**
**BY ORDER — INTERIM AUTHORITY**

Aldric looked up from his map.

"Post-Fall," he said. "The Interim Authority put these up in the first years after the Tear, when they still believed the zones could be fenced. That sign is about forty years older than this car."

"It's still here," Kael said.

"Yes. Who was going to take it down?"

They drove past. The sign dwindled in the wing mirror. A mile further on there was another, knocked sideways by some long-ago collision, its face cracked and its words fading into the same uselessness. A third lay flat in the ditch with weeds growing through the post holes. Kael found himself counting them without meaning to. Someone in a city office had once believed that a row of signs could hold the shape of the old world against what was coming, and the Authority had put them up in sequence. The signs had been wrong. So had the belief.

The engine coughed for the first time ten miles past the third sign.

It was a small stutter in the firing order, the kind of thing a driver might put down to dirty fuel. Kael didn't. He watched the road and waited. Two minutes later the engine coughed again, and this time the saloon lost a hair of speed before recovering. The tachometer needle dropped, climbed and dropped. The ignition timing was drifting in ways that had nothing to do with the carburettor.

"Here we go," Kael said.

"Already?" Sera had been watching the trees by the road. She turned towards the front, her face bright with something other than alarm. "I can feel it."

"Describe."

"There's weight in the air. Not like the Quarter. Heavier. Lower. Something under the ground, if that makes sense."

"It doesn't," Kael said, "but I believe you."

The paving stones ran out. The road became packed earth again, then earth rutted by wooden wheels, then really just a farmer's path kept open by use. The fields here were less well kept than those nearer the city. Hedgerows had thickened, unpruned for a decade. A stone wall was missing its top two courses, a gate hung off its hinges. The air changed by small degrees. The trees were taller than they should have been. The birdsong thinned to a few calls Kael didn't recognize, and then to silence.

The engine coughed again, harder, and ran rough for a quarter of a mile before smoothing out.

He fell into a pattern without thinking about it, letting the revs drop when the mechanism complained and coaxing the saloon forward rather than demanding. Driving was a conversation between driver and machine, and the machine had begun to speak another language. Out past the edge, where the signs had been telling people to turn back for forty years, the conversation would stop being productive at all.

"You've studied the Wilds," Kael said.

"Academically. Zone drift patterns, Aetheric accumulation data. I've never been inside a Wild Zone stronger than moderate." Aldric shifted the satchel between his feet. "The literature suggests the edges are navigable. In the deeper zones the reports become contradictory."

"Contradictory how?"

"People who go in together come out with different accounts of what they experienced. I don't mean conflicting testimony. I mean genuinely different experiences, as if the zone doesn't present itself the same way to different observers." Aldric paused, choosing between the precise answer and the useful one. "The practical concern is navigation. Distance and time don't behave normally in strong Aetheric zones. The more saturated the environment, the less reliable ordinary wayfinding becomes."

"Compasses?"

"Useless past moderate saturation. Watches are unreliable. Landmarks shift."

"So we navigate by what?"

Aldric glanced into the back, where Sera sat with her head slightly tilted, her attention split between the road ahead and something only she could feel. "In the academic literature, the most reliable navigators in Wild Zones are high-sensitivity casters. They can feel the direction and strength of the Aetheric current. It isn't a compass. It's closer to how a fish finds its way in a river."

"By feeling which way the water pushes."

"Essentially."

Kael considered this. "So we're relying on Sera."

"In the Wilds, yes. She may be our most important asset."

Kael said nothing. He trusted Sera: her intentions, her courage, the basic decency she carried like a lantern in a dark room. What he didn't trust was the thing she reached into when she cast, the vast, alien force that gave her power and asked for pieces of her in return. In the Wilds, where that force was strongest, she'd be reaching deeper than ever. The Aether would give her more and take more, and nobody fully understood the bargain.

His revolver sat holstered at his hip. He didn't touch it. Out here, on the last honest stretch of road, it was just a well-kept machine doing exactly what it was designed to do. The car under his hands was the same, for now. In a few hours, or a few miles, both would change. Their mechanisms would start to question themselves, as all mechanisms did when reality got uncertain.

The knife at the small of his back didn't have that problem. It was steel, with an edge. It worked the same in a Dead Zone or a Deep Wild, and it would work the same in whatever they were driving towards.

The afternoon wore on. The road climbed into hills that grew less gentle, steeper folds over rockier soil, farmland giving way to pasture and then to scrub. The trees thickened. This wasn't Wild yet, only forest of the kind that had always been here, oaks and beeches with new leaves pale green against the grey sky. But they were taller than they should have been. Kael noticed it as he noticed everything that didn't fit: a quiet wrongness, an excess of growth with no farming explanation.

The engine coughed three more times in the next hour, each time worse. At the end of the hour the road rose over a final shoulder of land and there was the last village: six low buildings and a barn gathered round a well. At the edge of the settlement stood a ward-post, a pine trunk stripped of bark and carved with symbols Kael didn't recognize.

And there was a yard full of abandoned cars.

There were a dozen, maybe fifteen, scattered without pattern in a field beside the road. The nearest had been there for decades, a boxy pre-war thing with its tyres rotted to the rims and ivy growing through the floorboards. The furthest was newer, its paint mostly intact, its headlamps reflecting the grey sky. Between them stood every year of motoring since the Tear. Each car was parked with a kind of patience, as if its owner had meant to come back and then reconsidered, or hadn't, or couldn't.

Kael slowed the saloon and coasted up to the gate. A man in a leather apron came out of a shed that was half forge and half tool shop, wiping his hands on a rag.

"Going in?" His accent was flat country.

"That's the idea."

"Most folks park it here. Car won't get you another mile past the post, and if it does, it won't come back." The man glanced at the saloon's grille, then at Kael, then at the three in the car. His eyes rested on Sera longest. Folk in a village like this knew how to spot someone who could feel the zone. "A mark a week for watching. Two marks extra if you want me to check the engine and the tyres. No promises on condition when you come back, but I'll keep the kids and the crows off it."

Kael looked at Mira. Mira nodded.

"We'll take the watching," Kael said. "Not the checking. She'll sit as she is."

"Suit yourself."

They got out. The air at the gate was different again. It no longer had the slow weight of a few miles back; it was something more distinct, a thickness with edges. Kael felt it against his skin like the humidity before a storm. Sera stood a pace from the car with her hand flat on her breastbone, breathing slowly. Aldric was studying the ward-post as if it were a type specimen.

"Crude work," he murmured. "Folk practice, not scholarly casting. But the symbols are in the right arrangement. Someone here had a grandfather who knew what he was doing."

They moved what they could carry from the car to their packs. Kael hefted the kit and adjusted the strap on his shoulder. Behind them the saloon sat ticking as its engine cooled, small and ordinary, already beginning to join the company of the yard.

The villagers watched with the flat, measuring gaze of people who lived on a boundary and had learned to size up everyone who crossed it. There was no hostility in it. A man mending a fence straightened and stared until they were past. A woman in a doorway pulled her child inside and closed the door, less against the Society than against whatever its passing implied.

Beyond the village the road simply stopped, as if its builders had reached a point where going on seemed unwise and turned round. Ahead, the tree line rose like a wall. The forest beyond was denser than anything they'd passed, the canopy closed and dark, the undergrowth grown thick without any human hand to check it.

The air was different too. Kael couldn't taste it as Sera could, or feel the current she described. But he could feel a thickness, a presence, the sense of standing at the edge of a room where something very large was breathing.

"That's it," Sera said. She was staring at the tree line as people stare at the sea from a dock, thinking less about the water than about what it said of the size of the world. "The Wilds."

Kael checked the road behind them once more. It was empty. The tyre tracks they'd followed most of the day hadn't come into this yard of rusting cars. They had gone on past the village, further east, onto a road that wasn't quite a road. Whoever was ahead of them was pushing their machine further than the village man thought wise. Kael filed that away.

"Let's find the settlement," he said, and started walking towards the trees.
