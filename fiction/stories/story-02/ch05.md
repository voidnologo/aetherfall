# Chapter 5

They left the settlement at dawn and were lost within an hour.

It wasn't how you got lost in a city, taking a wrong turn and ending up three blocks from where you meant to be. It was how you got lost in a dream, where the mistake lay in assuming navigation was possible at all.

Mira tracked it as carefully as she could. They entered the forest heading east, following a gap in the undergrowth that might have been a game trail or might have been nothing. Sera walked point. It reversed the usual order, and Kael accepted it without comment, because in here Sera's sense was the only compass that worked. The real compass round her neck had stopped moving entirely. Its needle pointed at a fixed spot that wasn't any direction Mira recognized.

The trees were enormous. They were broad as well as tall, their trunks smooth and silver-grey, their roots spreading across the forest floor like the fingers of buried hands. The canopy was high enough that the space beneath felt like a cathedral, vaulted and dim and filled with a green-gold light that glowed faintly. The undergrowth was thick but passable: ferns, moss, low bushes with leaves darker than they should have been, everything growing with an enthusiasm close to aggression. The air was warm and heavy and tasted of copper and soil and something sweet Mira couldn't name.

By Kael's watch they had walked forty minutes when the rock appeared.

It was distinctive: a tall, narrow stone standing upright at the foot of a ridge, pale grey, its surface worn smooth by something other than weather. From Thom's descriptions Aldric had picked it as a likely landmark. They passed it, noted its position, and went on east.

Twenty minutes later the rock was ahead of them again.

Mira stopped. "We've been here."

"We haven't turned," Kael said. He'd been tracking their course by pace count and by what light came through the canopy. His voice was flat. He wasn't arguing, only reporting.

"Sera?"

Sera was frowning, her head tilted, her hands open at her sides as they went when she was reading the air. "The current shifted. I felt it move, like a river changing course. The direction I was following is different now."

"Different how?"

"It's still east. But east isn't where it was."

Mira looked at the rock. It was the same rock, the same shape, the same smooth pale stone, standing exactly where it had stood half an hour ago. Either they'd walked in a circle without any of them noticing, or the forest had rearranged itself round them.

Neither option was encouraging.

"Adjust and continue," Mira said. "Sera, follow the current as it is now. Kael, mark trees."

Kael drew his knife and scored a cross into the nearest trunk. The bark parted easily over pale wood. He looked at the mark, nodded, and they moved on.

The marks helped, though the forest didn't respect them. Twice more in the next hour a marked tree turned up ahead of them when it should have been behind. But the pattern of the marks showed them when the forest had shifted. When a mark appeared in the wrong place they knew the ground had moved, and Sera could adjust.

It was slow and exhausting, and the deeper they went the worse it got.

Time was the cruellest trick. Mira checked Kael's watch now and then and found the intervals didn't match her own sense of them. A stretch that felt like twenty minutes was five by the watch. One that felt like five was forty. After the third mismatch she stopped checking. Not knowing was better than the gap between felt time and measured time.

Kael's revolver was the first piece of equipment to go. He checked it at what Mira guessed was the two-hour mark, or the four-hour, or the one-hour. The action was sluggish and the cylinder resisted turning. It wasn't jammed. It was reluctant, as if the mechanism had developed opinions about whether it wanted to work. He holstered it without expression and moved his knife to his main hand.

"My casting is harder too," Aldric said quietly. He'd been trying small workings, a Detect here, a Reveal there, to map the Aetheric landscape. They took more out of him than they should have and left him grey-faced and sweating. "Not as I expected. The power doesn't resist. Quite the opposite. Every cantrip comes in at full strength when I want a whisper. A Detect I meant to cast at six feet went out to fifteen and brought back twice as much as I could hold in my head at once. It's like writing with a pen too full of ink. Everything bleeds. The effort is all in holding the spell down to a useful size."

Sera, by contrast, was incandescent. Mira wouldn't have been surprised if it were literal. The flush on her face wasn't exertion. Her eyes were bright and her movements fluid. When she reached for the Aether to test their direction, Mira could see it: a ripple in the air round her hands, there for a fraction of a second, like heat haze shaped by intent.

"I can feel the structure," Sera said. They were resting on a fallen trunk wider than the kitchen table at the flat. "It's a pull. Not strong, but constant. Like a stone at the bottom of a stream, with the current flowing round it."

"How far?"

Sera closed her eyes. The ripple came again, stronger, and the hair rose on Mira's arms. "Close. Maybe an hour. But..."

"But time doesn't mean what it should in here."

"Right."

Mira looked at her team. Kael was watchful and steady, knife in hand, scanning the forest with the patience of a man who'd decided unpredictable terrain was just another beat to walk. Aldric was tired but determined, his satchel clutched to him, his notebook already half full. Sera was alive in a way Mira had never seen. Her connection to this place was clearly powerful, and clearly not entirely under her control.

They were working on guesswork and instinct in a place that followed no rules. They had no guide, no map, and no reliable sense of time or distance. Their ordinary equipment was failing by degrees. And somewhere behind them, back through the shifting forest and past the settlement, Ironwork Solutions was either following their trail or making its own way to the same place.

No briefing prepared you for this part of a job, when the plan dissolved and all you had left were the people you'd chosen and your bet that they were enough.

"We keep going," Mira said. "Stay close. Mark everything."

The clearing appeared an hour later, or three hours later. It was sometime in the middle of the afternoon, if the light through the canopy could be trusted, which it couldn't.

For the last stretch the undergrowth had been thickening, chest-high ferns whose fronds trembled at their passing as if startled. They pushed through the last of it and stepped into open space. The canopy thinned and grey light poured into a clearing perhaps two hundred yards across. The ground was flat and bare. It looked like packed earth, but when Mira knelt and pressed her palm to it, it felt like stone. Warm stone, with no source of warmth she could find.

The structure sat in the middle of the clearing, and first of all it was a mill.

Mira hadn't expected to recognize it. The silhouette came from someone else's life a century ago: a pair of grain silos rising from a lower mill house, a loading bay yawning open on one side, the stub of a smokestack snapped off at half its height. Fifty years of forest had gnawed at its edges. A roof had collapsed on the far side. One silo cap was gone, leaving the cylinder open to the sky. Ivy had made a long assault on the wall facing them and failed, because the wall wasn't anything ivy knew how to climb. Six feet up the plants had died and dried into brown rope on pale stone that wasn't stone.

That was the second thing. The shell was still, in outline, a mill, but its material had been replaced. This wasn't overgrowth. The concrete, steel and timber of the original had been bleached and remade into the seamless pale stuff in front of them. The mill's shape remained and its substance was gone. The silos were the same diameter as ever. The loading bay opened onto the same rectangle of ground where wagons had once backed up to the chutes. Only the matter of the thing was wrong.

Sera had stopped walking. She was staring at the structure with an expression Mira couldn't read, something more private than fear or awe.

The openings, the loading bay, a doorway in the mill house, a gap where a window had been knocked out, were darker than the early afternoon could explain. The light that should have spilled into them simply didn't.

"That's it," Aldric said, hardly louder than breath. "Somebody built this. A long time ago. People put up the silos and milled grain here. And then..." He fumbled for his notebook with hands gone clumsy. "And then something else started building inside it. Something's still building inside it. Look at the walls. That's not the original material. That's not *any* original material."

Here the Aether was visible. It wasn't the faint glow of the deeper forest but real light, pale blue-white, drifting like dust motes in the still air round the structure. It was thickest where the shell had been most completely remade.

Kael said nothing. He was looking at the openings, counting them, measuring angles, doing what he always did with a space he might have to fight in or escape from. His knife was in his hand. The revolver sat useless in its holster.

Mira looked at the structure. In the way of things that shouldn't be alive but somehow occupied space with intention, the structure looked back.

"We go in," she said. "Stay together. Mark your entry. If anything feels wrong, we get out. No arguments, no second chances."

They walked towards the pale shape in the clearing, and the Aetheric light drifted round them like snow.
