# Chapter 5

The literature said that a sustained working was like carrying a weight uphill. The literature was wrong. Aldric had been holding the wall for three minutes, and it was not like carrying anything. It was like being slowly emptied through a hole in the bottom of himself, and having to keep the hole open with his own hands.

He knelt in his nest of rocks with both palms pressed flat to the stone, as though he could steady the working by steadying himself, and looked across the valley at what he had made.

It stood across the road where it climbed out of the ford, a little higher than a house and a little wider than the road. In the morning sun it was almost invisible, a faint distortion, like the air above a stove. But he could feel every inch of it. He had built it in the space of one breath, out of nothing, on the instant the rifle cracked — the frame thrown out across the valley like a net, the anchors driven into the stone of the far bank, the shape pulled taut between them, and then the Aether let in, measured and bounded and *held*. It had been the fastest construction of his life. It had been, he thought with a detached and slightly hysterical pride, rather good.

And every second since, it had been drinking him.

He could feel his reserves the way he could feel his own pulse — not as a number but as a pressure, a fullness, and the fullness was going down. A little under half, now. Less than he'd guessed. The outrider had tried to drive his motorcycle through the wall in the first minute, and the wall had thrown him back into the river, and the shock of it had gone through the working and up the anchors and into Aldric's hands like a blow on a funny-bone. A man with a rifle had emptied a magazine into it at point-blank range. Each round had cost him. He'd felt them all.

But it held. The convoy was boxed. Kael was on the valley floor.

Aldric did not look at Kael. He had decided, before the engines came, that he would not, because looking would make him want to do something, and his only job was to hold. He looked at the wall, and at the switchback, and at the anchors. He breathed. He held.

He looked anyway.

---

He had the field glasses up when the gun failed.

He did not know, at first, what he was seeing. Kael at the back of the gun transport, turning. A grey-coated man at the front of it. Kael's arm coming up — fast, so fast — and then simply stopping. As if a film had caught in the projector. Aldric's whole body went cold from the scalp down.

Then the grey man fired, and Kael fell.

Aldric did not hear the shot. The valley was full of shots. He saw Kael go back against the wheel and down out of sight, and he saw, through the glasses, the way he went down — all at once, legs first, the way a building goes when someone has knocked out a supporting wall — and he knew. He had spent fifteen years studying the body and a year patching it up in the neighbourhood clinics, and he knew what it meant when a man fell like that. It meant something deep. Something that held him up had been broken.

*Hold*, said the part of him that had built the wall. *Hold. That is the job.*

He held.

He watched Kael crawl. He watched the dark trail on the gravel behind him. He counted, without meaning to, the seconds until the fuses must go.

Then the big lorry went up, and the world went flat.

He had never felt anything like it. It was not an explosion, to the part of him that was holding the wall. It was an absence. The dead-zone engine on the lorry's back tore itself apart, and as it died it did, for one instant, everything it had been built to do — and the Aether in the valley simply *wasn't there*. The air went grey and empty and dead. Across the valley, his wall shuddered from end to end like a sail with the wind gone out of it. The anchors slipped. The frame began to fold.

He caught it.

He did not know how. He would try to write it down, later, and fail. Something in him reached out across a quarter-mile of dead air and took the folding working in both hands and *held it up* — held it with no Aether in it at all, nothing but the frame and his own stubbornness — for the long, long instant until the Aether came seeping back into the valley. It cost him more than the whole three minutes before. He felt it go out of him like blood. When the moment passed and the wall stood shimmering again, he was at less than a quarter, and his hands were shaking against the stone, and there was a taste in his mouth like old pennies.

Then the second lorry went up, and through the smoke he saw Kael.

He was lying on his back against a spur of grey rock at the foot of this very ridge, perhaps a hundred and fifty yards below. He had been thrown. He was not moving. His arm lay at an angle no arm should lie at. And three men in grey were walking towards him through the smoke with their rifles up.

Then the light came off the west ridge.

Aldric felt it before he saw it. A pressure, a great slow wave rolling out across the valley from Sera's position like the ripple from a stone thrown into a pond — except that the stone was the size of a church. His wall rang with it like a struck bell. Every hair on his body stood up. Across the valley, on the far ridge, there was a light among the rocks, blue-white, as bright as a magnesium flare. Down below, the three men walking towards Kael were swept away like chaff off a threshing floor. The smoke blew back in a ring. A burning lorry leapt sideways.

Then it was gone, and the valley was ringing in a way he could feel in his teeth, and Kael lay alone in the middle of a bare circle, not moving.

*What has she done*, Aldric thought, *what has she done to herself* — and then he saw the researchers coming out from behind their cars, gathering, pointing, and he understood with total clarity what was going to happen next. They would reach Kael before anyone else did. They would find him alive, or not alive, and either way they would *have* him.

*Hold*, said the voice. It was very reasonable. It was the voice of his teachers and his textbooks and fifteen years of discipline. *The wall is the job. The wall is the whole plan. Drop it, and the far bank opens, and the convoy can move, and everything they did today was for nothing. The rational act is to hold.*

He let go.

It was not a decision. He would examine it later, the way he examined everything, and he would find no moment in which he had weighed one thing against another and chosen. There was the voice saying *hold*, and then there was his hands lifting off the stone. That was all. Something underneath the scholar had simply stood up and walked out of the room.

Across the valley a voice screamed *GO*, and he was already gone.

---

The wall did not fall. It unthreaded.

He felt it come apart — the frame first, then the shape, the anchors pulling loose one by one from the far bank like nails drawn from wet wood. And all the Aether he had been holding in it for three minutes came rushing back up the lines towards him, unshaped, unchannelled, like water from a burst dam finding the lowest ground. It went through him. It went through his hands and his arms and his chest and out again. He staggered. The world tipped. Colours came unstuck from the things they belonged to and floated a quarter-inch to the left. Something deep in his casting sense rang, high and clear and wrong, like a wineglass flicked with a fingernail, and kept ringing.

He would still hear it eight days later. He could not stop to hear it now.

He went straight down the hill.

Not the deer trail. There was no time for the deer trail. He went over the lip of the rocks and down through the bracken, the slope so steep that he was half running and half falling, scree sliding out from under his boots. He fell once and rolled and got up. He fell again and lost his spectacles, and found them, by some miracle, hanging from a fern, and jammed them back on with one lens cracked. His satchel banged against his hip. He was dimly aware that someone was shooting, somewhere. He was dimly aware that he did not care.

He came out of the trees at the bottom at a dead run and crossed the gravel.

---

Kael was very pale.

That was the first thing. Aldric had seen a great many people who were hurt, and pallor was the first thing he always looked for, and Kael's face — weathered, sun-browned, eighteen years of beats in the weather written on it — had gone the colour of wet ash. His lips were blue at the edges. His eyes were closed. He was breathing, but shallowly, quickly, in little catching gasps, and every third or fourth breath there was a wet sound under it that Aldric did not like at all.

He knelt in the gravel. It was soaked. It took him a moment to understand with what.

*Work.*

He opened Kael's jacket with fingers that would not stop shaking. Low on the right side, just above the hip, there was a hole in his shirt, and under it a hole in him, small and neat and dark. He slid a hand under Kael's back. There was another, on the other side, larger and ragged. *Through and through.* Good. Or bad. He did not know yet. The left forearm was torn open from wrist to elbow — shrapnel, he thought — and bleeding steadily but not fast. The arm itself was broken above the elbow; he could see the shape of it through the sleeve. And when he laid his palm very lightly on the right side of Kael's chest, he felt, under his hand, a soft wrongness where there should have been the spring of ribs. Two broken. Three. Pushed in. The wet sound in his breathing was coming from there.

He made himself think. It was the only thing he could give Kael that nobody else could.

A bullet low in the body, front to back. Placement unknown. If it had found one of the great vessels, Kael would be dead already; so it had not. If it had found the liver, or a kidney, there was a quarter of an hour, perhaps less, before the bleeding inside him became the kind no one came back from. If it had found only muscle and gut — longer. Hours. But the ribs had gone into the lung, and the lung was filling. And he had lost so much blood on this gravel. So much.

*Assume the worst*, said his teachers. *Always, in the field, assume the worst.*

Fifteen minutes. Perhaps.

He laid both hands on Kael's side, over the wound, and began to build.

---

It was nothing like the tunnels.

In the tunnels, he had built his workings the way his teachers had taught him, a piece at a time, each piece tested before the next was laid. Here there was no time to test. He threw up the frame in a single motion — the shape of a mending, the shape he knew best of all the shapes he knew, the first one he had ever learned. He pulled it tight around the wound. He opened it.

And the Aether came in like a tide.

It was the aftermath of Sera's surge. He understood that at once. The valley was still ringing with it, the whole air thick and heavy and charged, and when he opened the frame the Aether did not trickle in. It *poured*. It pressed against the edges of his working from every side. It wanted in. It offered itself — more than he'd asked for, far more, a vast warm flood of power that could have filled the frame and broken it and filled the space beyond it and gone on filling. He could feel what it would do. It would close everything. The torn vessels, the punctured lung, the broken ribs, the lost blood — he could feel that if he let it in, all of it, it would seal Kael up whole in one great rush and he would sit up and open his eyes and ask what all the fuss was about.

And it would empty Aldric to the bottom. To the bottom and past it. And then if the wound reopened on the carry, or the bleeding started again in the night, or a fever came, there would be nothing left in him to give. Not a drop. Not for days.

*No.*

He held the boundary.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done. Harder than the wall. He held the frame at the size he could afford — the size he could afford *twice* — and let in exactly what it was built to take, and not one drop more, while the flood pressed against the edges and begged. He could feel it straining. He could feel himself straining. Sweat ran into his eyes. His cracked lens fogged. He held.

The working went in.

It went in under his palms, warm, a slow heat sinking down into Kael's side. He felt it find the torn places. He felt it — he would not write this part in his notebook, later, because there were no words for it in any notebook — he felt it *touch* them, from the inside, and begin to draw them together. Not close. Not heal. Gather. The way a stitch gathers. The bleeding inside slowed. Slowed further. Did not stop. The great tide of shock that was carrying Kael out to sea slackened, a little, and held.

It was not enough to save him. It was enough to keep him for a while.

When he took his hands away, the wound was still there, and still bleeding, though slowly now. Kael's lips were still blue. But his breathing had steadied, and the wet sound had eased, and his pulse under Aldric's fingers was faster and thinner than any pulse should be — but it was there.

Aldric sat back on his heels in the bloody gravel.

He was nearly empty. He could feel the bottom of himself. Perhaps one more thing. One small thing.

He looked around. The convoy was burning — four of the eight vehicles now, the fire jumping from one to the next, black smoke rolling down the valley towards the ford on the wind. The far bank was open. Through the smoke he could see men on it, a few, running north up the switchback away from the fire. Nobody was coming this way. Not yet.

They would have to move him. Up the hill, along the trails, three miles. Every jolt would tear at what he had just gathered.

He laid one hand flat on Kael's chest and, with the very last of what he had, raised a ward around him — not a wall this time but a skin, thin as a soap bubble, wrapped close around Kael's ribs and side to hold them still. It went on like a cold breath. It would not stop a bullet. It would stop a jolt. It would keep the broken bones from moving and the gathered places from tearing open on the carry. It was a small, quiet, ordinary working, the kind he had cast a hundred times in the practice rooms at the Scholars.

When it was done, there was nothing left at all.

He knew the feeling now. He had watched it on Sera's face in the tunnels and the next day at the flat — the hollowed look, the stillness. The ringing in the ears. The world seen down a long tube. He had wondered what it felt like from the inside. It felt like being made of paper.

---

Kael's eyes opened.

Not far. Just a slit. They wandered, unfocused, and found the sky, and then found Aldric's face, and stayed there.

"Aldric," he said. It was barely a sound.

"I'm here." Aldric heard his own voice come out perfectly level. He did not know where it came from. His hands, he noticed, were shaking so hard he had to press them flat on his knees. "I'm here. We're getting you out. Stay with me, Kael."

Kael's mouth moved. Nothing came out. His eyes stayed on Aldric's face a moment longer, as if they were trying to say something his mouth couldn't, and then they closed.

His chest went on rising and falling. Shallow. Steady.

Aldric stayed where he was, crouched in the gravel with his palm on Kael's chest, feeling it rise and fall. He did not trust himself to move. He did not want to. As long as he could feel it, it was still happening.

---

Mira found him like that.

She had come down the west ridge and across the river by the deer stones above the ford, half carrying Sera, who was walking on her own feet in the way that drunk men walk on theirs. Sera's eyes were still glowing faintly — not the blaze he'd seen from across the valley, but a cold steady shine, like light coming through ice. Her face was the colour of paper. There was blood dried black under her nose and on her chin.

"How bad?" Mira said.

"Through and through, low on the right. Broken arm. Ribs, and the lung under them." Aldric heard himself reciting it as if from a case file. "I've stopped most of the bleeding. Most. I've held him. That's all I've done. He's at the edge, Mira. He needs shelter and he needs me, and I need time. Real time. Hours. Not —" he looked around at the smoke, the burning lorries, the bodies in grey on the gravel — "not this."

Mira looked at Kael for a long moment. Her face did not change at all. Then she looked up at the hill, and Aldric watched her start planning, the way you might watch a clock start ticking again after someone had wound it.

"Stretcher," she said.

They made one from Kael's rifle and a pole from the wreck of the timber and Aldric's coat and Mira's, buttoned round them both. It took four minutes. Aldric counted them. They rolled Kael onto it, gently, so gently, while Aldric held his head, and Kael made a sound in his throat but did not wake.

"Sera," Mira said. "You can't carry —"

"I'm carrying." Sera had already taken hold of one of the poles. Her hands were shaking. Her knuckles were white on the wood. She did not look at Mira. "I'm carrying him."

Mira opened her mouth, and closed it, and took the other pole at the front.

They lifted. Three people at the corners of a stretcher meant for four. They carried Kael across the gravel and into the trees and up the deer trail, the same thread of path he had come running down ten minutes and a lifetime ago, and the slope was steep and the trail was narrow and every step was a negotiation between the ground and their legs and the weight. They stopped to rest twice in the first hundred yards. Nobody spoke. At the top, at the cairn, they turned south-east onto the Sootborn trail and went into the deep woods.

Behind them, the valley burned.

Aldric looked back once, where the trail crested the ridge. He could see it all from there: the ford, and the lorry stalled in it with the water piling white against its wheels. The far bank, bare now, ordinary, the switchback climbing up through the trees with nothing standing across it. The timber heaped across the road behind. The convoy between them — eight vehicles, five burning, the black smoke going up in a great slow pillar into the blue morning and leaning east on the wind.

The road was blocked at both ends. The machines that were meant to break open the mill were twisted metal on a riverbank. Whatever the Collective had wanted to do in the Wilds this summer, it was not going to do it.

The job was done.

He looked down at Kael's grey face on the stretcher between his hands, and at the dark stain spreading slowly through the coats beneath him, and turned, and went on into the trees.
