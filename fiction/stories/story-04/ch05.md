# Chapter 5

The literature said a sustained working was like carrying a weight uphill. The literature was wrong. Aldric had held the wall for three minutes, and it was nothing like carrying. It was like being slowly emptied through a hole in the bottom of himself while he kept the hole open with his own hands.

He knelt in his nest of rocks with both palms pressed flat to the stone, as though steadying himself would steady the working, and looked across the valley at what he had made.

It stood across the road where it climbed out of the ford, a little higher than a house and a little wider than the road. In the morning sun it was almost invisible, a faint distortion like the air above a stove. But he could feel every inch of it. He had built it in one breath, out of nothing, the instant the rifle cracked. He had thrown the frame across the valley, driven the anchors into the stone of the far bank and pulled the shape taut between them. Then he had let the Aether in, measured and bounded and *held*. It was the fastest construction of his life. It was also, he thought with a detached and slightly hysterical pride, rather good.

Every second since, it had been drinking him.

He could feel his reserves as he felt his own pulse: not a number but a pressure, a fullness, and the fullness was going down. He was a little under half now, less than he'd guessed. In the first minute the outrider had tried to drive his motorcycle through the wall, and the wall had thrown him back into the river. The shock went through the working, up the anchors and into Aldric's hands with a jolt. A man with a rifle had emptied a magazine into the wall at point-blank range. He'd felt every round.

But it held. The convoy was boxed. Kael was on the valley floor.

Before the engines came, Aldric had decided he would not look at Kael. Looking would make him want to do something, and his only job was to hold. He looked at the wall, the switchback, the anchors. He breathed. He held.

He looked anyway.

---

He had the field glasses up when the gun failed.

At first he didn't know what he was seeing. Kael at the back of the gun transport, turning. A grey-coated man at the front of it. Kael's arm came up, fast, so fast, and then simply stopped, as though a film had caught in the projector. Aldric went cold from the scalp down.

Then the grey man fired, and Kael fell.

Aldric didn't hear the shot; the valley was full of shots. He saw Kael go back against the wheel and down out of sight. Through the glasses he saw how he went down: all at once, legs first, like a building with a supporting wall knocked out. He knew what that meant. He had spent fifteen years studying the body and a year patching it up in the neighbourhood clinics. When a man fell like that, something deep had been hit, something that held him up.

*Hold*, said the part of him that had built the wall. *Hold. That is the job.*

He held.

He watched Kael crawl, and the dark trail on the gravel behind him. Without meaning to, he counted the seconds until the fuses must go.

Then the big lorry went up, and the world went flat.

He had never felt anything like it. To the part of him holding the wall, it wasn't an explosion. It was an absence. The dead-zone engine on the lorry's back tore itself apart, and in dying it did, for one instant, everything it had been built to do. The Aether in the valley simply *wasn't there*. The air went grey and empty and dead. Across the valley his wall shuddered from end to end like a sail with the wind gone out of it. The anchors slipped. The frame began to fold.

He caught it.

He didn't know how, and later, when he tried to write it down, he failed. Something in him reached across a quarter-mile of dead air, took the folding working in both hands and *held it up*. There was no Aether in it at all, only the frame and his own stubbornness, and he held it through the long, long instant until the Aether seeped back. It cost him more than the whole three minutes before. He felt it go out of him like blood. When the wall stood shimmering again he was at less than a quarter. His hands were shaking against the stone, and his mouth tasted of old pennies.

Then the second lorry went up, and through the smoke he saw Kael.

He lay on his back against a spur of grey rock at the foot of this very ridge, perhaps a hundred and fifty yards below. He had been thrown, and he wasn't moving. His arm lay at an angle no arm should. Three men in grey were walking towards him through the smoke with their rifles up.

Then the light came off the west ridge.

Aldric felt it before he saw it: a pressure, a great slow wave rolling across the valley from Sera's position, the ripple from a stone the size of a church. His wall rang with it. Every hair on his body stood up. On the far ridge a blue-white light blazed among the rocks, bright as a magnesium flare. Below, the three men walking towards Kael were swept away like chaff off a threshing floor. The smoke blew back in a ring. A burning lorry leapt sideways.

Then it was gone. The valley rang in a way he could feel in his teeth, and Kael lay alone in the middle of a bare circle, not moving.

*What has she done*, Aldric thought, *what has she done to herself.* Then he saw the researchers coming out from behind their cars, gathering and pointing, and he understood exactly what would happen next. They would reach Kael before anyone else. They would find him alive or not alive, and either way they would *have* him.

*Hold*, said the voice. It was very reasonable. It was the voice of his teachers and his textbooks and fifteen years of discipline. *The wall is the job. The wall is the whole plan. Drop it and the far bank opens, the convoy can move, and everything they did today was for nothing. The rational act is to hold.*

He let go.

It wasn't a decision. Later he would examine it as he examined everything, and find no moment where he had weighed one thing against another and chosen. There was the voice saying *hold*, and then his hands were lifting off the stone. Something underneath the scholar had stood up and walked out of the room.

Across the valley a voice screamed *GO*, and he was already gone.

---

The wall didn't fall. It unthreaded.

He felt it come apart: the frame first, then the shape, then the anchors pulling loose from the far bank one by one like nails drawn from wet wood. All the Aether he had held in it for three minutes came rushing back up the lines towards him, unshaped and unchannelled, water from a burst dam looking for the lowest ground. It went through his hands, his arms, his chest and out again. He staggered. The world tipped. Colours came unstuck from their things and floated a quarter-inch to the left. Something deep in his casting sense rang, high and clear and wrong, a wineglass flicked with a fingernail, and kept ringing.

He would still hear it eight days later. He couldn't stop to hear it now.

He went straight down the hill.

There was no time for the deer trail. He went over the lip of the rocks and down through the bracken, half running and half falling on a slope so steep the scree slid out from under his boots. Once he fell, rolled and got up. The second time he lost his spectacles. By some miracle he found them hanging from a fern, and jammed them back on with one lens cracked. His satchel banged against his hip. Someone was shooting somewhere. He was dimly aware that he didn't care.

He came out of the trees at a dead run and crossed the gravel.

---

Kael was very pale.

Aldric had seen a great many hurt people, and pallor was always the first thing he looked for. Kael's face, weathered and sun-browned by eighteen years of beats, had gone the colour of wet ash. His lips were blue at the edges and his eyes were closed. He was breathing in quick, shallow, catching gasps. Every third or fourth breath carried a wet sound that Aldric did not like at all.

He knelt in the gravel. It was soaked. It took him a moment to understand with what.

*Work.*

He opened Kael's jacket with fingers that wouldn't stop shaking. Low on the right side, just above the hip, there was a small, neat, dark hole in his shirt and in him. Aldric slid a hand under his back and found the other, larger and ragged. *Through and through.* Good, or bad; he didn't know yet. The left forearm was torn open from wrist to elbow, by shrapnel he thought, bleeding steadily but not fast. The arm itself was broken above the elbow; he could see its shape through the sleeve. When he laid his palm very lightly on the left side of Kael's chest, he felt a soft wrongness where the ribs should spring. Two broken. Three. Pushed in. The wet sound in his breathing came from there.

He made himself think. It was the one thing he could give Kael that nobody else could.

A bullet low in the body, front to back, placement unknown. If it had found one of the great vessels Kael would be dead already, so it hadn't. If it had found the liver or a kidney, there was a quarter of an hour, perhaps less, before the bleeding inside him became the kind nobody came back from. If it had found only muscle and gut, there were hours. But the ribs had gone into the lung, and the lung was filling. And he had lost so much blood on this gravel.

*Assume the worst*, said his teachers. *In the field, always assume the worst.*

Fifteen minutes, perhaps.

He laid both hands on Kael's side, over the wound, and began to build.

---

It was nothing like the tunnels.

There he had built his workings as his teachers taught him, a piece at a time, testing each piece before laying the next. Here there was no time to test. He threw up the frame in a single motion. It was a mending, the shape he knew best, the first he had ever learned. He pulled it tight around the wound and opened it.

The Aether came in like a tide.

He understood at once that it was the aftermath of Sera's surge. The valley still rang with it, the air thick and heavy and charged. When he opened the frame the Aether didn't trickle in. It *poured*, and pressed against the edges of his working from every side, wanting in. It offered far more than he'd asked for, a vast warm flood that could have filled the frame, broken it, and gone on filling the space beyond. He could feel what it would do. It would close everything: the torn vessels, the punctured lung, the broken ribs, the lost blood. If he let it all in, it would seal Kael up whole in one great rush. Kael would sit up, open his eyes and ask what all the fuss was about.

It would also empty Aldric to the bottom and past it. If the wound reopened on the carry, or the bleeding started again in the night, or a fever came, he would have nothing left to give. Not a drop, not for days.

*No.*

He held the boundary.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the wall. He held the frame at the size he could afford, the size he could afford *twice*. He let in exactly what it was built to take and not one drop more, while the flood pressed against the edges and begged. The flood strained, and so did he. Sweat ran into his eyes and fogged his cracked lens. He held.

The working went in.

It sank warm under his palms, a slow heat going down into Kael's side. He felt it find the torn places. He felt it *touch* them from the inside, though he would leave that out of his notebook later, having no words for it. It began to draw them together. Rather than close or heal them, it gathered them, as a stitch gathers. The bleeding inside slowed, slowed further, and didn't stop. The great tide of shock carrying Kael out to sea slackened a little and held.

It wasn't enough to save him. It was enough to keep him for a while.

When Aldric took his hands away the wound was still there, still bleeding, though slowly now. Kael's lips were still blue. But his breathing had steadied and the wet sound had eased. Under Aldric's fingers his pulse was faster and thinner than any pulse should be, but it was there.

Aldric sat back on his heels in the bloody gravel.

He was nearly empty. He could feel the bottom of himself. One more thing, perhaps. One small thing.

He looked around. Four of the eight vehicles were burning now, the fire jumping from one to the next, black smoke rolling down the valley towards the ford on the wind. The far bank was open. Through the smoke he could see a few men on it running north up the switchback, away from the fire. Nobody was coming this way yet.

They would have to move him up the hill and along the trails, three miles. Every jolt would tear at what he had just gathered.

He laid one hand flat on Kael's chest and, with the very last of what he had, raised a ward around him. This time it wasn't a wall but a skin, thin as a soap bubble, wrapped close around Kael's ribs and side to hold them still. It went on like a cold breath. A bullet would go straight through it, but a jolt wouldn't. It would keep the broken bones from moving and the gathered places from tearing open on the carry. It was a small, quiet, ordinary working, the kind he had cast a hundred times in the practice rooms at the Scholars.

When it was done, there was nothing left at all.

He knew the feeling now. Sera's face had shown it in the tunnels and the next day at the flat: the hollowed look, the stillness, the ringing in the ears, the world seen down a long tube. He had wondered what it felt like from the inside. It felt like being made of paper.

---

Kael's eyes opened a slit. They wandered, unfocused, found the sky, then found Aldric's face and stayed there.

"Aldric," he said. It was barely a sound.

"I'm here." Aldric heard his own voice come out perfectly level, and didn't know where it came from. His hands were shaking so hard he had to press them flat on his knees. "I'm here. We're getting you out. Stay with me, Kael."

Kael's mouth moved, but nothing came out. His eyes held Aldric's face a moment longer, trying to say what his mouth couldn't. Then they closed.

His chest went on rising and falling, shallow and steady.

Aldric stayed crouched in the gravel with his palm on Kael's chest, feeling it rise and fall. He didn't trust himself to move, and didn't want to. As long as he could feel it, it was still happening.

---

Mira found him like that.

She had come down the west ridge and across the river by the deer stones above the ford, half carrying Sera. Sera was walking on her own feet the way drunk men walk on theirs. Her eyes still glowed faintly, no longer the blaze he'd seen across the valley but a cold steady shine like light through ice. Her face was the colour of paper. Blood had dried black under her nose and on her chin.

"How bad?" Mira said.

"Through and through, low on the right. Broken arm. Ribs, and the lung under them." Aldric heard himself reciting it. "I've stopped most of the bleeding. Most. I've held him. That's all. He's at the edge, Mira. He needs shelter and he needs me, and I need time. Real time. Hours. Not..." He looked around at the smoke, the burning lorries, the bodies in grey on the gravel. "Not this."

Mira looked at Kael a long time without any change in her face. Then she looked up at the hill. Aldric watched her start planning again.

"Stretcher," she said.

They made one from Kael's rifle, a pole from the wreck of the timber, and Aldric's coat and Mira's buttoned round both. It took four minutes; Aldric counted them. They rolled Kael onto it very gently while Aldric held his head. Kael made a sound in his throat but didn't wake.

"Sera," Mira said. "You can't carry..."

"I'm carrying." Sera had already taken hold of one of the poles. Her hands were shaking and her knuckles were white on the wood. She didn't look at Mira. "I'm carrying him."

Mira opened her mouth, closed it, and took the other pole at the front.

They lifted, three people at the corners of a stretcher meant for four. They carried Kael across the gravel, into the trees and up the deer trail he had come running down ten minutes and a lifetime ago. The slope was steep and the trail narrow. Every step was a negotiation between the ground, their legs and the weight. They stopped to rest twice in the first hundred yards. Nobody spoke. At the cairn on top they turned south-east onto the Sootborn trail and went into the deep woods.

Behind them, the valley burned.

Where the trail crested the ridge, Aldric looked back once. He could see all of it from there. The lorry was still stalled in the ford with the water piling white against its wheels. The far bank was bare now and ordinary, the switchback climbing through the trees with nothing across it. Behind the convoy the timber lay heaped across the road. Between them were the eight vehicles, five of them burning, the black smoke going up in a great slow pillar into the blue morning and leaning east on the wind.

The road was blocked at both ends. The machines meant to break open the mill were twisted metal on a riverbank. Whatever the Collective had wanted to do in the Wilds this summer, it was not going to do it.

The job was done.

He looked down at Kael's grey face on the stretcher between his hands, and at the dark stain spreading slowly through the coats beneath him. Then he turned and went on into the trees.
