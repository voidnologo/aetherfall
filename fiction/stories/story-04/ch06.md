# Chapter 6

*Moving.*

That was the first thing. He was moving and he wasn't doing it. Something under him swayed, side to side, a slow rocking like a boat on a canal. Hands. He could feel hands, through the cloth under his back — not on him, but on the things that were carrying him. He knew the hands. He couldn't have said how. The left-hand front one was Mira. The back ones were Aldric and somebody smaller.

The smell of smoke was in everything. In the cloth. In his own hair. In the breath of the people carrying him.

He tried to say *put me down, I can walk*. Nothing happened. It occurred to him, distantly, that this was because he couldn't.

---

*Sky.*

Leaves against it. Moving past, slowly, overhead, green and gold and dark. He watched them go. Oak. Ash. Oak again. A beech with the light coming through it so bright it hurt. More oak. He was scouting. He was cataloguing the canopy, from habit, the way he'd catalogued doorways and windows and the hands of men in taverns for twenty-three years. Good cover here. Bad sightlines. Someone could come down that slope and you'd never see them till they were on you.

*Stop it*, he told himself. *You're not working.*

He kept counting trees anyway. It was something to hold on to.

---

*Sera's face.*

Very close. Upside down. She must have been walking at his head. Her hair hung down on either side of her face like a black curtain, jagged where she'd cut it herself with the kitchen scissors, and her eyes were wrong. They were glowing. Not much. A pale, cold, steady light, like frost with the moon behind it. There was dried blood under her nose.

"Stay with us," she was saying. "Kael. Stay with us. Please."

He wanted to tell her that her eyes were doing the thing again. He wanted to tell her to wipe her nose. He wanted, more than he had wanted anything for a long time, to say *I'm all right, kid*. His mouth wouldn't do it.

---

*Pain.*

It came and went. When it came it was very large and very far away, like weather over the hills, a storm he could see coming across a valley but that belonged to some other place. Then it would arrive, and it was his, and it was everywhere, and it went on and on — and then it would roll off again and leave him lying in the quiet after it, washed out, too tired to be afraid.

There was something wrapped round his chest. He couldn't see it. He could feel it, a cool close pressure like a hand holding his ribs still. When the stretcher jolted on a root, the pain in his side flared, but his ribs didn't move. He thought that was probably Aldric's doing. He thought he should say thank you. He thought he'd say it later.

---

*Water at his lips.*

Cold. It ran down his chin. He couldn't swallow. It hurt too much to swallow and his throat had forgotten how.

"Small sips." Aldric's voice. Close. Hoarse. "Come on. Try, Kael. Just a little. Try."

He tried. Some of it went down. Most of it didn't.

"Good. That's good. Again."

---

There was a man he'd shot, once, the day after his twenty-first birthday. He'd been a Greycoat for two years. The man had a knife and a sister he was trying to cut, in a kitchen in the Low Docks, and Kael had told him three times to drop it, and the third time the man had turned round with the knife up and Kael had shot him in the shoulder. He hadn't died. Kael had gone to see him in the infirmary afterwards, and the man had spat at him. That had been fine. He'd deserved worse. Kael had gone home and been sick in the sink, and his landlady had heard and not said anything, and in the morning there'd been a cup of tea outside his door.

He didn't know why he was thinking about that. It had been a long time.

He thought about his father, who'd laid track for the Northern line until the Wild took it — a whole line, sixty miles of steel and sleepers, just gone under the trees in a single season — and had come home after a quieter man. His father had taught him to clean a rifle at the kitchen table on Sunday nights, the parts laid out in a row on a towel in the order they went back together. *Same order every time*, his father used to say. *Then your hands know it when your head's not there.*

His hands knew it. His head wasn't there.

He thought about a girl called Teodora, with freckles and a gap in her teeth, who'd sold flowers on the corner of Ludd Street the summer he was nineteen. He'd bought a bunch of violets every day for a month and never once worked out what to do with them. He wondered where she was. He hoped she had a good life. He hoped somebody had bought her flowers who knew what to do with them.

He thought about Mira. Mira at a corner table in the Wet Ember, last spring, with her hands round a glass she wasn't drinking. *I'm putting together a Society. A charter, Kael. Our own. I need somebody I trust. I need you.* He'd been out of the grey coat a year by then, drinking slowly in a rented room, and she'd known it, and she hadn't said anything about it. She'd just asked.

*Yes*, he'd said. It was the easiest word he'd ever said in his life.

He thought: *I don't want to die here.*

And then, slower, from somewhere further down: *If I have to, I'd rather it was here. With them. Than anywhere else without them.*

He didn't say any of it. He didn't need to. There was nobody to say it to who didn't know.

---

*Stopping.*

Voices. Horses — he could smell them, warm and dusty and alive, and hear them shifting their feet. A clearing. Someone said a word he didn't know, in an accent from the Wild edge, and someone else answered.

He was being lifted. He was being put down on something hard that smelled of straw and sacking. A cart. The Covenant's cart, from the settlement. They must have brought it.

"How far?" Mira's voice, somewhere above and to the left. Very calm. Too calm.

"Eight miles." A man's voice, flat and Sootborn. "By the back trails. We wait for dark. They'll have people on the road by now."

"He might not have eight miles."

A pause. "Then we'd best go careful."

---

*Jolting.*

The cart had no springs. Every stone on the trail came up through the wood and the straw and into his back like a fist. The cool hand round his ribs held, but everything else moved. Each bump found the hole in his side and opened it a little. Each bump, the storm came over the hills.

Somebody was holding his hand. The good one. The right one. He thought it was Sera. He held on.

---

*Aldric, at dusk.*

His face very close, lit from below by a little hand-lamp somebody had lent him, so that the shadows went up instead of down and he looked like a schoolboy telling a ghost story. He was holding Kael's eyelid open with his thumb and shining the light into it. Then the other. He put two fingers on Kael's neck and held them there and his lips moved, counting.

His spectacles were cracked. One lens was a spiderweb. Kael wondered when that had happened. He hadn't noticed.

"Still here," Aldric said. Not to Kael. To himself, Kael thought. "Still here. Good man. We're close now. Stay with me."

He checked again an hour later. And an hour after that. Every time Kael surfaced, there was Aldric's face, and the little lamp, and the fingers on his neck. Every time, *still here*.

---

*Firelight.*

The cart had stopped. There were voices, a lot of them, low. A dog barking. He was lifted again, and this time it hurt so badly that the world went white and then dark, and when it came back he was on his back on a bed, a real bed, with a rough wool blanket under him and a fire burning in a stone hearth that he could see from the corner of his eye. The ceiling was planks. There was a smell of woodsmoke and boiled onions and lye soap.

The settlement. They'd made it.

Aldric's face again. Different now. He'd washed it, or someone had made him, and he'd taken off his spectacles, and without them his eyes looked very naked and very tired. The skin under them was purple. There was a tremor in his jaw.

"Kael." He waited until Kael's eyes found him. "Kael, listen. I'm going to do what I can now. I've rested a little. I've got a little back. It's going to go deep this time, and it's going to hurt. I'm sorry."

Kael couldn't answer. He moved his fingers on the blanket. Aldric saw it, and nodded, as if it had been a whole sentence.

He put his hands on Kael's side.

The first one had been warm. This was hot. It went in under Aldric's palms and down, and down, and kept going, deeper than Kael had known there was to go — into places he had never felt before and had never wanted to. He felt it find the hole. He felt it find the other hole, at the back. He felt it slide in between them, through him, along the line the bullet had taken, and *pull*. It wasn't pain. It wasn't not pain. It was like his body was having an argument with itself, loud, all at once, every part shouting — and something patient and stubborn sat in the middle of the shouting and said, *no, like this*, over and over, until the shouting stopped.

His ribs moved. He felt them go back. It was like a door closing.

The heat ran out of Aldric's hands and he took them away and sat down hard on a stool beside the bed, as if somebody had cut his strings.

Kael lay and breathed. In. Out. In.

It didn't bubble.

He was still weak. Weaker than he'd ever been. His side still hurt with a deep, dull, steady ache like a bad tooth, and his arm was splinted and strapped to his chest and throbbing, and he felt as though he'd been emptied out and refilled with sand. But he could breathe. He could feel his own heart beating, slow and solid and his.

He opened his eyes properly for the first time since the valley.

Aldric was sitting on the stool with his elbows on his knees and his head down. Sweat was running off the end of his nose. His hands were hanging between his knees, and they were shaking — not trembling, *shaking*, the way hands shake in the cold.

"Aldric," Kael said. It came out as a rasp, a sound like a boot on gravel.

Aldric looked up.

"Thank you," Kael said.

Aldric blinked at him. For a moment he didn't seem to understand. Then something happened at the corner of his mouth — small, contained, the smile of a man who didn't do it often and wasn't sure he was doing it right.

"Anytime," he said.

Kael breathed for a while. Then: "Next time. Your turn to get shot. I'll fix you."

Aldric laughed. It was short and dry and so tired it was almost a cough. "Deal," he said.

---

There were people in the doorway.

Mira, first. She was leaning on the frame with her arms folded. She'd washed her face too, but she hadn't changed her clothes, and there was blood on her sleeves that wasn't hers. Her face was perfectly composed. Behind her and a little to one side was Sera, with a blanket round her shoulders like a cloak, holding on to the doorframe as if it were the only thing keeping her up. Her eyes had stopped glowing. They were just eyes again, with the pale ring round them. They were wet.

"Hi," Kael said.

"Hi," said Sera. Her voice wobbled. She didn't wipe her eyes. She just let them be wet.

Mira didn't say anything at all. She looked at him for a long time, across the room, and he looked back, and he saw it all in her face, as clearly as if she'd written it down for him: *I was scared. I am so glad you are here. I am not going to say that I was scared.*

He nodded to her. She nodded back. That was the conversation.

He closed his eyes.

---

He slept for three days.

He knew it was three days afterwards because they told him. At the time, there was only the sleep, deep and dark and warm, and the sounds that came down through it from very far away, like sounds heard from the bottom of a pool. A woman laughing outside the window at something somebody had said. Children running past, shrieking, a game with rules he couldn't follow. The slow creak and rattle of a well crank. A dog. Mira and Aldric in the next room, talking in low voices, a long way off, the words lost but the shape of them steady — two people planning something together. And overhead, soft and light, somebody's footsteps going back and forth across the floor of the loft, back and forth, back and forth, the way you pace when you're waiting for news.

Ordinary sounds. People living their lives. The world going on around him, not caring that he'd nearly left it, not needing him to do anything at all.

It was the best sound he'd ever heard. He slept in it like a stone in a river.
