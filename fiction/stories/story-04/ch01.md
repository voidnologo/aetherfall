# Chapter 1

Thessaly Vane came to the Wet Ember on a Thursday, which was the first thing Mira noticed, because Covenant business happened on Mondays.

The second thing was that she came alone. No courier waiting by the door, no driver idling at the kerb with the engine running and his eyes on the street. Just Thessaly, in a dark wool coat cut for someone who never had to run anywhere, coming in out of the drizzle at a quarter past five with the lamplighters still working their way down Harrier Street behind her. She stood inside the door long enough to find them. Then she crossed the room.

Hesper watched her come from behind the bar. Hesper watched everyone. She had one arm and a towel over the shoulder where the other had been, and she ran the Wet Ember the way a ship's captain ran a ship in weather — by being the fixed point that everything else moved around. Covenant rings did not impress her. Nothing impressed her. She poured Thessaly a glass of the house red without being asked, set it at the end of the bar, and went back to pretending not to listen.

"Ms. Cade." Thessaly sat without waiting to be invited. Her rings caught the firelight, silver on every finger but the thumbs. "Mr. Dunn."

Kael nodded. He had his back to the wall and his chair angled to the door, the way he always did. The revolver was on his hip under the jacket. His hands were on the table, around a beer he'd been nursing for an hour, and he did not move them.

"You read my letter," Mira said.

"The Covenant read your letter." Thessaly glanced at the bar, where Hesper had started on a row of glasses that did not need polishing. She lowered her voice anyway. "It was passed upward. Quickly. Further upward than I've seen a letter go."

Mira kept her face where it was. Inside, she was counting. Twice now. The first time, before the mill, Thessaly had arrived early and in a hurry and pretended to be neither. This time she wasn't pretending. Twice in a season, across the city, in the rain, to a working-class tavern on the gradient where the beer was honest and the clientele was not. Patrons did not travel to Societies. Societies were summoned to patrons, and sat in the good chairs, and were told. When the direction of travel changed, it meant the weight had shifted, and Mira wanted very much to know how far, and which way.

"The Collective's expedition," Thessaly said. "Your source was accurate. We've confirmed the equipment from two other directions. Dampeners, disruptors, three of the portable dead-zone engines — they had to commission those from a works in the south, and the works talks. Ironwork is leading. Two freelance outfits are riding with them as guns." She turned the wine glass by its stem without drinking. "They intend to put a hole in the mill's defences by shutting off the Aether around it. Like stopping a river with a dam."

"Will it work?" Kael asked.

"We don't know." The admission cost her something. Mira watched it cost her. "Nobody knows what's inside that building. But if they're right — if they can suppress the defences long enough to walk in — then whatever is sitting in that chamber goes back to Ashworth in a lead-lined crate. And then it's theirs. To study. To break open. To *use*." Thessaly's mouth tightened. "The Covenant's position is that this outcome is unacceptable."

"Then oppose it," Mira said.

"The Covenant cannot be seen to oppose it." Thessaly said it simply, like a fact of weather. "If we move openly against the Collective, it stops being about the mill and starts being about us. Patrons don't fight each other in the street, Ms. Cade. It's the one rule that keeps this city from burning. The Greycoats would have to take a side. The Circle would have to take a side. Everyone would." She let that sit. "We need the expedition delayed. Not prevented — delayed. Long enough for us to find out what we're dealing with. Weeks. Months, if you can manage it."

There it was. Mira had seen it coming from the moment Thessaly walked in without a driver, but it was still a particular feeling to watch it arrive. *Deniable.* The word wasn't said. It didn't need to be.

"You want us to hit the convoy," Kael said.

"I want the convoy to have an accident on the road to the Wilds." Thessaly finally drank. "A Society with a charter and a public record, working on its own account, with no Covenant seal anywhere near it. Equipment damaged. Contractors scattered. The Collective forced to go back to the works and order everything again." She set the glass down. "No massacre. That's important. A body count brings the Greycoats out past the city line, and a body count of Collective employees brings questions in the Assembly. Break the machines. Frighten the men. Come home."

Mira looked at Kael. Kael looked at his beer.

"The Covenant will pay," Thessaly said. "Well. And we'll supply what you need. A cart and horses. Communications. Charges — agricultural blasting powder, the kind the Sootborn buy from a supplier in Millbridge to clear stumps. If it's ever traced, it traces to a field." A pause. "And you won't be alone out there. Our people have spoken to the settlement at the Wild edge. The woman called Pell."

Something moved in Kael's face. Mira saw it, small and quick: the memory of a cold welcome and a colder road.

"She wants the convoy stopped," Thessaly went on. "The road the Collective needs runs through her people's country. They went out there to get away from the city. They don't want a column of lorries and hired guns rolling past their children. They won't fight. They've made that very clear. But they'll give you the ground. Trails, maps, a place to sleep." She folded her hands. "They're not your allies. I'd like that understood. They're people who want the same thing you do, for a different reason."

"I understand the difference," Mira said.

"I thought you might." Thessaly stood. She left a coin on the bar for the wine, which Hesper would ignore until she'd gone. At the door she turned back. "I'll need an answer by tomorrow noon. The convoy leaves the Ashworth yards in six days."

The door opened on rain and closed on firelight. Hesper went on polishing her glass.

"Well," Kael said.

"Well," said Mira.

---

The flat on Harrier Street was three rooms and a kitchen over a cobbler's, with a back window that looked onto an alley and a front window that looked onto the gradient. Mira had chosen it for both. You could leave by the alley. You could watch the street. And from the front window, on the right sort of evening, you could see the exact place where the arc light from the west met the soft copper glow from the east, and the air between them shimmered like heat over a road.

They talked in the kitchen with the lamp lit and the kettle on, which was where they talked about everything that mattered.

Kael spoke first. Kael usually did, when the thing was hard and practical.

"We're not soldiers," he said. "But we're closer to it than most. I've been shot at. I've done crowd work. Mira's run people through the gradient under the Greycoats' noses since she was fourteen." He turned his cup on the table. "If the Covenant's giving us the tools and the Sootborn are giving us the ground — we can do this. And if the Collective gets into that mill, I don't want to be the people who could have stopped it and didn't."

"Aldric?"

Aldric had his spectacles off and was polishing them on the end of his waistcoat, which meant he had already started an answer and was trying to find the end of it. "It's different," he said. "From the tunnels. Down there we chose when to cast. We had time to build. This would be —" He put the spectacles back on. "Fast. Loud. People shooting at us while we work. The costs change. Everything costs more, and you can't stop to think about whether you can afford it." He looked at each of them in turn. "If I do this, I need to hold something back the whole time. For when one of us is hurt. And one of us *will* be hurt. I want everyone to know that before we say yes."

"Noted," Mira said. "Sera?"

Sera was at the window seat with her knees drawn up and her coat around her, though the kitchen was warm. She had been there since they came in. The ring around her irises had settled since the tunnels to a pale, steady blue-white, like frost on a dark window, and in the lamplight it was easy to see.

"I'll go," she said.

Mira waited. Sera did not add anything.

That was the thing that had changed. Before the tunnels, Sera had come into every decision with both hands — arguing, wanting, asking a dozen questions at once. Since she'd touched the thing in the case, she'd been quiet. Not sullen. Not afraid, exactly. Quiet the way a house goes quiet when somebody in it is listening for a sound. Mira had noticed it on the second day. She suspected the others had too. Nobody had named it, and Mira, who named things for a living, found that she did not want to be the first.

"All right," Mira said. "Then I'll tell you how I see it."

She told them. That this job would take them further from cover than anything they'd done. That if it went well, the Covenant would owe them something, and the Covenant paid its debts. That if it went badly, there would be no seal to hide behind and no patron to say *they were ours*. That a deniable Society was, by definition, a Society nobody would come for.

"So we don't let it go badly," Kael said.

"So we don't let it go badly."

She sent the answer to Thessaly at eight the next morning.

---

They spent four days getting ready, and it was the part of the work Mira loved best, though she would never have said so out loud.

Other Societies had better guns, deeper pockets, longer names on their charters. What Ash & Veil had was the habit of preparing as though everything would go wrong. Mira had learned it on the gradient as a girl, running packages for people who paid in coins and threats: you walked the route the day before, and the day before that, and you knew where every door was. Kael had learned it on eighteen years of beats. Aldric had been born with it. Sera had absorbed it from the rest of them the way she absorbed everything — quickly, and without seeming to notice.

Kael took his rifle down from the top of the wardrobe for the first time since spring.

It was a long, plain, bolt-action thing with a worn walnut stock and a telescopic sight he'd bought second-hand from a gamekeeper. He'd carried the revolver on every job since the foundry, because the work had been close — tunnels, tavern rooms, alleys. This would not be close. He stripped the rifle on the kitchen table with the window open for the smell of the oil, and cleaned each piece, and laid them out in a row on a towel in the order they went back together. Then he cleaned the revolver anyway, though he had cleaned it three days ago. Mira watched him hesitate over the cylinder for half a second longer than usual. She thought about the tunnels, and the way the gun had gone slow and strange in his hand, and she didn't say anything.

On the third day he came back from the pawnbrokers on Cinder Lane with a small blackpowder pistol wrapped in a rag. It was ugly and old and had a single barrel and a hammer like a thumbnail.

"Antique," Mira said.

"Simple." He strapped a holster for it above his left ankle. "Nothing in it to go wrong."

Aldric packed his healer's kit the way other men packed for a wedding. Bandages, rolled and pinned. Two kinds of splint. Catgut and needles in a folded oilcloth. Carbolic. Three flasks of boiled water. A bottle of laudanum he looked at for a long time before packing. He checked his notebook was in the satchel with his spellbook, and then he checked again, and then he took out the notebook and put it in his coat instead. "I always write after," he said, when he caught Mira watching. "I expect there'll be a great deal to write after."

Sera tested herself. She didn't call it that, but that was what it was. In the evenings she sat at the window with her compass in her lap and reached, very gently, into the air of the flat — a teaspoon lifted off the table and set down again, a page of the newspaper turned without a hand. Small things. Careful things. Each time, there was a pause before she reached, a breath held and let go, that had not been there a month ago. Each time, she looked at her hands afterwards as if she expected them to have changed.

"How is it?" Mira asked on the last evening.

"Fine." Sera turned the compass over. The needle swung, wavered and found north. "Normal. I think it's normal." She was quiet a moment. "I don't remember what normal felt like, exactly. So I'm not sure."

Mira packed the plan.

She had a folded survey map of the north-east road, bought from a surveyor's office under the name of a timber company that did not exist. A Covenant seal in an oilskin pouch at the bottom of her pack, to be shown to no one unless the alternative was being shot. A schedule written in her own shorthand that nobody else could read. A flare pistol with three cartridges — red for abort. And, last, the charges: twelve squat waxed-paper cylinders of Sootborn stump powder, packed in sawdust in a crate stencilled AGRICULTURAL, delivered to the alley door by a Covenant carter who did not look at her face.

She left the arc pistol behind. It was the best weapon she owned and it would be dead weight past the last village; Galvanic machinery didn't like the Wild edge, and the Wild edge didn't like it back. She took the derringer instead, a two-shot thing that lived in her boot, and the knife that lived in her sleeve. Then she sat down at the desk and did the thing she always did last, which was to imagine each of them dead, one at a time, and ask herself what she would have done differently.

It was a terrible habit. It had kept people alive.

---

They left Ashwick at first light on the fifth day, with the cart, two Covenant horses that did not know them yet and a tarpaulin over the crate that said AGRICULTURAL.

The north-east road went out through the factory suburbs and the market gardens and then into country — real country, hedges and stubble fields and stone farmhouses with smoke going straight up from their chimneys into a sky that had nothing in it. Mira had grown up on the gradient, where every street had an opinion about the world. Out here, nothing argued. The cows didn't care about the Aether. The hedges were full of sparrows. The farm dogs ran out barking at the cart wheels and gave up at their own gates. It was so ordinary it felt like a painted backdrop.

They didn't talk much. Kael drove. Aldric sat beside him with a book open that he did not read. Sera rode in the back on the tarpaulin with her arms around her knees, watching the land go by. Mira walked beside the horses for long stretches to spare them, and to think.

The silence had a quality to it. She'd heard the same silence in rooms where someone was about to say something difficult and hadn't decided how. Four people who knew where they were going and what they meant to do when they got there, and who were each, privately, doing the arithmetic of what it might cost.

They passed the place where, on the mill job, the motorcar had started coughing. They passed the quarantine posts the old government had driven into the verge forty years back, their paint long gone, their wire long stolen. They passed the last village with its field of dead cars — saloons and lorries and a charabanc with grass growing through the seats, all of them left where they'd stopped because engines didn't run past this point and nobody had ever bothered to tow them back.

Horses didn't care about the Aether either. That was why the Covenant had sent horses.

On the evening of the second day the hedges gave way to scrub, and the scrub to the first tall trees, and the light went green and gold through leaves that were very slightly the wrong shape. Sera lifted her head in the back of the cart like someone hearing their name across a crowded room. The air took on a taste — copper, faintly, and something sweeter underneath, like cut grass in a place where no grass had been cut.

Then the road bent, and there was the settlement: the patched roofs and railway-carriage walls, the vegetable plots, the fence of sharpened poles, the smoke. And at the edge of the road, as if she had been standing there all afternoon, a small grey-haired woman with her arms folded and a shawl over her shoulders.

Pell did not smile. Mira had not expected her to. She looked at each of them in turn — Kael on the driving seat, Aldric with his book, Sera in the back, Mira at the horses' heads — the way a farmer looks at weather.

"You came," she said.

"We said we would."

"People say a lot of things." Pell turned and started towards the gate. Over her shoulder, without breaking stride, she said, "Come inside. We have maps."
