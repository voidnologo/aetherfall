# Chapter 1

Thessaly Vane came to the Wet Ember on a Thursday. That was the first thing Mira noticed, because Covenant business happened on Mondays.

The second was that she came alone. There was no courier waiting by the door, no driver idling at the kerb with the engine running and his eyes on the street. Thessaly came in out of the drizzle at a quarter past five in a dark wool coat cut for someone who never had to run anywhere. Behind her the lamplighters were still working their way down Harrier Street. She stood inside the door long enough to find them, then crossed the room.

Hesper watched her come from behind the bar. Hesper watched everyone. She had one arm and kept a towel over the shoulder where the other had been. She ran the Wet Ember like a ship's captain in weather, by being the fixed point everything else moved around. Covenant rings did not impress her. Nothing did. She poured Thessaly a glass of the house red unasked, set it at the end of the bar and went back to pretending not to listen.

"Ms. Cade." Thessaly sat without waiting to be invited. Her rings caught the firelight, silver on every finger but the thumbs. "Mr. Dunn."

Kael nodded. He had his back to the wall and his chair angled to the door. The revolver was on his hip under the jacket. His hands stayed on the table, around a beer he'd been nursing for an hour.

"You read my letter," Mira said.

"The Covenant read your letter." Thessaly glanced at the bar, where Hesper had started on a row of glasses that needed no polishing. She lowered her voice anyway. "It was passed upward. Quickly. Further upward than I've seen a letter go."

Mira kept her face still and counted. The first time, before the mill, Thessaly had arrived early and in a hurry and pretended to be neither. This time she wasn't pretending. Twice in a season she had crossed the city in the rain to a gradient tavern where the beer was honest and the clientele was not. Patrons did not travel to Societies. Societies were summoned to patrons, sat in the good chairs and were told. When the direction of travel changed, the weight had shifted. Mira wanted very much to know how far, and which way.

"The Collective's expedition," Thessaly said. "Your source was accurate. We've confirmed the equipment from two other directions. Dampeners, disruptors, three of the portable dead-zone engines. They had to commission those from a works in the south, and the works talks. Ironwork is leading. Two freelance outfits are riding with them as guns." She turned the wine glass by its stem without drinking. "They intend to put a hole in the mill's defences by shutting off the Aether around it. Like damming a river."

"Will it work?" Kael asked.

"We don't know." The admission cost her something, and Mira watched it cost her. "Nobody knows what's inside that building. If they're right, if they can suppress the defences long enough to walk in, whatever is sitting in that chamber goes back to Ashworth in a lead-lined crate. Then it's theirs to study, to break open, to *use*." Her mouth tightened. "The Covenant's position is that this outcome is unacceptable."

"Then oppose it," Mira said.

"The Covenant cannot be seen to oppose it." She said it as plainly as a weather report. "If we move openly against the Collective, it stops being about the mill and starts being about us. Patrons don't fight each other in the street, Ms. Cade. It's the one rule that keeps this city from burning. The Greycoats would have to take a side. So would the Circle. So would everyone." She paused. "We need the expedition delayed. Delayed, not prevented, long enough for us to find out what we're dealing with. Weeks. Months, if you can manage it."

There it was. Mira had seen it coming from the moment Thessaly walked in without a driver, but it was still a particular feeling to watch it arrive. Nobody said *deniable*. Nobody had to.

"You want us to hit the convoy," Kael said.

"I want the convoy to have an accident on the road to the Wilds." Thessaly finally drank. "A Society with a charter and a public record, working on its own account, with no Covenant seal anywhere near it. Equipment damaged. Contractors scattered. The Collective forced to go back to the works and order everything again." She set the glass down. "No massacre. That's important. A body count brings the Greycoats out past the city line, and a body count of Collective employees brings questions in the Assembly. Break the machines. Frighten the men. Come home."

Mira looked at Kael. Kael looked at his beer.

"The Covenant will pay," Thessaly said. "Well. And we'll supply what you need: a cart and horses, communications, charges. Agricultural blasting powder, the kind the Sootborn buy from a supplier in Millbridge to clear stumps. If it's ever traced, it traces to a field." She paused again. "And you won't be alone out there. Our people have spoken to the settlement at the Wild edge. The woman called Pell."

Kael's face moved, small and quick. Mira knew he was remembering a cold welcome and a colder road.

"She wants the convoy stopped," Thessaly went on. "The road the Collective needs runs through her people's country. They went out there to get away from the city, and they don't want a column of lorries and hired guns rolling past their children. They won't fight. They've made that very clear. But they'll give you the ground: trails, maps, a place to sleep." She folded her hands. "They're not your allies. I'd like that understood. They want the same thing you do, for a different reason."

"I understand the difference," Mira said.

"I thought you might." Thessaly stood. She left a coin on the bar for the wine, which Hesper would ignore until she'd gone. At the door she turned. "I'll need an answer by tomorrow noon. The convoy leaves the Ashworth yards in six days."

The door opened on rain and closed on firelight. Hesper went on polishing her glass.

"Well," Kael said.

"Well," said Mira.

---

The flat on Harrier Street was three rooms and a kitchen over a cobbler's. Mira had chosen it for its windows: the back one looked onto an alley, the front one onto the gradient. You could leave by the alley. You could watch the street. On the right sort of evening the front window showed the exact place where the arc light from the west met the soft copper glow from the east, and the air between them shimmered like heat over a road.

They talked in the kitchen with the lamp lit and the kettle on. It was where they talked about everything that mattered.

Kael spoke first. When the thing was hard and practical, he usually did.

"We're not soldiers," he said. "But we're closer to it than most. I've been shot at. I've done crowd work. Mira's run people through the gradient under the Greycoats' noses since she was fourteen." He turned his cup on the table. "If the Covenant's giving us the tools and the Sootborn are giving us the ground, we can do this. And if the Collective gets into that mill, I don't want to be the people who could have stopped it and didn't."

"Aldric?"

Aldric had his spectacles off and was polishing them on the end of his waistcoat. That meant he had started an answer and was trying to find its end. "It's different," he said. "From the tunnels. Down there we chose when to cast. We had time to build. This would be..." He put the spectacles back on. "Fast. Loud. People shooting at us while we work. Everything costs more, and you can't stop to think about whether you can afford it." He looked at each of them. "If I do this, I need to hold something back the whole time, for when one of us is hurt. And one of us *will* be hurt. I want everyone to know that before we say yes."

"Noted," Mira said. "Sera?"

Sera sat in the window seat with her knees drawn up and her coat around her, though the kitchen was warm. She had been there since they came in. Since the tunnels, the ring around her irises had settled to a pale, steady blue-white, frost on a dark window. In the lamplight it was easy to see.

"I'll go," she said.

Mira waited. Sera said nothing more.

Before the tunnels, Sera had come into every decision with both hands, arguing and wanting and asking a dozen questions at once. Since she'd touched the thing in the case she'd been quiet. She wasn't sullen, or exactly afraid. It was the quiet of a house where somebody is listening for a sound. Mira had noticed it on the second day and suspected the others had too. Nobody had named it. Mira named things for a living, and she found she didn't want to be the first.

"All right," Mira said. "Then I'll tell you how I see it."

This job would take them further from cover than anything they'd done. If it went well, the Covenant would owe them, and the Covenant paid its debts. If it went badly, there would be no seal to hide behind and no patron to say *they were ours*. A deniable Society was, by definition, one nobody would come for.

"So we don't let it go badly," Kael said.

"So we don't let it go badly."

She sent the answer to Thessaly at eight the next morning.

---

They spent four days getting ready. It was the part of the work Mira loved best, though she would never have said so.

Other Societies had better guns, deeper pockets and longer names on their charters. Ash & Veil had the habit of preparing as though everything would go wrong. Mira had learned it as a girl on the gradient, running packages for people who paid in coins and threats: you walked the route the day before, and the day before that, until you knew where every door was. Kael had learned it on eighteen years of beats. Aldric had been born with it. Sera had picked it up from the rest of them as she picked up everything, quickly and without seeming to notice.

Kael took his rifle down from the top of the wardrobe for the first time since spring.

It was a long, plain bolt-action with a worn walnut stock and a telescopic sight he'd bought second-hand from a gamekeeper. Since the foundry he'd carried the revolver on every job, because the work had been close: tunnels, tavern rooms, alleys. This would not be close. He stripped the rifle on the kitchen table with the window open for the smell of the oil. He cleaned each piece and laid them out on a towel in the order they went back together. Then he cleaned the revolver too, though he'd cleaned it three days ago. Mira watched him hesitate over the cylinder half a second longer than usual. She thought about the tunnels, and how the gun had gone slow and strange in his hand. She didn't say anything.

On the third day he came back from the pawnbrokers on Cinder Lane with a small blackpowder pistol wrapped in a rag. It was old and ugly, with a single barrel and a hammer like a thumbnail.

"Antique," Mira said.

"Simple." He strapped a holster for it above his left ankle. "Nothing in it to go wrong."

Aldric packed his healer's kit as carefully as other men pack for a wedding. Bandages, rolled and pinned. Two kinds of splint. Catgut and needles in folded oilcloth. Carbolic. Three flasks of boiled water. He looked at the bottle of laudanum for a long time before packing it. He checked that his notebook was in the satchel with his spellbook, checked again, then moved the notebook to his coat. "I always write after," he said when he caught Mira watching. "I expect there'll be a great deal to write after."

Sera tested herself. She didn't call it that, but that was what it was. In the evenings she sat at the window with her compass in her lap and reached gently into the air of the flat. A teaspoon lifted off the table and set down again. A page of the newspaper turned without a hand. They were small, careful things. Before each one she held a breath and let it go, a pause that had not been there a month ago. Afterwards she looked at her hands as if she expected them to have changed.

"How is it?" Mira asked on the last evening.

"Fine." Sera turned the compass over. The needle swung, wavered and found north. "Normal. I think it's normal." She was quiet a moment. "I don't remember what normal felt like, exactly. So I'm not sure."

Mira packed the plan.

There was a folded survey map of the north-east road, bought from a surveyor's office in the name of a timber company that did not exist. There was a Covenant seal in an oilskin pouch at the bottom of her pack, to be shown to no one unless the alternative was being shot. She had a schedule in her own shorthand that nobody else could read, and a flare pistol with three cartridges: red for abort. Last came the charges, twelve squat waxed-paper cylinders of Sootborn stump powder. They were packed in sawdust in a crate stencilled AGRICULTURAL, and a Covenant carter who did not look at her face had delivered them to the alley door.

She left the arc pistol behind. It was the best weapon she owned and it would be dead weight past the last village; Galvanic machinery didn't like the Wild edge, and the Wild edge didn't like it back. She took the two-shot derringer that lived in her boot and the knife that lived in her sleeve. Then she sat down at the desk and did what she always did last. She imagined each of them dead, one at a time, and asked herself what she would have done differently.

It was a terrible habit. It had kept people alive.

---

They left Ashwick at first light on the fifth day with the cart, two Covenant horses that did not know them yet, and a tarpaulin over the crate that said AGRICULTURAL.

The north-east road went out through the factory suburbs and the market gardens and into real country: hedges and stubble fields, stone farmhouses with smoke going straight up into an empty sky. Mira had grown up on the gradient, where every street had an opinion about the world. Out here nothing argued. The cows didn't care about the Aether. The hedges were full of sparrows. Farm dogs ran out barking at the cart wheels and gave up at their own gates. It was so ordinary it looked painted.

They didn't talk much. Kael drove. Aldric sat beside him with a book open that he did not read. Sera rode in the back on the tarpaulin with her arms around her knees, watching the land go by. Mira walked beside the horses for long stretches to spare them, and to think.

Mira knew this kind of silence from rooms where someone was about to say something difficult and hadn't decided how. All four of them knew where they were going and what they meant to do there. Each of them, privately, was working out what it might cost.

They passed the place where the motorcar had started coughing on the mill job. Further on, quarantine posts the old government had driven into the verge forty years back still leaned there, the paint long gone and the wire long stolen. Then came the last village and its field of dead cars: saloons and lorries and a charabanc with grass growing through the seats. Engines didn't run past this point, so the cars had been left where they stopped, and nobody had ever bothered to tow them back.

Horses didn't care about the Aether either. That was why the Covenant had sent horses.

On the evening of the second day the hedges gave way to scrub and the scrub to the first tall trees. The light went green and gold through leaves very slightly the wrong shape. In the back of the cart, Sera lifted her head like someone who'd heard her name across a crowded room. The air took on a faint taste of copper, with something sweeter underneath, like cut grass where nothing had been cut.

Then the road bent, and there was the settlement: patched roofs and railway-carriage walls, vegetable plots, a fence of sharpened poles, smoke. At the edge of the road stood a small grey-haired woman with her arms folded and a shawl over her shoulders. She looked as if she had been standing there all afternoon.

Pell did not smile. Mira had not expected her to. She looked them over one by one, Kael on the driving seat, Aldric with his book, Sera in the back, Mira at the horses' heads, as a farmer looks at the sky.

"You came," she said.

"We said we would."

"People say a lot of things." Pell turned and started for the gate. Over her shoulder, without breaking stride, she said, "Come inside. We have maps."
