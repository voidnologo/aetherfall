# Chapter 7

On the third morning, Sera looked at herself in the mirror over the washstand, and the girl in the mirror looked back with somebody else's eyes.

Not entirely somebody else's. They were still hers — still dark, still a little too intense, still with the shadows under them that she'd had since she was nine. But the ring was wider. Before the valley it had been a thin pale line round the edge of each iris, like frost on the rim of a cup; you had to look for it. Now it was a band. A clear, cold blue-white, a third of the way in towards the pupil, and it caught the morning light from the window and gave it back. Not glowing. Just *there*. In daylight, in a plain room, with nothing magical happening anywhere for miles.

She leaned closer to the glass. Something in the blue-white moved — or seemed to. Like light on water at the bottom of a well.

She stood there for a long time. Then she washed her face, and went out.

---

Aldric checked her every day. He checked everyone every day — Kael three times, morning, noon and night — but he checked her with a particular care that she noticed and did not mention. Pulse. Pupils. A hand flat on her forehead. He'd look into her eyes with the little borrowed hand-lamp, first one and then the other, and make a note in his notebook, and frown, and not say anything.

On the fourth day she said, "Tell me."

He was sitting on the end of her cot with the lamp in his hand. He'd got new spectacles from somewhere — an old pair of a Sootborn grandmother's, round and gold-rimmed and slightly too small, so that he looked like an owl that had borrowed a disguise. He took them off. He cleaned them on the end of his waistcoat. She waited.

"What you did in the valley," he said at last. "When Kael fell. I felt it from the other ridge. Everyone in the valley felt it. I've been trying to work out what it was, and I can't. It wasn't any working I know. It had no shape. There was force in it — a great deal of force — and something else, something I don't have a word for. It went out from you in every direction at once." He put the spectacles back on. "I don't think you cast it, Sera. I think you opened a door and stood in it."

"I know," she said. "I didn't — I don't remember choosing anything."

"No." He was quiet a moment. "A thing like that leaves marks. The ring is wider. You've seen that. It's visible now in ordinary light, without any strain on you — that's new. That's permanent, I think. And there's something else, deeper, that I can feel when I look but can't measure. As if something in you has been stretched and hasn't gone all the way back."

"Like a jumper," Sera said. "That's been on a tall person."

Aldric's mouth twitched. "Not the metaphor I'd have chosen. But yes."

She looked at her hands. They had stopped shaking on the second day. She could lift a cup with them now, a real one, without spilling. Yesterday she'd reached, very gently, for a feather on the windowsill and turned it over, and it had been all right — a small clean push, no more than she meant, and then the ache under her breastbone saying *enough, enough for today*. She was so tired. Not sleepy. Tired in her bones, as if they'd been hollowed out and were slowly filling back up with something that wasn't quite marrow.

"You're changing," Aldric said. "Faster than anyone has written down. I'm not going to tell you it's progress, and I'm not going to tell you it's damage, because I don't know. It's change. That's all I can honestly call it."

"Is it bad?"

He didn't answer right away. He never answered right away when it mattered. It used to drive her mad. She'd come to understand, slowly, that it was a kind of respect.

"It's dangerous," he said finally. "I'm sure of that. Whether it's *bad* —" He spread his hands. "I think that depends on what you become."

Sera turned that over. It sat oddly in her. It was a frightening thing to be told. It was also, she realised, the first time anyone had talked about her changing as if she might have some say in it.

"Thank you," she said. "For not lying."

"I'd never lie to you about this," Aldric said, and went to check on Kael.

---

Pell came on the fifth day, with soup.

She didn't knock. She came in and put the bowl down on the stool by Sera's cot and then sat on the end of the cot herself — not close, not far, exactly where she'd decided to sit — and watched Sera eat. It was good soup. Barley and onions and something green and sharp that Sera didn't recognise and suspected grew only out here.

"There's a boy in the settlement," Pell said, when the bowl was half empty. "Tobin. Eleven. Born here. He has pale patches on the insides of his wrists, like somebody's spilled milk on him. Started when he was four. They don't wash off. In the dark, if you look hard, they shine a little." She folded her hands. "And there's a girl, Wenna. Fourteen. Her hair grows in copper at the roots. Real copper — you can see it catch the light. Her mother cuts it short so the other children don't stare. Doesn't help. It grows back copper."

Sera had stopped eating.

"They aren't sick," Pell said. "They aren't cursed, whatever the chapel people in the last village say. They're Wild-edge children, that's all. The Aether's in the water here, in the bread, in the air they breathe. It's what they grew up in. It's their baseline." She looked at Sera's eyes, steadily, the way she looked at everything. "I don't know if what's happening to you is like what happened to them. You came to it late, and you came to it hard. Maybe it's different. Maybe it isn't." She stood up and took the bowl, though it wasn't finished. "You'll find out. The Wild's slow. But it tells the truth, in the end. More than the city does."

She was at the door when Sera said, "Pell."

Pell turned.

"Could I meet them? Tobin and Wenna?"

Pell considered her. Something moved in her stone-grey face that might, on another face, have been the beginning of a smile.

"They'd like that," she said. "They've been asking about the lady with the lamp eyes."

---

Kael was up on the fifth day too.

Up was a generous word for it. He came out of the cabin in the middle of the afternoon on Aldric's arm, moving the way old men move on ice, every step thought about, one hand pressed flat to his side as though holding something in. His arm was strapped across his chest in a sling Pell had made from a flour sack. His face had some colour back in it, not much. He made it as far as the bench against the wall of the common house, in the sun, and lowered himself onto it in three stages with his teeth set, and then sat very still with his eyes closed until the grey went out of his lips.

Sera came and sat beside him. They both had blankets round their shoulders, like invalids, which was what they were.

They didn't talk. They sat in the sun and watched the settlement. A woman was hanging washing between two railway carriages. A pair of goats were eating something they weren't supposed to. Tobin, the boy with the milk-pale wrists, was trying to teach a much smaller girl to whistle through her fingers, and failing, and the small girl was laughing too hard to try.

After a long time Sera said, "Thank you for not dying."

Kael opened one eye and looked at her sideways. Then he closed it again.

"Thank you for not letting me," he said.

That was all. Neither of them said anything else. They didn't need to.

---

Aldric wrote.

He'd been writing since the second day. He sat at the end of the long table in the common house with his notebook and a borrowed pen and a pot of ink that was mostly soot, and he wrote in his small, square, careful hand for hours at a time, stopping only to check on Kael and Sera and to eat when somebody put food in front of him. Sera watched him over the edge of her own journal. He wrote about the wall — the way it had felt to build, the way it had drunk him, the dead instant when the big lorry went and he'd held it up with nothing. He wrote about letting it go, and what came back up the lines, and the ringing. He told her about the ringing on the sixth day, unprompted, as though reporting a symptom: a high clear note somewhere behind his hearing, like a wineglass that had been struck, very faint now, fading a little each day but still there.

"Will it go?" she asked.

"I think so. I'll write down how long it takes." He turned a page. "I'm keeping a record. Of all of it. How far I went past what I had, and for how long, and what it's been like coming back." He looked up at her, over the grandmother's spectacles. "So that next time — before the next job — I can read it, and remember what it cost. I'm afraid I'll forget otherwise. I think everyone does."

Sera thought about that. She thought about her own journal, the one nobody was allowed to read, with its looping half-legible pages about the tunnels and the case and the thing she'd seen when she touched it.

She closed it, and held it in her lap.

---

Mira spent a lot of time with Pell.

Sera saw them from the bench, most days: two small figures in shawls walking the boundary fence together, slowly, all the way round, the way you'd walk a field you were thinking of buying. Mostly Pell talked and Mira listened, which was the wrong way round from how Mira usually did things. Sometimes they stopped and stood together looking out at the Wild beyond the fence, not talking at all.

On the evening of the sixth day Mira came back and sat by the fire in the common house and told them, in the voice she used for things that mattered and that she didn't want to make a fuss of, what Pell had said.

The settlement had cabins. Out at the edge of the trees, past the last of the vegetable plots, where the Wild began. Nobody used them much. If the Society ever needed somewhere to go — somewhere off the roads and out of the city and away from people who wanted things from them — the cabins were there. No questions. No price.

"Is that an alliance?" Aldric asked.

"No," Mira said. "She was very clear about that." She looked into the fire. "It's not membership. It's not a contract. It's —" She stopped. Mira almost never stopped in the middle of a sentence. "It's what people do, I think. People who've stopped trusting the factions. They leave a door open for each other."

"Kinship," said Kael, from the corner, where he was propped up on cushions with his eyes closed.

Mira looked at him. "Yes," she said. "That."

---

It was the small things that Sera remembered, later.

Kael's first proper meal at the common table, on the sixth day, and Aldric, without saying anything or seeming to notice he was doing it, reaching over and cutting up Kael's meat for him because Kael's good arm was still slow. And Kael letting him, without saying anything either.

Sitting by the fire on the seventh evening, so tired she could barely keep her eyes open, and finding at some point that her head had come to rest against Kael's shoulder — the good one — and that he hadn't moved it, and wasn't going to.

Mira coming round with the teapot, filling everyone's cups, looking at the two of them on the bench and not saying a single word about it. Just filling the cup and moving on.

Aldric and Mira at the end of the long table late at night with a map between them and their heads close, talking low, the way people talk when they're sharing a weight between them and trying to work out how to carry it home.

It was the first time, Sera thought, that she had looked at the four of them and seen not a Society, not a charter, not a job. Just a family. A strange one, patched together out of spare parts like the carriages in this settlement. But a family.

---

She walked to the edge of the settlement on the evening of the seventh day, alone, to look at the Wild.

The fence was poles sharpened at the top and lashed together with wire, and beyond it there was a strip of rough grass, and beyond the grass the trees began. Not the ordinary trees of the valley. The Wild's trees: tall and close and dark, their trunks furred with moss that glowed faintly blue in the dusk, their leaves very slightly the wrong shape. Between them the air hung still and luminous, like the air inside a church.

She stood with her hands on the fence and listened.

It was different now. She'd felt the Wild before, on the mill job — a great vast pressure, a presence, the feeling of something huge turning to look at her. That was still there. But the pulse that had come and gone in the valley like weather — the surge and break, the rising and falling — wasn't here. Out here, past the fence, the Aether didn't pulse. It just *was*. Deep and quiet and steady. It didn't want anything from her. It didn't push or pull. It was like standing at the edge of a very deep, very still lake at night, and feeling it breathe.

She understood, standing there, that she was going to have to choose. Not tonight. Not soon, maybe. But at some point. Between being the girl the Aether poured through whenever it liked, deeper each time, wider, until there was more of it in her than there was of her — and being a person who decided things. Who opened the door because she meant to, and closed it again.

She didn't know yet which she'd be. She thought about Aldric on the end of her cot, cleaning his spectacles. *It depends on what you become.*

She thought about the case in the tunnels. About laying her hand on it. About the thing she'd seen when she did — the network, the lines of light running out under the ground from the foundry to the mill and on, further, to places she couldn't name, all of them pulsing together like a great slow heart. She'd carried it alone for a month. She hadn't told anyone. Not Aldric, who'd want to write it down. Not Kael, who'd want to do something about it. Not Mira, who'd want to decide who else should know.

She would tell them. She decided it there, at the fence, with her hands on the cold wire. Not yet — not with Kael still unable to cut up his own dinner, and Aldric's ears still ringing, and her own well still so low she could feel the bottom. But soon. When they were themselves again.

She didn't know when that would be. She hoped it would be soon.

Behind her, across the settlement, somebody called her name for supper. She went.

---

On the morning of the eighth day they left.

Kael could walk without anyone's arm, if he went slowly. Aldric had most of what he'd lost back; the ringing was fainter. Sera could reach for small things without it hurting, though the well inside her was nowhere near full and she wasn't sure, any more, what full would feel like. Mira had the cart loaded and the route planned and the horses fed, and Sera suspected she'd had all three done for two days and had been waiting for the rest of them.

Pell was at the edge of the road, where she'd been when they arrived. Arms folded. Shawl round her shoulders.

Mira went to her. They stood looking at each other for a moment, two small women with the same kind of eyes. It wasn't a goodbye, Sera thought. It wasn't a handshake on a deal. It was something else — the look of two people who'd seen each other clearly and decided that what they'd seen was all right.

"Stay alive," Pell said.

"You too," said Mira.

That was all. They climbed into the cart. Kael rode in the back, propped on the sacks, his rifle across his knees, Sera beside him. Aldric and Mira rode the horses. The cart creaked out onto the road and turned south-west, towards the hedges and the stubble fields and, beyond them, the city.

Sera looked back once. The settlement was already disappearing among the trees — the patched roofs, the smoke, a small figure at the gate with one hand raised. Two smaller figures beside her, waving. A boy and a girl. Copper hair catching the morning sun.

Then the road bent, and the trees closed behind them, and she turned round, and looked forward, towards home.
