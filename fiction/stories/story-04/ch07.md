# Chapter 7

On the third morning Sera looked at herself in the mirror over the washstand, and the girl in the mirror looked back with somebody else's eyes.

They were still mostly hers: dark, a little too intense, with the shadows under them she'd had since she was nine. But the ring was wider. Before the valley it had been a thin pale line round the edge of each iris, frost on the rim of a cup; you had to look for it. Now it was a band of clear, cold blue-white reaching a third of the way in towards the pupil. It caught the morning light from the window and gave it back. It wasn't glowing. It was just *there*, in daylight, in a plain room, with nothing magical happening for miles.

She leaned closer to the glass. Something in the blue-white seemed to move, like light on water at the bottom of a well.

She stood there a long time. Then she washed her face and went out.

---

Aldric checked her every day. He checked everyone every day, Kael three times, morning, noon and night. But he checked her with a particular care that she noticed and didn't mention. Pulse. Pupils. A hand flat on her forehead. He looked into each of her eyes with the little borrowed hand-lamp, made a note in his notebook, frowned, and said nothing.

On the fourth day she said, "Tell me."

He was sitting on the end of her cot with the lamp in his hand. He'd found new spectacles somewhere, a Sootborn grandmother's old pair, round and gold-rimmed and slightly too small, so that he looked like an owl in a borrowed disguise. He took them off and cleaned them on the end of his waistcoat. She waited.

"What you did in the valley," he said at last. "When Kael fell. I felt it from the other ridge. Everyone in the valley felt it. I've been trying to work out what it was, and I can't. It was no working I know. It had no shape. There was force in it, a great deal of force, and something else I don't have a word for. It went out from you in every direction at once." He put the spectacles back on. "I don't think you cast it, Sera. I think you opened a door and stood in it."

"I know," she said. "I didn't... I don't remember choosing anything."

"No." He was quiet a moment. "A thing like that leaves marks. The ring is wider; you've seen that. It shows now in ordinary light, without any strain on you, and that's new. I think it's permanent. There's something else, deeper, that I can feel when I look but can't measure. Something in you has been stretched and hasn't gone all the way back."

"Like a jumper," Sera said. "That's been on a tall person."

Aldric's mouth twitched. "Not the metaphor I'd have chosen. But yes."

She looked at her hands. They had stopped shaking on the second day. She could lift a real cup with them now without spilling. Yesterday she had reached very gently for a feather on the windowsill and turned it over. It had been all right: a small clean push, no more than she meant, and then the ache under her breastbone saying *enough, enough for today*. She was so tired. She wasn't sleepy. She was tired in her bones, as though they'd been hollowed out and were slowly filling with something that wasn't quite marrow.

"You're changing," Aldric said. "Faster than anyone has written down. I won't tell you it's progress, and I won't tell you it's damage, because I don't know. It's change. That's all I can honestly call it."

"Is it bad?"

He didn't answer right away. He never did when it mattered. It used to drive her mad, until she slowly understood it was a kind of respect.

"It's dangerous," he said finally. "I'm sure of that. Whether it's *bad*..." He spread his hands. "I think that depends on what you become."

Sera turned that over. It sat oddly in her. It was a frightening thing to be told. It was also, she realised, the first time anyone had talked about her changing as if she might have some say in it.

"Thank you," she said. "For not lying."

"I'd never lie to you about this," Aldric said, and went to check on Kael.

---

Pell came on the fifth day with soup.

She didn't knock. She came in, put the bowl down on the stool by Sera's cot, and sat on the end of the cot herself, neither close nor far, exactly where she'd decided to sit. She watched Sera eat. It was good soup: barley and onions and something green and sharp that Sera didn't recognise and suspected grew only out here.

"There's a boy in the settlement," Pell said when the bowl was half empty. "Tobin. Eleven. Born here. He has pale patches on the insides of his wrists, like somebody's spilled milk on him. Started when he was four. They don't wash off. In the dark, if you look hard, they shine a little." She folded her hands. "And there's a girl, Wenna. Fourteen. Her hair grows in copper at the roots. Real copper; you can see it catch the light. Her mother cuts it short so the other children don't stare. Doesn't help. It grows back copper."

Sera had stopped eating.

"They aren't sick," Pell said. "They aren't cursed, whatever the chapel people in the last village say. They're Wild-edge children, that's all. The Aether's in the water here, in the bread, in the air they breathe. It's what they grew up in. It's their baseline." She looked at Sera's eyes as steadily as she looked at everything. "I don't know if what's happening to you is like what happened to them. You came to it late, and you came to it hard. Maybe it's different. Maybe it isn't." She stood and took the bowl, though it wasn't finished. "You'll find out. The Wild's slow. But it tells the truth in the end. More than the city does."

She was at the door when Sera said, "Pell."

Pell turned.

"Could I meet them? Tobin and Wenna?"

Pell considered her. Something moved in her stone-grey face that, on another face, might have been the start of a smile.

"They'd like that," she said. "They've been asking about the lady with the lamp eyes."

---

Kael was up on the fifth day too.

Up was a generous word for it. In the middle of the afternoon he came out of the cabin on Aldric's arm, moving as old men move on ice, thinking about every step, one hand pressed flat to his side. His arm was strapped across his chest in a sling Pell had made from a flour sack. His face had a little colour back. He made it as far as the bench against the wall of the common house, in the sun. He lowered himself onto it in three stages with his teeth set, then sat very still with his eyes closed until the grey went out of his lips.

Sera came and sat beside him. They both had blankets round their shoulders like the invalids they were.

They didn't talk. They sat in the sun and watched the settlement. A woman was hanging washing between two railway carriages. A pair of goats were eating something they weren't supposed to. Tobin, the boy with the milk-pale wrists, was trying to teach a much smaller girl to whistle through her fingers. She was laughing too hard to try.

After a long time Sera said, "Thank you for not dying."

Kael opened one eye, looked at her sideways and closed it again.

"Thank you for not letting me," he said.

Neither of them said anything else.

---

Aldric wrote.

He'd been writing since the second day. He sat at the end of the long table in the common house with his notebook, a borrowed pen and a pot of ink that was mostly soot. For hours at a time he wrote in his small, square, careful hand, stopping only to check on Kael and Sera and to eat when somebody put food in front of him. Sera watched him over the edge of her own journal. He wrote about building the wall and how it had drunk him, and the dead instant when the big lorry went and he'd held it up with nothing. He wrote about letting it go, what came back up the lines, and the ringing. On the sixth day he told her about the ringing, unprompted, as though reporting a symptom. It was a high clear note somewhere behind his hearing, a struck wineglass, very faint now and fading a little each day, but still there.

"Will it go?" she asked.

"I think so. I'll write down how long it takes." He turned a page. "I'm keeping a record of all of it. How far I went past what I had, and for how long, and what it's been like coming back." He looked up at her over the grandmother's spectacles. "So that before the next job I can read it and remember what it cost. I'm afraid I'll forget otherwise. I think everyone does."

Sera thought about that, and about her own journal, the one nobody was allowed to read. Its looping, half-legible pages were about the tunnels and the case and the thing she'd seen when she touched it.

She closed it and held it in her lap.

---

Mira spent a lot of time with Pell.

Most days Sera saw them from the bench: two small figures in shawls walking the boundary fence together, slowly, all the way round, as you'd walk a field you were thinking of buying. Mostly Pell talked and Mira listened, the wrong way round for Mira. Sometimes they stopped and stood looking out at the Wild beyond the fence without talking at all.

On the evening of the sixth day Mira came back and sat by the fire in the common house. She told them what Pell had said, in the voice she used for things that mattered and that she didn't want to make a fuss of.

The settlement had cabins out at the edge of the trees, past the last of the vegetable plots, where the Wild began. Nobody used them much. If the Society ever needed somewhere to go, off the roads and out of the city and away from people who wanted things from them, the cabins were there. No questions. No price.

"Is that an alliance?" Aldric asked.

"No," Mira said. "She was very clear about that." She looked into the fire. "It's not membership. It's not a contract. It's..." She stopped. Mira almost never stopped in the middle of a sentence. "It's what people do, I think. People who've stopped trusting the factions. They leave a door open for each other."

"Kinship," said Kael from the corner, where he was propped on cushions with his eyes closed.

Mira looked at him. "Yes," she said. "That."

---

Later, Sera remembered the small things.

She remembered Kael's first proper meal at the common table on the sixth day. Without a word, without seeming to notice he was doing it, Aldric reached over and cut up Kael's meat for him, because Kael's good arm was still slow. Kael let him, and didn't say anything either.

She remembered sitting by the fire on the seventh evening, so tired she could barely keep her eyes open. At some point her head came to rest against Kael's good shoulder. He didn't move it, and wasn't going to.

She remembered Mira coming round with the teapot and filling everyone's cups. Mira looked at the two of them on the bench, said not a single word about it, filled the cup and moved on.

She remembered Aldric and Mira late at night at the end of the long table, heads close over a map, talking low. They talked like people sharing a weight and working out how to carry it home.

It was the first time, Sera thought, that she had looked at the four of them and seen a family instead of a Society or a charter or a job. It was a strange family, patched together out of spare parts like the carriages in this settlement. But it was a family.

---

On the evening of the seventh day she walked alone to the edge of the settlement to look at the Wild.

The fence was poles sharpened at the top and lashed together with wire. Beyond it lay a strip of rough grass, and beyond the grass the trees began. These weren't the ordinary trees of the valley. They were the Wild's trees, tall and close and dark, their trunks furred with moss that glowed faintly blue in the dusk, their leaves very slightly the wrong shape. The air between them hung still and luminous, like the air inside a church.

She stood with her hands on the fence and listened.

It was different now. She'd felt the Wild before on the mill job: a vast pressure, a presence, something huge turning to look at her. That was still there. But the pulse that had come and gone in the valley like weather, surging and breaking, wasn't here. Past the fence the Aether didn't pulse. It just *was*, deep and quiet and steady. It didn't want anything from her. It didn't push or pull. She might have been standing at the edge of a very deep, very still lake at night, feeling it breathe.

Standing there, she understood that she would have to choose. Not tonight, and maybe not soon, but at some point. She could be the girl the Aether poured through whenever it liked, deeper and wider each time, until there was more of it in her than there was of her. Or she could be a person who decided things: who opened the door because she meant to, and closed it again.

She didn't know yet which she'd be. She thought of Aldric on the end of her cot, cleaning his spectacles. *It depends on what you become.*

She thought about the case in the tunnels. When she laid her hand on it she had seen a network, lines of light running out under the ground from the foundry to the mill and on, further, to places she couldn't name, all pulsing together like a great slow heart. She'd carried it alone for a month. She hadn't told Aldric, who'd want to write it down, or Kael, who'd want to do something about it, or Mira, who'd want to decide who else should know.

She would tell them. She decided it there at the fence, with her hands on the cold wire. Not yet, with Kael still unable to cut up his own dinner, Aldric's ears still ringing and her own well so low she could feel the bottom. But soon, when they were themselves again.

She didn't know when that would be. She hoped it would be soon.

Behind her, across the settlement, somebody called her name for supper. She went.

---

On the morning of the eighth day they left.

Kael could walk without anyone's arm if he went slowly. Aldric had most of what he'd lost back, and the ringing was fainter. Sera could reach for small things without it hurting, though the well inside her was nowhere near full, and she was no longer sure what full would feel like. Mira had the cart loaded, the route planned and the horses fed. Sera suspected she'd had all three done for two days and had been waiting for the rest of them.

Pell stood at the edge of the road where she'd stood when they arrived, arms folded, shawl round her shoulders.

Mira went to her. For a moment they stood looking at each other, two small women with the same kind of eyes. Sera didn't think it was a goodbye, or a handshake on a deal. It was the look of two people who had seen each other clearly and decided that what they saw was all right.

"Stay alive," Pell said.

"You too," said Mira.

They climbed into the cart. Kael rode in the back propped on the sacks, his rifle across his knees, with Sera beside him. Aldric and Mira rode the horses. The cart creaked out onto the road and turned south-west, towards the hedges and the stubble fields and the city beyond them.

Sera looked back once. The settlement was already disappearing among the trees: the patched roofs, the smoke, a small figure at the gate with one hand raised. Two smaller figures waved beside her, a boy and a girl, copper hair catching the morning sun.

Then the road bent and the trees closed behind them. She turned round and looked forward, towards home.
