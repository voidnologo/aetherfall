# Chapter 3

The thing about a long shot was that it was mostly waiting, and the waiting was mostly breathing.

Kael lay behind the beech with his cheek on the stock and his eye a hand's breadth from the glass, and breathed. In for four. Hold. Out for six. Let the body settle a little lower into the leaves each time, the way a stone settles into mud. The rifle sat on his folded jacket on the trunk, and the jacket sat on the trunk, and the trunk sat on the hill, and the hill had been sitting there since before anyone had a word for it. All he had to do was become part of the stack.

He'd paced the range twice yesterday evening. Two hundred and ten yards to the near edge of the water, a little downhill. He'd thrown handfuls of dry grass into the air at dusk and again this morning, and watched them fall. Light breeze from the west, quartering, a touch stronger down by the river where the valley funnelled it. Hold a finger's width into it. Hold a hair low for the slope. He'd zeroed the scope in a quarry outside Ashwick on Tuesday with eleven rounds and a paper target nailed to a fencepost, and the rifle shot true and a fraction left, which he knew, and had already accounted for.

None of it was clever. That was the point. A good shot wasn't a gift. It was a list of small things done in the right order by someone who'd stopped thinking about them.

The engines got louder.

---

The convoy came down into the valley from the south at the speed of a walking horse, and Kael counted it the way he'd counted crowds on Harrier Street on market days: front to back, twice, and then once more because the third count was the one you trusted.

An outrider first — a man on a motorcycle with a rifle slung across his back, riding slow, standing on the pegs to see over the hedgerow. Then a lorry, square-nosed, canvas over its bed, riding heavy on its springs. Then two armoured transports, slab-sided, painted the grey of wet slate, with slits for windows and a stubby gun on a ring mount on the roof of the second. Then a second lorry. Then a third, bigger than the others, with something on its flatbed under a tarpaulin that stood up in odd shapes — a dish, a frame of coils, a mast folded down along the cab. Then two light utility cars with their hoods up, men in civilian coats in the back. Ironwork in the transports. Collective researchers in the cars.

The big lorry was the one. Even under the canvas he could tell. The other vehicles had their windows down to the morning. The big lorry's driver had his shut tight.

Eight vehicles. Two of them full of men who were paid to shoot people. Kael let that sit in him for a moment, and then he set it down.

The outrider reached the ford, stopped, looked at the water, looked up at the ridges on either side. Kael didn't move. You didn't move when someone was looking. The outrider's gaze crossed the beech, the rocks, the trees, and went on. He waved the convoy forward and drove into the river himself, legs out, the motorcycle bucking over the stones, and came up spraying on the far side and stopped at the bottom of the switchback to wait.

The lead lorry eased down the bank. Water came up to its axles. The driver was a man in a flat cap with his elbow out of the window. From two hundred and ten yards, through the glass, Kael could see that he needed a shave. He could see that he was talking to someone in the cab beside him, and laughing. He could see his teeth.

He'll have a mother, Kael thought. A wife, maybe. A boy who waits up.

That was the trade. He'd known it was the trade since the kitchen on Harrier Street, and he'd known it before that, on the night eighteen years into his service when he'd taken off the grey coat and hung it on a hook and walked away. You didn't get to choose what things cost. You only got to choose whether you paid.

He checked the wind. A sparrow on a twig below the beech, its feathers lifting the same way as before. Same as before.

In for four. Out for six. Halfway through the next breath, in the empty place between one heartbeat and the next, he squeezed.

The rifle kicked against his shoulder like a door slammed in a stairwell.

Down in the river, the driver's head went back. The lorry rolled another yard on its own momentum, slewed, and stopped broadside in the deepest part of the ford with the water piling white against its wheels and the engine coughing out. Someone in the cab started screaming.

Kael worked the bolt. The brass went spinning into the leaves. He didn't watch it.

Behind the lorry the column concertinaed — brakes, horns, a shout, the second lorry nosing into the back of the transport in front of it with a crunch he heard a half-second late. On the far bank the outrider spun his machine round and looked back down at the river, mouth open.

Then the far bank changed.

Kael saw it at the edge of the glass. A shimmer, first, rising up out of the road at the foot of the switchback like heat off summer cobbles. Then it thickened. The light bent around it. Behind it the slope and the trees went soft and swimming, as if he were looking at them through the bottom of a bottle. It stood twenty feet high and as wide as the road and a little more, and it hummed — not a sound so much as a pressure on his teeth. The outrider, caught on the near side of it, put out a hand towards it and snatched the hand back as if he'd touched a stove.

*There you are*, Kael thought, and something in his chest he hadn't noticed tightening let go a little. *Good man.*

From the west ridge came a noise like the end of the world.

He didn't look. He didn't need to. He heard the timber go: the crack of the chains, a deep groaning crunch, then a sound like a train coming downhill without a track, wood on wood on stone, rolling and rolling and rolling, and under it a roar of soil and shale. When he risked a glance, the road behind the last car was gone. In its place was a raw brown scar and a heap of trunks as high as a house, still shifting, a last log tumbling end over end down the slope and coming to rest against the back wheel of a utility car as gently as a cat.

Nose. Tail. Far bank.

The convoy was in a box.

---

Kael slung the rifle across his back by its strap. He picked up the revolver off the flat stone and put it in his right hand. The knife came out of its sheath into his left. He went down the hill.

The deer trail was a narrow brown thread through bracken that he and Mira had walked twice last night with a shuttered lamp, and he ran it now with his body remembering what his head had already forgotten — root here, drop there, a beech with a split trunk where the trail bent left. Somewhere behind and above him Aldric was holding up a piece of the sky. Somewhere across the valley Sera was finding her breath. Two and a half minutes, Mira had said. Three, he'd said. He'd been lying. He thought it would be four.

The Ironwork men were good. He'd give them that.

He could hear them before he could see them — no shouting now, no horns. Orders. Short ones. The transports' rear doors banging open. Men spilling out in grey and taking cover behind wheels and engine blocks without being told twice. And then the firing started, a ragged crackle at first and then steady, a dozen rifles laying rounds up into both ridges, not at anything in particular. They didn't have targets. They didn't need targets. They were telling the hillside to keep its head down.

Rounds went through the trees above him with a sound like someone tearing canvas. A branch dropped across the trail ahead. He went over it.

At the bottom the trail opened out into a strip of rough grass and gravel, fifty yards wide, between the foot of the ridge and the road. No cover. He'd known there would be no cover. He crouched at the treeline for exactly one breath, picked his line — the gap between the second transport and the second lorry — and went.

He ran the way his sergeant had taught him at nineteen on the drill square at Coldwater Barracks: low, fast, not in a straight line, never looking at the thing that wanted to kill you. Gravel skidded under his boots. Something cracked past his right shoulder close enough that he felt the air move, and then he was there, behind the second lorry, back against its wheel with the smell of hot oil and canvas and his own breath sawing in his chest.

Someone had seen him. That was all right. Someone was always going to see him.

He jammed the revolver into his belt and pulled the first charge out of the satchel at his hip.

---

The big lorry first. It was the next vehicle down, just ahead of the cars, and he went along the line on the river side of the column, away from the firing, bent double, the river loud on his left. Under the tarpaulin, up close, he could see a corner of what they'd brought — a curved dish of grey metal the width of a cartwheel, rows of copper coils wrapped round porcelain posts, a squat iron box with a dozen thick cables coming out of it. It hummed faintly. He could feel the hum in the fillings of his teeth, the same way he'd felt Aldric's barrier, but colder. Duller. Like standing too close to a tram line.

He wedged the first cylinder of stump powder into the joint where the coil frame met the deck, pushed the fuse in, struck a match on the iron, and lit it. It caught with a hiss and a spit of sparks. Two minutes. Then the second charge on the box with the cables. Then he went under the lorry and set the third on the fuel tank, because Mira had said *if we hit nothing else, we hit these*, and he intended to hit them very hard.

Back the other way. Lorry two: munitions, almost certainly, from the way its springs were bowed and the way the Ironwork men kept carefully away from it. Charge. Match. Fuse.

He came round the back of lorry two and there was a man there.

Grey coat. Young — younger than Kael by twenty years. A rifle held across his chest that he'd been carrying from one bit of cover to the next and wasn't ready to use. His eyes went wide. Kael was already moving, because his body had done this before, a long time ago, in an alley behind a dockside warehouse where nobody had been ready either. He didn't decide. He didn't think about the man's mother. The knife went in under the arm and the young man made a sound like a sigh and folded over it, and Kael pulled the knife back and stepped over him and did not look at his face.

Afterwards, he would. He knew he would. Not now.

Above him, on the west ridge, something like a gust of wind went through the trees all at once, and a second Ironwork man who had been coming round the corner of the transport with a pistol was picked up off his feet and slammed into the slab-grey side of the vehicle hard enough to dent it, and dropped, and lay still.

*Sera.* He didn't look up at the ridge. He touched two fingers to the brim of a hat he wasn't wearing, and went on.

Third charge. Fourth, on the first transport's engine, because it was there. He was counting now without meaning to — seconds since the first match, seconds since the second. Ninety. A hundred. The firing on the ridges had changed. Fewer rifles shooting at nothing. More shooting at the west ridge, together, in bursts. The ring-mounted gun on the second transport had woken up and was hammering away at the slope where the timber had come from, and under the hammering he could hear men shouting a word over and over that he eventually understood to be *caster*.

They'd found her. Of course they had. You couldn't drop a hill on someone and stay hidden.

Something round and black bounced off the gravel beside his boot and rolled under the lorry.

He went flat. He didn't think about it — he was already down, arms over his head, face in the stones, when the grenade went off. It was close. The noise was a solid thing that picked him up an inch and set him down again. Gravel rained on his back. A hot line opened across his left forearm from wrist to elbow, as if someone had laid a poker on it, and when he looked, his sleeve was torn and there was blood coming through and a sliver of grey metal standing out of the meat of his arm like a splinter.

He pulled it out with his teeth and spat it into the river. He picked up the knife from where he'd dropped it. He kept moving.

One more. The last charge in the satchel, for the second transport — the one with the gun on its roof that was chewing up Sera's hillside.

He set it at the back of the transport, under the iron ladder that went up to the gun ring, where the gunner above him was too busy to look down. He struck the match. It took two strikes — his hands had begun to shake, and the left one was slick — but it caught, and the fuse caught, and then it was done. Every charge set. Every fuse burning. All he had to do now was cross fifty yards of open gravel and climb a hill before the valley floor became the inside of a furnace.

He turned.

There was a man at the corner of the transport.

Not a boy, this time. An older man, steady, an Ironwork sergeant by the cut of him, with a moustache gone grey and a long-barrelled service pistol held out in front of him in one hand in the old way, aimed at the middle of Kael's chest from ten feet off. He'd come round the front while Kael was busy with the match. He'd been quiet about it. He knew his work.

Kael's revolver was out of his belt and up. Eighteen years of training and seven years of this exact gun, and the muzzle found the man's chest before Kael knew he'd lifted it, and he squeezed.

*Click.*

Not a bang. Not a jam he could feel — no grind, no stiffness. The hammer fell. Nothing answered. The gun in his hand felt, for one long and terrible instant, like a stranger's gun. Like a gun made of wood. Like the gun he'd held in the foundry tunnels, gone slow and dull and far away.

He had time to think, very clearly: *the air*. The Aether was thick down here. It was thick everywhere today. Sera's push, Aldric's wall, the whole valley humming with it. And under all of it, he had been holding a machine that did not like this kind of air, and it had chosen now to tell him so.

The sergeant fired.

It hit him low on the right side like a hammer swung by a big man. He went back against the transport's wheel. His legs went out from under him all at once, the way legs do when they've been told about something terrible before the rest of you has, and he slid down the tyre into the gravel and sat there with the revolver still in his hand and his mouth open.

The pain came a moment later. When it did, it was enormous. It came up from his side and filled him, the way water fills a lock — no room for anything else, no up or down — and he heard a voice saying *hell*, quietly, in a conversational sort of way, and understood that it was his own.

He'd been shot once before. Twenty-three years old, a warehouse in the Low Docks, a thief with a pocket pistol and a bad night. It had felt like this. Not like being stabbed. Like being punched by something that didn't stop punching.

The sergeant was coming. Kael heard his boots on the gravel. Unhurried. A professional, finishing his work.

Kael dropped the revolver. He rolled onto his left side, and the world went white with it, and he pulled up his trouser leg with fingers that would not quite close and found the little blackpowder pistol in its ankle holster where it had been all morning, ugly and old and stupid, nothing in it but a spring and a hammer and a pinch of powder. He thumbed the hammer back. He pointed it at the sergeant's legs because they were what he could see.

It went off with a bang like a door blown open and a cloud of white smoke that stank of rotten eggs. The sergeant screamed and went down sideways, clutching his knee.

*Nothing in it to go wrong*, Kael thought, and almost laughed, and didn't, because laughing would have been a very bad idea.

He tried to stand. He got as far as his knees. His legs would not hold. There was a lot of blood on the gravel, more than there should have been, and it took him a long, slow moment to understand that it was his. His vision had begun to narrow at the edges, the world drawing in like curtains on a stage.

The fuses. The fuses were still burning.

He began to crawl. Knife in his left hand, empty pistol in his right, elbows and knees. Away from the lorries. Towards the strip of gravel and the hill and the deer trail and the beech with the split trunk and the long brown way back up. His boots left marks behind him. Red ones. He saw them over his shoulder and then he stopped looking over his shoulder.

He'd made about fifteen yards when the first charge went.

---

The big lorry went up first. He knew it was the big lorry, because the sound had something else in it — a high, tearing shriek like metal being pulled apart by hands, and a flash at the edge of his sight that was not the colour fire was supposed to be. Then the munitions lorry, a moment after, and that one was just noise, so much noise that it stopped being noise and became weather.

The ground left him.

He was in the air. He was aware of it in a mild, interested way. He was turning slowly over, and there was sky, and then gravel, and then sky. Then a wall of stone came up out of nowhere and hit him along the whole left side of his body, and he heard — clearly, distinctly, the way you hear a twig snap in a quiet wood — the sound of his own ribs breaking.

Everything went white.

---

When it came back he was on his back looking at the sky.

It was a very blue sky. A clear morning. There was smoke across it, black and brown, rolling and folding in on itself, and through the smoke now and then a bird going over, high up, minding its own business. Somewhere off to his left, things were burning with a roar like a furnace door left open. Somewhere further off, men were still shooting, but less now, and further away, and it didn't seem to have much to do with him.

His left arm was wrong. He could feel that it was wrong without looking. His side was wet and hot and his back was wet and cold. He tried to take a breath, a proper breath, the kind you took before a long shot, in for four — and something in his chest caught and bubbled, and he coughed, and tasted copper, and it wasn't the Aether.

*Well*, he thought. *That's not good.*

"Aldric," he said. Or thought he said. He couldn't hear whether the word had left his mouth. "Mira."

The edges of the sky had started to go dark. Not dusk-dark. A different kind. It came in from the corners and it was very patient.

He lay and looked up at the blue part, in the middle, while there was still a blue part.

From somewhere a long way off — from up on the ridge, maybe, or from the bottom of a well — there came a voice he knew, thin and cracked with running, calling his name.
