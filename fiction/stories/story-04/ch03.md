# Chapter 3

A long shot was mostly waiting, and the waiting was mostly breathing.

Kael lay behind the beech with his cheek on the stock and his eye a hand's breadth from the glass, and breathed. In for four. Hold. Out for six. Each breath let his body settle a little lower into the leaves, a stone settling into mud. The rifle sat on his folded jacket, the jacket on the trunk, the trunk on the hill. The hill had been sitting there since before anyone had a word for it. All he had to do was become part of the stack.

He'd paced the range twice yesterday evening: two hundred and ten yards to the near edge of the water, a little downhill. At dusk and again this morning he'd thrown handfuls of dry grass into the air and watched them fall. The breeze was light, from the west, quartering, a touch stronger by the river where the valley funnelled it. Hold a finger's width into it. Hold a hair low for the slope. On Tuesday he'd zeroed the scope in a quarry outside Ashwick with eleven rounds and a paper target nailed to a fencepost. The rifle shot true and a fraction left. He knew that and had already allowed for it.

None of it was clever. A good shot wasn't a gift. It was a list of small things done in the right order by someone who'd stopped thinking about them.

The engines got louder.

---

The convoy came down into the valley from the south at the speed of a walking horse. Kael counted it as he'd counted market-day crowds on Harrier Street: front to back, twice, and then once more, because the third count was the one you trusted.

An outrider led on a motorcycle, a rifle slung across his back, riding slow and standing on the pegs to see over the hedgerow. Then came a lorry, square-nosed, canvas over its bed, riding heavy on its springs. Two armoured transports followed, slab-sided and painted the grey of wet slate, with slits for windows and a stubby gun on a ring mount on the roof of the second. Behind them were a second lorry and a third, bigger than the others. Something on its flatbed stood up in odd shapes under a tarpaulin: a dish, a frame of coils, a mast folded down along the cab. Last came two light utility cars with their hoods up and men in civilian coats in the back. Ironwork rode in the transports, Collective researchers in the cars.

The big lorry was the one. Even under the canvas he could tell. Every other driver had his window down to the morning. The big lorry's driver kept his shut tight.

Eight vehicles. Two of them full of men paid to shoot people. Kael let that sit in him for a breath, then set it down.

The outrider reached the ford and stopped. He looked at the water, then up at the ridges on either side. Kael didn't move. You didn't move when someone was looking. The outrider's gaze crossed the beech, the rocks, the trees, and went on. He waved the convoy forward and drove into the river himself, legs out, the motorcycle bucking over the stones. He came up spraying on the far side and stopped at the bottom of the switchback to wait.

The lead lorry eased down the bank until the water reached its axles. Its driver wore a flat cap and had his elbow out of the window. From two hundred and ten yards, through the glass, Kael could see that he needed a shave. He was talking to someone in the cab beside him, and laughing. Kael could see his teeth.

He'll have a mother, Kael thought. A wife, maybe. A boy who waits up.

That was the trade. He'd known it since the kitchen on Harrier Street. He'd known it before that, too, on the night eighteen years into his service when he took off the grey coat, hung it on a hook and walked away. You didn't get to choose what things cost. You only chose whether you paid.

He checked the wind. Below the beech a sparrow sat on a twig, its feathers lifting the same way as before.

In for four. Out for six. Halfway through the next breath, in the empty place between one heartbeat and the next, he squeezed.

The rifle kicked against his shoulder like a door slammed in a stairwell.

Down in the river the driver's head went back. The lorry rolled another yard on its own momentum and slewed. It stopped broadside in the deepest part of the ford with the water piling white against its wheels and the engine coughing out. Someone in the cab started screaming.

Kael worked the bolt. The brass spun away into the leaves. He didn't watch it.

Behind the lorry the column concertinaed: brakes, horns, a shout, then the crunch of the second lorry nosing into the transport ahead, which reached him half a second late. On the far bank the outrider spun his machine round and stared back down at the river, mouth open.

Then the far bank changed.

Kael saw it at the edge of the glass. First a shimmer rose out of the road at the foot of the switchback, like heat off summer cobbles. It thickened. The light bent around it, and behind it the slope and the trees went soft and swimming, as though seen through the bottom of a bottle. It stood twenty feet high and a little wider than the road. It hummed, less a sound than a pressure on his teeth. Caught on the near side, the outrider put out a hand towards it and snatched it back as if from a stove.

*There you are*, Kael thought. Something in his chest he hadn't noticed tightening let go a little. *Good man.*

Then the west ridge let go.

He didn't need to look to know the timber had gone. He heard the chains crack and a deep groaning crunch, and then a sound like a train coming downhill without a track, wood on wood on stone, rolling and rolling and rolling, and under it a roar of soil and shale. When he risked a glance, the road behind the last car was gone. In its place lay a raw brown scar and a heap of trunks as high as a house, still shifting. A last log tumbled end over end down the slope and came to rest against the back wheel of a utility car, as gently as a cat.

Nose. Tail. Far bank.

The convoy was in a box.

---

Kael slung the rifle across his back. He took the revolver off the flat stone in his right hand and drew the knife with his left. He went down the hill.

The deer trail was a narrow brown thread through bracken. He and Mira had walked it twice last night with a shuttered lamp, and now his body remembered what his head had already forgotten: root here, drop there, a split-trunked beech where the trail bent left. Somewhere behind and above him Aldric was holding up a piece of the sky. Somewhere across the valley Sera was getting her breath back. Mira had said two and a half minutes. He'd said three. He'd been lying. He thought it would be four.

The Ironwork men were good. He'd give them that.

He heard them before he saw them. There was no shouting now, and no horns, only short orders. The transports' rear doors banged open. Men in grey spilled out and took cover behind wheels and engine blocks without being told twice. Then the firing started, ragged at first and then steady, a dozen rifles laying rounds into both ridges. They had no targets and needed none. They were telling the hillside to keep its head down.

Rounds went through the trees above him with a sound like tearing canvas. A branch dropped across the trail ahead. He went over it.

At the bottom, the trail opened onto a strip of rough grass and gravel fifty yards wide between the foot of the ridge and the road. There was no cover, as he'd known there wouldn't be. He crouched at the treeline for one breath and picked his line, the gap between the second transport and the second lorry. Then he went.

He ran as his sergeant had taught him at nineteen on the drill square at Coldwater Barracks: low, fast, never in a straight line, never looking at the thing that wanted to kill you. Gravel skidded under his boots. Something cracked past his right shoulder close enough that he felt the air move. Then he was behind the second lorry with his back against its wheel. He smelled hot oil and canvas, and his breath sawed in his chest.

Someone had seen him. That was all right. Someone was always going to see him.

He jammed the revolver into his belt and pulled the first charge out of the satchel at his hip.

---

The big lorry came first. It was the next vehicle down, just ahead of the cars. He worked along the river side of the column, away from the firing, bent double, the river loud on his left. Close up, under the tarpaulin, he could see a corner of what they'd brought. There was a curved grey dish the width of a cartwheel, rows of copper coils wrapped round porcelain posts, and a squat iron box with a dozen thick cables coming out of it. It hummed faintly in the fillings of his teeth. Aldric's barrier had done the same, but this was colder and duller.

He wedged the first cylinder of stump powder into the joint where the coil frame met the deck and pushed in the fuse. He struck a match on the iron and lit it. It caught with a hiss and a spit of sparks. Two minutes. The second charge went on the box with the cables. Then he crawled under the lorry and set the third on the fuel tank. Mira had said *if we hit nothing else, we hit these*, and he meant to hit them very hard.

He went back the other way to lorry two. It was almost certainly munitions: its springs were bowed, and the Ironwork men kept carefully away from it. Charge. Match. Fuse.

He came round the back of lorry two and there was a man there.

He wore a grey coat and was twenty years younger than Kael. He held a rifle across his chest, carrying it from one bit of cover to the next, not ready to use it. His eyes went wide. Kael was already moving. His body had done this before, long ago, in an alley behind a dockside warehouse where nobody had been ready either. He didn't decide, and he didn't think about the man's mother. The knife went in under the arm. The young man made a sound like a sigh and folded over it. Kael pulled the knife back, stepped over him and did not look at his face.

He would, afterwards. He knew he would. Not now.

On the west ridge a gust went through all the trees at once. A second Ironwork man was coming round the corner of the transport with a pistol. He was picked up off his feet and slammed into the slab-grey side of the vehicle hard enough to dent it. He dropped and lay still.

*Sera.* Kael didn't look up at the ridge. He touched two fingers to the brim of a hat he wasn't wearing, and went on.

The third charge went in. The fourth went on the first transport's engine, because it was there. He was counting now without meaning to, seconds since the first match and the second. Ninety. A hundred. The firing on the ridges had changed. Fewer rifles were shooting at nothing. More were firing at the west ridge, together, in bursts. The ring-mounted gun on the second transport had woken up and was hammering at the slope where the timber had come from. Under the hammering, men were shouting one word over and over. Eventually he made it out: *caster*.

They'd found her. Of course they had. You couldn't drop a hill on someone and stay hidden.

Something round and black bounced off the gravel beside his boot and rolled under the lorry.

He was flat before he'd thought about it, arms over his head, face in the stones, when the grenade went off. It was close. The noise was a solid thing that lifted him an inch and set him down. Gravel rained on his back. A hot line opened across his left forearm from wrist to elbow, as though someone had laid a poker on it. When he looked, his sleeve was torn and bleeding, and a sliver of grey metal stood out of the meat of his arm.

He pulled it out with his teeth and spat it into the river. He picked up the knife where he'd dropped it. He kept moving.

One charge was left in the satchel, for the second transport, the one whose roof gun was chewing up Sera's hillside.

He set it at the back, under the iron ladder that went up to the gun ring, where the gunner was too busy to look down. The match took two strikes, because his hands had begun to shake and the left one was slick. Then the match caught, and the fuse caught, and it was done. Every charge was set and every fuse burning. All he had to do now was cross fifty yards of open gravel and climb a hill before the valley floor became the inside of a furnace.

He turned.

A man stood at the corner of the transport.

This one wasn't a boy. He was older and steady, an Ironwork sergeant by the cut of him, with a moustache gone grey. He held a long-barrelled service pistol out in one hand in the old way, aimed at the middle of Kael's chest from ten feet off. He'd come round the front while Kael was busy with the match, and he'd been quiet about it. He knew his work.

Kael's revolver was out of his belt and up. After eighteen years of training and seven years with this exact gun, the muzzle found the man's chest before Kael knew he'd lifted it. He squeezed.

*Click.*

There was no bang, and no jam he could feel, no grind or stiffness. The hammer fell and nothing answered. For one long, terrible instant the gun in his hand felt like a stranger's. It felt like a gun made of wood, like the gun that had gone slow and dull and far away in the foundry tunnels.

He had time to think, very clearly: *the air*. The Aether was thick down here, thick everywhere today, with Sera's push and Aldric's wall and the whole valley humming. All morning he had been holding a machine that disliked this kind of air, and it had chosen now to tell him so.

The sergeant fired.

It hit him low on the right side like a hammer swung by a big man. He fell back against the transport's wheel. His legs went out from under him all at once, as legs do when they've been told something terrible before the rest of you has. He slid down the tyre into the gravel and sat there with the revolver still in his hand and his mouth open.

The pain came a moment later, and it was enormous. It rose from his side and filled him as water fills a lock, leaving no room for anything else, no up or down. He heard a voice say *hell* quietly, in a conversational sort of way, and understood that it was his own.

He'd been shot once before, at twenty-three, in a warehouse in the Low Docks: a thief with a pocket pistol and a bad night. It had felt like this. It wasn't like being stabbed. It was like being punched by something that didn't stop punching.

The sergeant's boots came across the gravel, unhurried. A professional, finishing his work.

Kael dropped the revolver and rolled onto his left side. The world went white. He pulled up his trouser leg with fingers that wouldn't quite close, and found the little blackpowder pistol in its ankle holster where it had been all morning: ugly, old and stupid, nothing in it but a spring and a hammer and a pinch of powder. He thumbed the hammer back and pointed it at the sergeant's legs, because they were what he could see.

It went off with a flat bang and a cloud of white smoke that stank of rotten eggs. The sergeant screamed and went down sideways, clutching his knee.

*Nothing in it to go wrong*, Kael thought. He almost laughed. He didn't, because laughing would have been a very bad idea.

He tried to stand and got as far as his knees. His legs wouldn't hold. There was more blood on the gravel than there should have been. It took him a long, slow moment to understand that it was his. His vision was narrowing at the edges, the world drawing in like stage curtains.

The fuses were still burning.

He began to crawl on elbows and knees, knife in his left hand, empty pistol in his right. He crawled away from the lorries, towards the strip of gravel and the hill and the deer trail, towards the split-trunked beech and the long brown way back up. His boots left red marks behind him. He saw them over his shoulder, and then he stopped looking over his shoulder.

He'd made about fifteen yards when the first charge went.

---

The big lorry went up first. He knew it from the sound, which had something else in it: a high, tearing shriek like metal pulled apart by hands. A flash at the edge of his sight was not the colour fire was supposed to be. The munitions lorry followed a moment later, and that was pure noise, so much of it that it stopped being noise and became weather.

The ground left him.

He was in the air, and he noticed it in a mild, interested way. He turned slowly over: sky, then gravel, then sky. A wall of stone came out of nowhere and hit him along the whole left side of his body. He heard his own ribs break, clear and distinct, a twig snapping in a quiet wood.

Everything went white.

---

When it came back he was on his back looking at the sky.

It was a very blue sky, a clear morning. Smoke rolled across it, black and brown, folding in on itself. Now and then a bird went over through the smoke, high up, minding its own business. Somewhere to his left things were burning with a steady roar. Further off, men were still shooting, but less now and further away. It didn't seem to have much to do with him.

His left arm was wrong. He could feel it without looking. His side was wet and hot and his back wet and cold. He tried to take a proper breath, the kind you took before a long shot, in for four. Something in his chest caught and bubbled. He coughed and tasted copper, and it wasn't the Aether.

*Well*, he thought. *That's not good.*

"Aldric," he said, or thought he said. He couldn't hear whether the word had left his mouth. "Mira."

The edges of the sky had started to go dark. It wasn't dusk. It was a different kind of dark, coming in from the corners, very patient.

He lay and looked up at the blue part in the middle, while there was still a blue part.

From a long way off, from up on the ridge maybe or from the bottom of a well, a voice he knew was calling his name, thin and cracked with running.
