# Chapter 8

They talked more on the way home than they had on the way out.

Mira noticed it on the first afternoon, riding beside the cart with the reins loose in one hand. Not about anything much. Kael, propped on the sacks in the back, told Sera a long and plainly untrue story about a goat he'd once had to arrest. Aldric explained to nobody in particular why hedgerows were a kind of history book, and how you could count the centuries in one by the number of different trees growing in thirty yards of it, and then got down off his horse and counted, and was pleased. Sera laughed at things. Not often, and not loudly. But she laughed.

On the way out, the silence had been four people doing sums in their heads. On the way back, the sums were done, and they'd all survived them, and the talking was what came out of the space where the sums had been.

The horses would have to go back to Pell. They'd send them with somebody, Mira thought, once things had settled. *Once things have settled.* The phrase kept coming to her, and every time it did, she didn't like it any more than the time before.

---

They saw Ashwick at sunset on the third day.

It came up over the last rise the way it always did, all at once — the long smudge of it across the river plain, the factory chimneys in the west standing up black against a sky the colour of a bruise, the smoke from them leaning east on the wind. The old town on the eastern side, all steep roofs and spires, softer, blurred at the edges. And between them, if you knew how to look, the gradient: a faint shimmering haze lying across the middle of the city like heat above a road in August, where the two halves of the world met and argued.

Home.

Twelve days. She'd been away from the city for longer — much longer, once, the year she was twenty and had run a string of jobs up the coast and come back with no money and a scar on her cheek that she still told different stories about. But she had never come back to it feeling like this. As if she were walking into a house she'd lived in all her life, and some small thing had been moved while she was out, and she couldn't yet see what.

She put it down to tiredness. She didn't believe it.

---

Thessaly Vane was at the Wet Ember.

Of course she was. They came in out of the dusk, the four of them — Kael on his own two feet, slowly, Aldric at his elbow not quite holding it — and Hesper looked up from behind the bar, and her eyes went straight to Kael's sling and the way he was walking, and stayed there a long moment. Then she put down the glass she was polishing and went into the back without a word and came out again with a chair with arms on it, the only one in the house, and set it by the fire.

Kael lowered himself into it. "Hesper," he said.

"Dunn." She looked at him. "Don't bleed on it."

That was Hesper's way of saying she was glad he wasn't dead. Mira filed it with the other things.

And at the end of the bar, with a glass of the house red she hadn't touched, sat Thessaly. The third time. Mira counted it. The third time in a season that the Covenant had come down out of the good chairs on Lantern Row to sit in a tavern on the gradient and wait for them. The weight had shifted again. Further, this time. She wondered how far it could shift before it tipped something over.

Thessaly didn't stand. She waited until they'd all sat, and Hesper had brought beer, and then she came to the table and sat with them and spoke in a low, clear voice, like someone reading out the minutes of a meeting.

The convoy action had succeeded as specified. Five vehicles destroyed. The suppression equipment — all of it, every dampener and disruptor and all three of the dead-zone engines — wrecked beyond salvage. Six Ironwork contractors dead; four wounded; one of the wounded a sergeant who would not walk without a stick again. No Collective research staff killed. The Collective had announced, through the proper channels and in the dullest possible language, that its northern survey was postponed for operational reasons. The Covenant's own estimate was that it would take them two months, at the least, to source and build replacement equipment.

"Two months," Mira said.

"At least." Thessaly turned her glass by the stem. "My principals are very pleased. Payment has been made. At the premium rate." She slid a folded paper across the table to Mira, who did not open it. "And a little more, for the cart. They'd like it to be understood that they consider the Society a significant asset."

*Significant asset.* Mira tasted the words. They were warm on top. She suspected they were cold underneath.

"Thank them for us," she said.

"I will."

Thessaly stood. She put a coin on the table for the wine. And then — as she turned to go, as though it had just occurred to her — she said, "Ms. Cade. A word. At the bar."

---

At the bar, with Hesper polishing at the far end, very carefully not listening, Thessaly said it quietly and without any change in her composure at all.

"They know it was you."

Mira didn't move.

"They don't have evidence," Thessaly went on. "Nothing that would stand in front of a magistrate or the Assembly. If they did, we'd be having a very different conversation. But they know. Somebody saw a caster on the west ridge. Somebody saw a tall man in spectacles running down the east one. Ironwork's sergeant has been talking." She looked at Mira steadily. "I can't protect you from what comes next. I want you to understand that. I can give you information. Money. Materiel. I can't put the Covenant's name between you and them. Not without breaking the thing that made you useful."

"What comes next?" Mira said.

"I don't know exactly." Thessaly's voice didn't change. "Agents. Observers. People who'll want to know where you sleep and who you talk to. Possibly worse than that. The Collective doesn't like losing money, and it hates being embarrassed, and you've done both to it in front of its own board." She paused. "Be careful where you sleep, Ms. Cade. Be careful who you trust. They play for keeps when they've been hurt."

Then, after a moment, quieter still, in a voice Mira had never heard her use: "I'm sorry."

Mira looked at her. For a moment she thought about asking *sorry for what* — for the job, or the warning, or for the fact that the Covenant would sleep easy tonight in its good chairs while four people on Harrier Street did not. She didn't ask. She nodded. Thessaly nodded back. The transaction was complete.

Thessaly went out into the dark. The door swung shut on the street, and the fire crackled, and Hesper put down her glass.

---

Mira went back to the table and told them.

She told them the convoy was destroyed. She told them the equipment was gone. She told them the Collective had postponed its expedition and that the Covenant's estimate was two months. She told them that the Covenant was pleased, and had paid well, and considered them an asset. She told them about Hesper's chair, because it would make Kael smile, and it did.

She didn't tell them about the warning.

She hadn't decided not to. That was what she would tell herself afterwards, and she would know, even as she told herself, that it was only half true. What happened was that she looked at them — Kael in the good chair by the fire with the colour still not right in his face, Aldric with his borrowed spectacles and the ringing he thought nobody knew he could still hear, Sera with her new eyes catching the firelight, holding her beer in both hands as if it were a cup of tea — and some quick, practised part of her that had been keeping people alive since she was fourteen made the decision without asking her permission. *Not tonight.* They'd been through the worst day of their lives. Kael needed one night at home in his own bed, feeling safe, whether or not he was. Tomorrow was soon enough to put the weight down on the table and share it out.

She could feel herself doing it. She did it anyway.

Kael was watching her.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. She saw him notice — the small pause in her account where the warning would have been, the shape of the gap — and she saw him file it, quietly, in the place where he'd filed the coffee house. He looked at her for a moment across the table with no expression at all. Then he looked at the fire.

The thing had a name between them now. She'd given it one herself, in Central, over Fels's coffee. *I'll keep doing it. Because I think it works.* And here she was, the same week, doing it, and he'd seen, and there was no taking it back.

She accepted that. She'd chosen it. She'd carry it.

---

The flat was as they'd left it. Almost.

Mira went up the stairs first, as she always did, with her key in her hand. And as she fitted the key into the lock, she saw — without looking, the way she saw most things — that there was a faint fresh scratch on the brass round the keyhole. A small bright crescent, a few hairs wide, at about the place where a pick would slip if the hand holding it was skilled, but not quite skilled enough.

She opened the door normally. She stood aside to let Kael through. She said, "Mind the step." She did not look at the lock again.

Inside: the kitchen, the table, the lamp, the smell of a room that had been shut up for twelve days. Dust on the windowsills. A stale loaf in the bread crock, gone green. Kael's chair. Aldric's books. Sera's window seat with the cushion still dented where she'd last sat. Everything where it should be.

She went to the dresser and opened the top drawer to put away Thessaly's folded paper.

Her arc pistol was in the drawer. She'd left it there twelve days ago, wrapped in its oilcloth, laid with its grip towards the back and its muzzle towards the left-hand corner, the way she always laid it, so that she could reach in without looking and come out with it ready. It was still wrapped. It was still in the drawer. Its muzzle was pointing at the right-hand corner.

Half an inch. Perhaps less. Someone had unwrapped it, and looked at it, and wrapped it up again, and put it back almost exactly where it had been.

Mira laid the paper on top of it. She closed the drawer.

Kael's revolver wasn't in the flat at all. It was lying somewhere on the gravel of a valley fifty miles north, with the river going past it, or else it was in an Ironwork evidence locker with a label tied to its trigger guard. She'd watched him pull the little blackpowder pistol from his ankle holster on the cart this morning, and check it, and put it in his coat pocket instead. He hadn't mentioned the revolver once. He'd carried it for seven years.

The back window. She went to it to let in some air, because the room was stale, and she always opened the back window first. The catch was a plain brass hook-and-eye that she'd fitted herself, and she always seated it with the hook turned a little to the left, because it held better that way, and because nobody else would think to.

It was turned a little to the right.

She opened the window, and let the air in, and closed it again, and seated the catch with the hook to the left.

---

Mrs. Brant knocked at nine.

She was the widow across the landing, a small round woman of seventy with a lace collar and a cat called Admiral, and she had brought a pot of tea on a tray because she had heard them come up the stairs and she knew, she said, what it was like to come back to a cold house. She was genuinely kind. Mira genuinely liked her. She sat at the kitchen table and poured, and admired Aldric's spectacles, and was told that Kael had had a fall off a cart, and was very sorry to hear it.

"Oh — and those two gentlemen found you, did they?" she said, as she was going. "The ones from the Authority?"

Mira's hand did not stop on the teapot. "Which gentlemen were those, Mrs. Brant?"

"Why, the two from the Greycoats. Last — oh, Tuesday, it would have been. Four days ago. They knocked at your door, and when there was no answer they knocked at mine." She smiled. "They said they were doing a welfare check. I thought that was very odd, myself. I've lived on this street for forty years and I've never known the Greycoats check on anybody's welfare. But they were very polite. I told them you'd all been gone a week and more, and I didn't know when you'd be back. And they thanked me and went off."

"How kind of them," Mira said. "What were they like? In case they call again."

Mrs. Brant thought. "Oh — ordinary. One tall, one not. Good coats. Not Greycoat coats, now I think of it — just ordinary grey ones. The tall one had a little moustache. The other one did the talking. Very nice manners." She paused at the door. "He had a ring. A signet. With a sort of — wheel on it. A cog." She shook her head. "Funny, the things you notice."

"Isn't it," said Mira, and smiled, and thanked her for the tea, and shut the door, and stood with her back against it for a moment with her eyes closed.

*A cog.*

---

She didn't tell them that night.

The others settled the way they always settled, as if the flat had been waiting to fold round them again. Aldric at the kitchen table with his notebook open, writing in the small square hand, the pen scratching. Sera at her window seat with her knees drawn up and her own journal on them, the one nobody was allowed to read. Kael on the couch under a blanket with a book he wasn't reading, the low lamp on beside him, one hand coming up now and again, without his seeming to notice, to rest flat against his side.

It felt like home. It *was* home. It felt like family, and Mira, who had built this one herself, on purpose, out of a disgraced constable and a sacked scholar and a girl with lightning in her hands, sat in the middle of it and felt it all round her like a coat, and was afraid for it in a way she had not let herself be in the valley.

When the others had gone to bed she took her chair to the front window and sat in the dark and looked down at Harrier Street.

It was quiet. The gas lamp on the corner made its familiar yellow pool on the cobbles. Somewhere down towards the factories a tram ground along its rails. On the far side of the street, opposite the cobbler's, there was the mouth of the alley that ran down behind the bakery; and in the mouth of the alley, just inside the shadow where the lamplight didn't reach, there was a shape.

It might have been a man standing very still. It might have been a dustbin with a sack on it. It might have been a trick of the light and her own tired eyes and twelve days of looking for enemies on every ridge.

She watched it for ten minutes.

It didn't move.

She watched it for ten more. In that time a cat crossed the street, and a drunk went by singing, and the tram came back the other way. The shape in the alley didn't move. That, she thought, was either a very good sign or a very bad one, and she found that she did not know which, and that not knowing was worse than either.

She got up quietly, and lit the small lamp on the desk, and opened her own journal, and wrote in the quick private shorthand that nobody else could read.

*Possible watcher, mouth of bakery alley. Lock scratched. Arc pistol moved. Back catch turned. Two men at the door Tuesday, "Greycoats", one signet with a cog. Collective — beginning. Watch. Tell them tomorrow.*

She looked at the last three words for a long time. Then she underlined them. Twice.

On the hook by the door, Sera's compass hung on its cord in the still air of the flat. As Mira watched, it turned — slowly, all the way round, the needle swinging past north without stopping — and swung back, and wavered, and settled.

She looked out of the window again.

The lamp. The cobbles. The mouth of the alley.

The shape had not moved.
