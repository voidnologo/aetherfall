# Chapter 8

They talked more on the way home than they had on the way out.

Mira noticed it on the first afternoon, riding beside the cart with the reins loose in one hand. They didn't talk about anything much. Kael, propped on the sacks in the back, told Sera a long and plainly untrue story about a goat he'd once had to arrest. Aldric explained to nobody in particular that hedgerows were a kind of history book: you could count the centuries in one by the number of different trees in thirty yards of it. Then he got down off his horse, counted, and was pleased. Sera laughed at things, not often or loudly, but she laughed.

On the way out the silence had been four people doing sums in their heads. Now the sums were done and they had all survived them. The talking came out of the space the sums had left.

The horses would have to go back to Pell. They'd send them with somebody, Mira thought, once things had settled. *Once things have settled.* The phrase kept coming back to her, and she liked it less each time.

---

They saw Ashwick at sunset on the third day.

It came up over the last rise all at once, as it always did: a long smudge across the river plain. In the west the factory chimneys stood black against a sky the colour of a bruise, their smoke leaning east on the wind. The old town on the eastern side was all steep roofs and spires, softer, blurred at the edges. Between them, if you knew how to look, lay the gradient, a faint shimmering haze across the middle of the city like heat above a road in August, where the two halves of the world met and argued.

Home.

Twelve days. She'd been away longer, once. The year she was twenty she ran a string of jobs up the coast and came back with no money and a scar on her cheek that she still told different stories about. But she had never come back feeling like this. It was like walking into a house she'd lived in all her life and sensing that some small thing had been moved while she was out, without yet seeing what.

She put it down to tiredness. She didn't believe it.

---

Thessaly Vane was at the Wet Ember.

Of course she was. The four of them came in out of the dusk, Kael on his own two feet, slowly, with Aldric at his elbow not quite holding it. Hesper looked up from behind the bar. Her eyes went straight to Kael's sling and the way he was walking, and stayed there. Then she put down the glass she was polishing, went into the back without a word, and came out with the only chair in the house that had arms. She set it by the fire.

Kael lowered himself into it. "Hesper," he said.

"Dunn." She looked at him. "Don't bleed on it."

That was Hesper's way of saying she was glad he wasn't dead. Mira filed it with the other things.

At the end of the bar, with a glass of house red she hadn't touched, sat Thessaly. Mira counted: the third time in a season the Covenant had come down out of the good chairs on Lantern Row to wait for them in a gradient tavern. The weight had shifted again, further this time. She wondered how far it could shift before something tipped over.

Thessaly didn't stand. She waited until they had all sat down and Hesper had brought beer. Then she came to the table, sat with them, and spoke in a low, clear voice, as if reading out the minutes of a meeting.

The convoy action had succeeded as specified. Five vehicles were destroyed. The suppression equipment, every dampener and disruptor and all three dead-zone engines, was wrecked beyond salvage. Six Ironwork contractors were dead and four wounded, one of them a sergeant who would never walk again without a stick. No Collective research staff had been killed. Through the proper channels and in the dullest possible language, the Collective had announced that its northern survey was postponed for operational reasons. The Covenant estimated it would take them at least two months to source and build replacement equipment.

"Two months," Mira said.

"At least." Thessaly turned her glass by the stem. "My principals are very pleased. Payment has been made at the premium rate." She slid a folded paper across the table. Mira didn't open it. "And a little more, for the cart. They'd like it understood that they consider the Society a significant asset."

*Significant asset.* Mira tasted the words. They were warm on top. She suspected they were cold underneath.

"Thank them for us," she said.

"I will."

Thessaly stood and put a coin on the table for the wine. As she turned to go, as though it had only just occurred to her, she said, "Ms. Cade. A word. At the bar."

---

At the bar, with Hesper polishing at the far end and very carefully not listening, Thessaly said it quietly, without any change in her composure.

"They know it was you."

Mira didn't move.

"They don't have evidence," Thessaly went on. "Nothing that would stand up in front of a magistrate or the Assembly. If they did, we'd be having a very different conversation. But they know. Somebody saw a caster on the west ridge. Somebody saw a tall man in spectacles running down the east one. Ironwork's sergeant has been talking." She looked at Mira steadily. "I can't protect you from what comes next. I want you to understand that. I can give you information, money, materiel. I can't put the Covenant's name between you and them without breaking the thing that made you useful."

"What comes next?" Mira said.

"I don't know exactly." Thessaly's voice didn't change. "Agents. Observers. People who'll want to know where you sleep and who you talk to. Possibly worse. The Collective doesn't like losing money, and it hates being embarrassed, and you've done both to it in front of its own board." She paused. "Be careful where you sleep, Ms. Cade. Be careful who you trust. They play for keeps when they've been hurt."

After a moment she added, quieter still, in a voice Mira had never heard her use: "I'm sorry."

Mira looked at her and thought about asking *sorry for what*. For the job? For the warning? Or because the Covenant would sleep easy in its good chairs tonight while four people on Harrier Street did not? She didn't ask. She nodded, and Thessaly nodded back. The transaction was complete.

Thessaly went out into the dark. The door swung shut. The fire crackled, and Hesper put down her glass.

---

Mira went back to the table and told them.

The convoy was destroyed. The equipment was gone. The Collective had postponed its expedition, by the Covenant's estimate for two months. The Covenant was pleased, had paid well and considered them an asset. She told them about Hesper's chair, because it would make Kael smile, and it did.

She didn't tell them about the warning.

She hadn't decided not to. That was what she would tell herself afterwards, knowing even as she told it that it was only half true. What happened was that she looked at them. Kael sat in the good chair by the fire, the colour still not right in his face. Aldric had his borrowed spectacles and the ringing he thought nobody knew he could still hear. Sera's new eyes caught the firelight as she held her beer in both hands like a cup of tea. And some quick, practised part of Mira, the part that had kept people alive since she was fourteen, made the decision without asking her permission. *Not tonight.* They'd been through the worst day of their lives. Kael needed one night at home in his own bed, feeling safe whether he was or not. Tomorrow was soon enough to put the weight on the table and share it out.

She could feel herself doing it. She did it anyway.

Kael was watching her.

He said nothing. She saw him notice the small pause in her account where the warning should have been, the shape of the gap. She saw him file it quietly in the place where he'd filed the coffee house. He looked at her across the table with no expression at all. Then he looked at the fire.

The thing had a name between them now. She'd given it one herself, in Central, over Fels's coffee: *I'll keep doing it. Because I think it works.* Here she was, the same week, doing it. He had seen, and there was no taking it back.

She accepted that. She'd chosen it. She would carry it.

---

The flat was almost as they'd left it.

Mira went up the stairs first, as she always did, key in hand. As she fitted the key into the lock she saw, without looking, a faint fresh scratch on the brass round the keyhole. It was a small bright crescent a few hairs wide, just where a pick would slip if the hand holding it was skilled, but not quite skilled enough.

She opened the door normally and stood aside to let Kael through. "Mind the step," she said. She didn't look at the lock again.

Inside were the kitchen, the table, the lamp, and the smell of a room shut up for twelve days. Dust lay on the windowsills. The loaf in the bread crock had gone green. Kael's chair, Aldric's books, Sera's window seat with the cushion still dented where she'd last sat: everything was where it should be.

She went to the dresser and opened the top drawer to put away Thessaly's folded paper.

Her arc pistol lay there. Twelve days ago she had left it wrapped in its oilcloth, grip towards the back and muzzle towards the left-hand corner, the way she always laid it, so she could reach in without looking and come out with it ready. It was still wrapped and still in the drawer. Its muzzle pointed at the right-hand corner.

The difference was half an inch, perhaps less. Someone had unwrapped it, looked at it, wrapped it again and put it back almost exactly where it had been.

Mira laid the paper on top of it and closed the drawer.

Kael's revolver wasn't in the flat at all. It was lying on the gravel of a valley fifty miles north with the river going past it, or else it was in an Ironwork evidence locker with a label tied to its trigger guard. This morning on the cart she had watched him take the little blackpowder pistol from his ankle holster, check it and put it in his coat pocket. He'd carried the revolver for seven years. He hadn't mentioned it once.

She went to the back window to let in some air, because the room was stale and she always opened the back window first. The catch was a plain brass hook-and-eye she'd fitted herself. She always seated it with the hook turned a little to the left, because it held better that way and because nobody else would think to.

It was turned a little to the right.

She opened the window and let the air in. Then she closed it and seated the catch with the hook to the left.

---

Mrs. Brant knocked at nine.

She was the widow across the landing, a small round woman of seventy with a lace collar and a cat called Admiral. She had heard them come up the stairs, and she brought a pot of tea on a tray because she knew, she said, what it was like to come back to a cold house. She was genuinely kind, and Mira genuinely liked her. At the kitchen table Mrs. Brant poured, admired Aldric's spectacles, heard that Kael had fallen off a cart, and was very sorry.

"Oh, and those two gentlemen found you, did they?" she said as she was going. "The ones from the Authority?"

Mira's hand did not stop on the teapot. "Which gentlemen were those, Mrs. Brant?"

"Why, the two from the Greycoats. Last... oh, Tuesday, it would have been. Four days ago. They knocked at your door, and when there was no answer they knocked at mine." She smiled. "They said they were doing a welfare check. I thought that was very odd, myself. I've lived on this street forty years and I've never known the Greycoats check on anybody's welfare. But they were very polite. I told them you'd all been gone a week and more and I didn't know when you'd be back. They thanked me and went off."

"How kind of them," Mira said. "What were they like? In case they call again."

Mrs. Brant thought. "Oh, ordinary. One tall, one not. Good coats. Not Greycoat coats, now I think of it. Just ordinary grey ones. The tall one had a little moustache. The other one did the talking. Very nice manners." She paused at the door. "He had a ring. A signet, with a sort of wheel on it. A cog." She shook her head. "Funny, the things you notice."

"Isn't it," said Mira. She smiled and thanked her for the tea. Then she shut the door and stood with her back against it for a moment, eyes closed.

*A cog.*

---

She didn't tell them that night.

The others settled as they always did, as if the flat had been waiting to fold round them again. Aldric sat at the kitchen table with his notebook open, his pen scratching in the small square hand. Sera had her window seat, knees drawn up, her own journal on them, the one nobody was allowed to read. Kael lay on the couch under a blanket with a book he wasn't reading and the low lamp on beside him. Now and again, without seeming to notice, he rested one hand flat against his side.

It felt like home, and it *was* home. It felt like family. Mira had built this one herself, on purpose, out of a disgraced constable, a sacked scholar and a girl with lightning in her hands. She sat in the middle of it and felt it round her like a coat. She was afraid for it in a way she had not let herself be in the valley.

When the others had gone to bed she took her chair to the front window and sat in the dark, looking down at Harrier Street.

It was quiet. The gas lamp on the corner made its familiar yellow pool on the cobbles. Somewhere down towards the factories a tram ground along its rails. Opposite the cobbler's, an alley ran down behind the bakery. Just inside its mouth, in the shadow beyond the lamplight, there was a shape.

It might have been a man standing very still. It might have been a dustbin with a sack on it. It might have been a trick of the light, her own tired eyes, and twelve days of looking for enemies on every ridge.

She watched it for ten minutes. It didn't move.

She watched it for ten more. A cat crossed the street. A drunk went by singing. The tram came back the other way. The shape in the alley didn't move. That was either a very good sign or a very bad one. She didn't know which, and not knowing was worse than either.

She got up quietly, lit the small lamp on the desk, and opened her own journal. She wrote in the quick private shorthand nobody else could read.

*Possible watcher, mouth of bakery alley. Lock scratched. Arc pistol moved. Back catch turned. Two men at the door Tuesday, "Greycoats", one signet with a cog. Collective — beginning. Watch. Tell them tomorrow.*

She looked at the last three words for a long time. Then she underlined them twice.

On the hook by the door, Sera's compass hung on its cord in the still air of the flat. As Mira watched, it turned slowly all the way round, the needle swinging past north without stopping. It swung back, wavered and settled.

She looked out of the window again.

The lamp. The cobbles. The mouth of the alley.

The shape had not moved.
