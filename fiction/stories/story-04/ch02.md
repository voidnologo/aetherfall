# Chapter 2

The Sootborn common house had been a railway carriage once, or several. Aldric could see the joins where three of them had been set end to end and roofed over with timber and tin, the old compartment doors still in place along one wall with their brass handles polished by fifty years of hands. Rain drummed on the roof. A stove at the far end ticked and roared. The long table down the middle had been built from a single plank of something dark and heavy, and on it, held flat at the corners with stones, Pell had laid out her maps.

They were not maps the way Aldric understood maps. There was no grid, no scale bar, no compass rose. They had been drawn by hand, in pencil and ink and what he suspected was soot mixed with oil, on the backs of feed sacks and the endpapers of books. Some were old. Some had been drawn over older ones, so that the same hillside appeared two or three times in different hands, each a little different from the last. Trails were marked in a red so faded it was almost brown. Water was blue. And everywhere, in small careful letters, there were notes — *ford is cold till noon*, *bad ground after rain*, *hawks nest here, they'll cry if someone comes*, *don't*.

He wanted, very badly, to copy all of it.

"The Old Cart Road," Pell said, and laid a finger on a long brown line that came down from the top of the sack. "Built before the Tear for timber. We've kept it up since — us and the village. It's the only road north of here wide enough for what they're bringing." Her finger moved. "Here it drops into the valley. Two ridges, east and west. Woods on both, thicker on the east. The road runs down the valley floor with the river beside it, then turns and crosses."

"How deep?" Kael asked.

"Knee. Thigh, after rain. Stone bottom. It's a good ford. It's the only ford." Her finger stopped on a small mark like a closed eye. "The road goes down to the water, crosses, and climbs out the far side on a switchback. Nothing with wheels can go through two abreast. Not with what they're carrying."

Mira leaned over the map with her hands flat on the table. Aldric watched the plan begin to happen in her face — not in words yet, but in the stillness that came over her when the pieces started to fit. It was a thing he had watched a dozen times now and still could not quite describe. Some people thought with their mouths. Mira thought with her whole attention, and you could see it narrow, like a lens drawing focus.

"They have to string out," she said.

"They have to string out," Pell agreed.

---

The plan came together over two hours and a pot of something Pell called tea and Aldric privately classified as a decoction of bark. Mira built it out loud, and they all put their hands on it.

There were three parts.

The strike came first, and it had to be simultaneous, or as near as four people on two ridges could make it. When the lead vehicle entered the water, Kael would stop it — from the east ridge, with the rifle, at a little over two hundred yards. "The driver," he said, and nobody asked him to say it twice. A stalled lorry in the middle of a ford would block the crossing as well as any wall.

Then the far bank. That was Aldric's.

"A barrier," he said. "Across the road where it climbs out. Wide enough to close the switchback. Strong enough that they can't just drive through it." He had worked out the dimensions on the back of an envelope during the drive and been mildly horrified by the arithmetic. "And it has to stay up. Not for a moment. For as long as Kael needs to be on the valley floor."

"Can you hold it that long?" Mira asked.

"I don't know," Aldric said. "I've held one for a minute in a practice yard, with nobody trying to kill me. This would be larger. And longer." He took off his spectacles. He put them back on. "I think so. I want to be honest about what it costs."

Sera would close the back door. Halfway up the west ridge, where the road came down into the valley, the Sootborn kept a stack of felled timber for the winter — trunks as thick as a man, chained and wedged against the slope. Knock out the wedges and the whole stack would come down across the road. "With a push," Pell said, looking at Sera with an expression Aldric could not read. "A big push."

"I can push," Sera said. It was the first thing she had said in an hour.

Nose blocked by a dead lorry. Tail blocked by a hill's worth of timber. Far bank closed by Aldric's working. A convoy with nowhere to go.

Then the sabotage. Two minutes, Mira said, and Kael said three, and they settled on two and a half and agreed that it would really be however long it took. Kael would come down off the east ridge by a trail Pell's people had cut years ago for hauling deer, cross the valley floor and go down the line of vehicles setting charges. The equipment lorries first. "The dampeners," Mira said. "The disruptors. The things they mean to use on the mill. If we hit nothing else, we hit those." Slow fuses — Pell's people used them on stumps. Two minutes from the striking of the match. Aldric and Sera would cover him from above, Sera with whatever force the moment gave her, Aldric with whatever he had left. Mira would watch the back of the column and the road beyond it, because there was always something you hadn't planned for, and in Mira's experience it usually came from behind.

Then the withdrawal. When the charges went, they went — back along the Sootborn trails, three miles south-east through the thickest woods in the valley, to a clearing Pell's people knew. Horses would be waiting. A gift, Pell said; she would want them back. From the clearing they would ride west and south, well away from the road, and come home to Ashwick by the long way round.

"And if it goes wrong?" Kael said.

Mira took the flare pistol out of her coat and put it on the table among the maps. "Red," she said. "One flare. If you see it, you stop whatever you're doing and you go. Not to me. Not to each other. To the trails, and then to the clearing. We meet there. We don't try to save it." She looked at each of them, and Aldric felt the look land on him like a hand on the shoulder. "Nothing in that convoy is worth one of us. If I fire this, the job's over. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Kael?"

"Understood," Kael said.

---

Aldric stood when they were done, because he had decided on the drive that he would, and if he did not do it now he would talk himself out of it.

"Before we sleep," he said, "I'd like to tell you what I have. So you know what to expect from me, and what not to."

It had become a habit, over the last three jobs — a thing he did and the others had come to expect, the way Kael checked doors and Mira checked faces. A ledger of the self. He had found that it helped. Not him, particularly; it never made the fear go away. It helped the others to know where the walls were.

"The barrier is the most expensive thing I've ever attempted," he said. "It will cost me a great deal to raise. And then it will go on costing me, every second I hold it — not like paying a bill but like carrying something heavy uphill. The longer it stands, the less I'll have." He made himself say the next part plainly. "By the time Kael's done on the valley floor, I expect to be at less than half of what I have now. Possibly much less."

"After that?" said Mira.

"After that I do nothing I don't have to. I keep back enough for one serious healing, and a little more for after. I won't attack anyone. That isn't my job tomorrow." He looked at Sera, and then at Kael. "Sera is the one who'll be hitting things. My job is keeping us alive. If I'm casting at a man with a gun, it's because something has already gone very wrong."

Kael nodded slowly. Sera did not look up from the map, but her hand, which had been flat on the paper, curled closed.

"Thank you," Mira said. She meant it. He could tell, because she did not add anything.

---

He did not sleep much.

Pell had given them a cabin at the edge of the settlement, a single room with four cots and a window that looked into the trees. Kael was asleep within minutes, the way soldiers and constables learned to be. Mira lay on her back with her eyes closed and her breathing even and Aldric did not believe for a moment that she was asleep. Sera was at the window. He could see the faint, steady pallor of the ring in her eyes reflected in the black glass, two small cold moons.

He lay and thought about lattices.

In the tunnels, his casting had been something like architecture. He had built each working the way his teachers had taught him, from the bottom up — the frame first, then the shape, then the opening through which the Aether was invited in, measured, bounded. Built well, a working held because its structure held. Built badly, it leaked, or buckled, or let in more than it was made to carry. It took time to build well. In the tunnels, he'd had time.

Tomorrow he would not. He would have to raise the largest structure of his life in the time it took Kael to work the bolt of a rifle, and then hold it, while men shot at the hillside around him, while his own heart tried to climb out of his throat. The literature had a great deal to say about casting under duress, most of it written by men who had never done it. The gist was: the frame thins. The boundary between disciplined casting and something rawer — a panicked hand thrust into a river — gets very narrow. Very narrow, the literature said, and moved on to another topic, as if that were a complete thought.

He had studied it. He had drilled it. He had never done it for real.

*I am frightened*, he thought, and was surprised by how simple the sentence was. He had expected fear to be more complicated. He turned the thought over and examined it, the way he examined everything, and found that it was not complicated at all. It was just there, sitting in his chest like a cold stone. He could study it. He could not move it.

He got up before dawn and laid out his kit on the floor by the lamp. Bandages. Splints. The flasks of boiled water. Catgut. Laudanum. His notebook. He put each thing in its place and then took it out and put it back again, the ritual that had calmed him before every examination of his student years, before every job with the Society. It did not calm him now. He noted that. He kept going anyway.

---

Pell's scouts went out at first light — two men and a girl of perhaps fifteen, dressed in ordinary country clothes with baskets of eggs and a pair of goats on a string. They would walk south on the Old Cart Road and meet the convoy coming north and stand aside and watch it pass, the way country people did. If its size or shape had changed, they would leave a sign at a cairn on the ridge trail: a stone on top, or two.

"Watching," Pell said, before they left. "That's all. My people don't fight."

"I know," Mira said.

"I want you to *know* it." Pell's eyes were the colour of river stones and held about as much give. "Whatever happens down there, it's yours. Not ours. We'll give you the ground. We'll hide you after. We won't stand in front of you."

"That's fair," Mira said.

It was not a warm exchange. Aldric remembered the first time they had come to this place, and how Pell had shut the gate in their faces, and he thought that this was perhaps warmer than that, in the way that a stone in the sun is warmer than a stone in the shade.

They walked out to their positions in the grey before sunrise. Mira and Sera went west, across the river by the deer stones above the ford and up into the trees on the far ridge. Kael and Aldric went east. At the cairn on the ridge trail there was one stone on top. Nothing had changed.

Kael's nest was a fold in the ground behind a fallen beech, high on the slope, with a clean line down through the trees to the ford. He had found it the evening before, paced the range twice, cut away two branches and left the rest. He settled into it now without a word: the rifle laid on his folded jacket on the trunk, the box of cartridges open at his elbow, the revolver unholstered and set on a flat stone, the knife loose in its sheath. Then he lay still. Aldric had seen Kael be still before. This was a different kind — not waiting so much as becoming part of the hillside.

Aldric's place was forty yards along and a little lower, in a nest of rocks with a view straight across the valley to where the road climbed out of the water and turned up the far slope. He laid his kit out behind him on a flat stone. He set his spellbook beside it, though he would not need to open it. He had the Greycoat field glasses Kael had lent him and he put them to his eyes and looked at the far bank until he could see each stone on the switchback, each tuft of grass, the exact place where he would anchor the first edge of the working and the exact place where he would anchor the last.

Across the valley, on the west ridge, he could not see Mira or Sera at all. He took that as a good sign.

The sun came up behind him. The valley filled with light from the top down, gold on the western trees, then the slope, then the road. Mist lifted off the river in slow white skeins. The water moved over its stones with a sound like low conversation, and a heron stood in the shallows below the ford on one leg, and the whole place was so quiet and so beautiful that for a moment Aldric forgot why he was there.

The air out here had a texture. It was not the thick copper sweetness of the Quarter or the strange, held-breath pressure of the Wilds proper. It was thinner than that and less steady — a moderate presence that came and went, like a tide that couldn't make up its mind. He could feel it moving across his skin in slow surges and ebbs. Every so often it swelled, and the hair on his arms lifted, and the colours of the valley seemed for a moment very slightly brighter. Then it ebbed again. Sera would be feeling it too, on the other ridge. More than he did. Much more.

He checked his watch. He checked the far bank. He checked his watch.

At half past eight the heron lifted out of the shallows all at once and flapped away low over the water, downstream, without a sound.

Aldric lowered the glasses.

From the south, from very far off, so faint at first that he could not be sure it was not the blood in his own ears: the sound of engines.
