# Chapter 4

Sera had been listening to the valley for an hour before the engines came, and the valley had not said the same thing twice.

She sat with her back to a rock on the west ridge, high above the road, with the timber stack below her and to the left — forty great trunks chained against the slope like a held breath. Mira was somewhere along the ridge behind her, fifty yards off, with the field glasses and the flare. Sera couldn't see her. She didn't try. She kept her eyes half-closed and her hands open on her knees and let the Aether come and go across her skin.

It came in pulses. That was the first thing she'd learned, last night, sitting in this same place in the dusk while the others slept. The air out here at the Wild's edge wasn't a river, like the Quarter, or a sea, like the Wilds proper. It was weather. It gathered and broke. One moment the valley was thin and grey and quiet, nothing there but the ordinary smells of leaf mould and river water. The next, something swelled up out of the ground and through the trees and over her like a warm wave, and her teeth tingled and the backs of her eyes went hot and the leaves on the beech below her seemed, for the space of a few breaths, to be outlined in very fine silver wire. Then it ebbed. It always ebbed. There was no rhythm to it that she could find. She'd tried, for an hour, to count. It didn't count.

So she'd stopped counting. Aldric would have kept trying. Aldric would have had a table by now. Sera had decided, sometime after midnight, that she wasn't going to know what was coming. She was going to know what was *here*. Now. And now. And now. And she was going to take whatever was here and use it.

It was a strange, loose, frightening way to stand on a hillside. It was also, she thought, the only honest way.

The engines came down out of the south. She opened her eyes.

---

Kael's shot was the signal.

She heard it — a single flat crack from the east ridge, echoing back off the slope behind her — and then nothing for a moment, as if the whole valley had drawn in its breath. Then the screaming from the river. She made herself wait. Two seconds, Mira had said. Let them stop. Let them bunch. One. Two.

She reached.

The Aether was thin. Of course it was thin. A pulse had just broken and gone, and the air around her was grey and empty, and when she pulled, what came was a trickle — a cupful scooped out of a dry well. She pushed it at the wedges under the timber anyway. The chains creaked. One wedge shifted. Two of the top trunks rolled half a turn and stopped, as if they'd thought about it and decided against.

*No.* She bit down on the word. *No, no —*

Below her the convoy was braking, shouting, the back end of it still rolling down the hill towards the gap. Ten seconds and the last car would be through. Ten seconds and there'd be no back door to close.

She reached again. Harder. Not a push this time — a blow. She shaped it like a fist.

And the Aether came up to meet her.

It was as if the ground had been waiting. A pulse broke under her at the exact instant she pulled, and suddenly there was everything — the warm wave, the silver wire on every leaf, a sound like tearing cloth that only she could hear — and it poured into the shape she'd made and filled it and overfilled it, and the fist went out of her and hit the timber stack like a train.

The chains snapped. All of them. She heard them go one after another, *ping ping ping*, like harp strings cut with shears. The stack bulged. Then the whole slope below her came apart and went down the hill.

It was the loudest thing she had ever heard. Louder than the mill. Wood and stone and earth, roaring and rolling and tumbling, a brown wave with logs in it like matches, all of it pouring down onto the road. Dust went up in a wall. When it cleared, the road behind the convoy was gone, buried under a hill of wreckage, and the last car in the column was sitting with its back wheel against a trunk like a dog nosing a stick.

She'd done it. The door was shut.

She sat back against her rock and found that she was shaking.

That was two, in five seconds. One that was nothing, and one that was everything. She could feel what the second one had cost. It was in her hands, which wouldn't stop trembling, and behind her eyes, where something had gone hot and stayed hot. A long ache had opened somewhere under her breastbone, the ache she'd come to know in the tunnels — the feeling of a well that had been drawn on hard and would take time to fill. She'd spent, in one breath, what she'd meant to spend in five minutes.

*Steady.* Aldric's voice in her head, the one he used when he was frightened and trying not to show it. *Steady, now.*

She took a breath. Let it out. Moved.

---

Her second position was a split in a boulder thirty yards along the ridge, with a clean view down to the valley floor. She went to it on her hands and knees through the bracken, her coat catching on everything, and settled into the gap with her cheek against cold stone.

Down there, the world had come apart into pieces.

The lorry in the ford, stalled, the water piling against it. The far bank — oh. She saw Aldric's wall for the first time, and for a moment she forgot everything else. It stood across the road where it climbed out of the river, tall and wide and shimmering, the trees behind it gone soft and liquid. She could *feel* it, even from here. It had a sound, like a held note on a cello. It had a texture, like glass with a grain in it. It was the most beautiful piece of work she had ever seen another person do, and she understood, looking at it, that every second it stood was a second Aldric was paying for.

And the convoy, trapped between the wall and the river and her hill. Men pouring out of the grey transports. Rifles. They were firing at the ridges now — at hers, and Kael's — blind, not aiming, just pouring bullets into the trees. She heard them come through the leaves above her with a sound like ripping paper. One hit the rock beside her face and went off it with a whine and left a bright grey smear on the stone. She pressed herself lower.

Then she saw Kael.

He came out of the trees at the bottom of the east ridge at a dead run, and she watched him cross the open ground — fifty yards of nothing, gravel and grass, low and fast and weaving — and every step of it she was sure was his last. Something kicked up the gravel behind him. Then he was across, behind a lorry, out of her sight, and she let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

She followed him down the line of vehicles. She couldn't see him most of the time; he was on the river side, hidden by the bulk of the lorries. But she could see where he'd been. Each time he moved on, a little thread of sparks came out from under a tarpaulin or a chassis, hissing. The fuses.

He came round the back of the second lorry, and there was a man in grey. Sera saw it from above, like looking down on a chessboard. The man had a rifle. Kael had a knife. Kael was faster. The man folded up around him and fell.

And behind them both, from the corner of the transport, came a second man, with a pistol out, turning, raising it —

She didn't think. There wasn't time to think. She reached.

A pulse had come in while she wasn't watching, and she caught the crest of it. The push went out of her like a thrown stone. The man with the pistol came off his feet as though a horse had kicked him, and flew backwards, and hit the grey side of the transport with a sound she heard from the ridge, and slid down it, and didn't get up.

Kael touched two fingers to his forehead without looking up. Then he was gone again, round the far side of the lorry.

Sera put her hands flat on the rock to stop them shaking. It didn't work.

Three. That was three. Every time she reached, she felt the well go lower. She could feel the bottom of it now, the way you can feel the bottom of a pond with your toes before you see it.

---

The men below had worked out where she was.

She heard the shouting change, the word going up and down the column, *caster, caster, west ridge*, and then the gun on top of the second transport — a squat ugly thing on a ring — turned and began to hammer at the hillside. Not at her rock. At the place where the timber had come from, forty yards along. Bark and splinters flew off the trees there in sprays. Then it walked along the slope towards her, a little at a time, chewing.

She left her rock and went further along the ridge on her belly. Her elbows were bleeding. She didn't notice until later.

From the new place, she could see the far end of the convoy, the big lorry with the strange shapes under its canvas. A thread of sparks was coming out from under it. And she could see Kael — a glimpse, between the lorries — kneeling at the back of the gun transport, his head bent, his shoulders working, setting the last charge. Fourteen yards off, coming round the front of the same transport, unhurried, a grey-moustached man with a long pistol held out at arm's length.

She saw it all with terrible clarity. She saw Kael finish and turn. She saw the revolver come up in his hand, smooth and fast, the way she had seen it come up a hundred times in practice yards and alleys.

She saw nothing happen.

There was no flash. No crack. The gun just — stayed. Pointed. Silent. And Kael stood there for a heartbeat with his arm out, looking at it, as though somebody had handed him a stranger's hand.

She reached. She was already reaching. But it was too far, and her well was nearly dry, and the Aether was between pulses, grey and thin — and the grey-moustached man fired, and Kael went backwards against the transport's wheel and slid down it out of sight.

Sera heard a sound. High and thin and wordless. It went on and on. She realised, after a while, that it was coming from her.

There was a bang, down there, and a puff of white smoke. The grey-moustached man fell over sideways and lay clutching his leg. Then, between the lorries, she saw Kael again — on his hands and knees, crawling. Away from the convoy. Towards the open ground and the hill. Slowly. So slowly. And behind him on the gravel, a trail of something dark.

*Get up*, she thought at him. *Get up, get up, please get up —*

The big lorry exploded.

---

It didn't sound like an explosion. It sounded like a scream — a high, tearing shriek that went up from under the canvas and through the soles of her feet and into her teeth, and with it a flash of light that was not fire-coloured. It was the colour of the dead zones. Grey-violet. The colour of nothing. For one instant the whole valley went *flat*. The Aether vanished. It didn't ebb; it was simply gone, sucked out of the air as if someone had pulled a plug, and Sera felt it go like a blow to the stomach, like falling through a floor she'd thought was solid.

Then the second lorry went, and that was just fire, a great rolling orange fist of it, and the shockwave came up the hill and slapped her flat against the rock.

When she looked again, the valley was smoke. The Aether was coming back, slowly, seeping in round the edges of where the dead light had been. Three of the lorries were burning. Men were running. And on the gravel strip below the east ridge, thrown twenty yards from where he'd been, Kael was lying on his back against a spur of grey rock, not moving.

He didn't move.

And three men in grey were walking towards him out of the smoke with their rifles up.

Not running. Walking. The way you walk towards something that isn't going to get up. The first of them was already working the bolt of his rifle. They were going to stand over him. They were going to make sure.

Sera stopped thinking.

---

She would try, later, to remember what she did, and she would not be able to. It was not a spell. It had no shape. She did not build a fist or a shove or a wall. She did not choose anything. She didn't reach for the Aether at all — or she did, but the way a drowning woman reaches for the surface, with everything, with her whole body, with no thought at all about what she'd find there.

What she found was the pulse.

It was coming back into the valley — the great slow wave of it rolling back in after the dead light, the biggest she'd felt all morning, bigger than any of them — and she opened herself to it like a door kicked off its hinges, and it came through her. All of it.

It didn't feel like casting. It felt like being a riverbed in a flood.

There was light. She saw it afterwards in Mira's face, so it must have been real — a glare of blue-white coming off her, off her skin and her hair and the rock around her, bright enough to throw shadows in full daylight. The ridge shook under her. Dust went up from the slope in a cloud. Small stones began to roll downhill on their own, then larger ones. The air in front of her went tight and strange and *full*, like the moment before a thunderclap, and then it went out of her —

— and down in the valley the three men walking towards Kael were picked up off the ground and thrown. Not pushed. Thrown. Like leaves in a gale. They went backwards across the gravel twenty yards, thirty, and hit the river, and the river closed over them. The burning lorry nearest Kael rocked up on two wheels and slammed down again a yard to the side. The smoke around him blew away in a ring. For thirty feet in every direction, everything that wasn't nailed down had simply been moved out of the way, and in the middle of the bare circle, Kael lay on his back by his rock, alone.

The backlash hit her a heartbeat later.

It began behind her eyes. A white-hot pressure, as if two thumbs were pushing them out from inside. Her vision went blue at the edges and stayed blue. She could feel her eyes glowing — feel it, as heat on her own cheeks — and when she put up her hand she could see the light from them shining through her fingers, pink and red, in broad daylight.

And then the floor fell out of her.

She was on her knees. She didn't remember kneeling. Her hands were on the rock and they were shaking so hard she could hear her nails scraping on the stone. The ache under her breastbone wasn't an ache any more. It was a hole. Not low. *Gone.* Everything she'd had this morning had gone out of her into that valley, and some of what she hadn't had, too, and the space where it had been was cold and enormous and roaring like a seashell. There was a sound in her ears, a high steady ringing, the same as in the tunnels after the barrier. Louder. Much louder.

*What did I do?* she thought, very slowly. *What was that?*

She didn't know. It hadn't been anything. It had just been everything.

---

Down in the valley, people were moving towards Kael again.

Not the grey men this time. Three men in civilian coats — the researchers, from the little cars at the back of the column. They'd come out from behind their vehicles when the shooting thinned. One of them had a pistol. One had a clipboard, of all things, still clutched to his chest. They were looking around at the burning lorries, the bodies, the wall on the far bank. One was pointing at Kael and shouting something to the others. Gathering. Organising. Deciding what to do.

And on the east ridge — she saw him; she would never know how, through the smoke and the blue haze at the edges of her eyes — a tall thin figure had come out of the rocks and was standing very still, staring down at the valley floor.

*Aldric.* Holding his wall. Looking at Kael. And the three men between them.

She had nothing left. She knew she had nothing left. She reached anyway.

It was like trying to lift a bucket from a dry well with a broken rope. She scraped at the bottom of herself. There was almost nothing there. A teaspoon. A few drops. She shaped it as small as she could and sent it down the hill at the researchers' feet — not to hurt them, she couldn't have hurt them, just to *move* them —

The gravel under the three men shifted. Just a little. Like a rug tugged. One of them sat down hard. The other two staggered and caught each other. The one with the pistol dropped it. They looked at the ground and at each other and then up at the west ridge, and whatever they saw up there, they didn't like it. They ran for the cars.

It was enough. It was just enough.

Sera filled her lungs with air that tasted of smoke and copper and blood from her own nose, and screamed across the valley with everything she had left.

"*GO!*"

On the east ridge, the tall figure was already moving.

She saw the wall on the far bank shiver. It flickered once, like a candle in a draught, and then it went out — not falling, just *ceasing*, the far slope suddenly hard and clear and ordinary again where it had swum a moment before. And Aldric was coming down the east ridge, running, not on the deer trail but straight down the slope through the bracken, slipping, falling, getting up, his coat flying out behind him, running in a way she had never seen him run, in a way she hadn't known he could.

He had let it go. The wall. All that work. He'd let it go.

Sera sat back on her heels. The world pulsed. In, out. Bright, dim. Her vision had narrowed to a long blue tunnel with the valley at the end of it, and the tunnel was getting longer.

There was a hand on her shoulder.

She didn't know when Mira had come. She hadn't heard her. But Mira was there, kneeling beside her in the bracken, the field glasses round her neck and the flare pistol in her belt, unfired. One of Mira's hands was on her shoulder. The other had found her wrist and was holding it, two fingers pressed to the inside, feeling for the pulse.

"Sera." Mira's voice was very calm. It was the calmest Sera had ever heard it, and she knew what that meant. "Stay with me. Breathe. Just breathe."

"Are you hit?" Mira asked. "Sera. Are you hurt?"

"Tired," Sera said. It was the only word she could find. It wasn't the right one. There wasn't a right one.

"Okay." Mira let out a breath. "Okay." She got an arm under Sera's shoulders and held her up. "Stay with me. Aldric's got him. You hear? Aldric's got him. Stay with me."

Sera leaned against her and watched. It was all she could do.

Down in the valley, through the smoke, a small dark figure in a long coat burst out of the trees at the foot of the east ridge and ran across the open gravel towards the place where Kael lay, alone in his bare circle, under the burning sky.
