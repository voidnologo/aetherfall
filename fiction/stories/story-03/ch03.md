# Chapter 3

The Galvanic hum hit Sera two blocks from the foundry.

It always started the same way, with pressure behind her eyes and a dryness in the air that had nothing to do with weather. The Factory Quarter broadcast on a frequency most people couldn't hear and Sera couldn't ignore. It pushed the Aether down and smothered it, turning the sweet copper current of the Veilwright Quarter into something thin and distant, like music heard through a wall. She hated it here. It wasn't the place she hated but the sensation, a basic sense pressed flat, like breathing through gauze that someone kept pulling tighter.

The foundry was as they'd left it months ago. Dark windows. Cold smokestacks against a sky that hadn't decided between clouds and stars. Chained loading-bay doors, the chains newer than last time, she noticed. Someone was keeping up the pretence that the building was merely abandoned.

They went round the west side in single file, through industrial rubbish that smelled of rust and old oil. Fels's instructions had been precise: the storm drain behind the third retaining wall, hidden by a sheet of corrugated metal bolted at the top and swinging free at the bottom. Kael went first, knife in hand, then Mira, then Aldric with his satchel cinched tight against his body. Sera went last and pulled the metal sheet back into place behind her.

The drain was wide enough to walk upright, though not comfortably. The air changed at once, cooler and damper, and the Galvanic hum receded with every step down. The lanterns made the walls orange and close.

Then the Aether came back.

There was nothing gradual about it, none of the slow days-long build she'd felt approaching the Wilds. Here it returned with a lurch. One step there was silence. The next, the copper taste flooded her mouth, her compass spun against her collarbone, and the air thickened into something she could almost feel on her skin. The change was so abrupt that she stumbled and caught herself against the tunnel wall. The brick was warm under her palm.

"You all right?" Kael, ahead of her, didn't turn. He was listening.

"Zone shift," she said. "Sharp one."

They kept moving. Ten steps on, the Aether dropped away again like a door slamming. The Galvanic hum pressed back in, drier and closer than on the surface. Sera's teeth ached. Her compass snapped to a hard heading and held. It wasn't north, or anywhere that mattered on a map, but some buried line in the foundry's foundations above them. The needle committed to its wrong answer with a rigidity that told her as much as the sweet copper current had. It said she was in a strong Galvanic pocket, the way a cat's fixed stare tells you there's something in the wall.

"The gradient here isn't a slope," she said, because Aldric would want to know and because saying it aloud helped her understand it. "It's a staircase. Sharp transitions. Like the two forces are fighting for territory inch by inch."

"Contested zone," Aldric murmured, half to himself. She heard his pencil scratching; he was writing as he walked, the field notebook balanced on his forearm. "The readings from the Harker job showed a gentle transition. This is fundamentally different."

The upper tunnels were recognizable from that first job: old brick-lined drainage channels and iron grating, the industrial bones of a water system older than the Tear. But in the lantern light the changes since their last visit showed. The crystal growths on the walls were thicker and denser than before. They followed the tunnel's geometry in patterns that looked less like mineral deposits than something planted. The blue-white glow inside them was bright enough that the tunnel ahead was faintly lit even before the lanterns reached it.

They came to a section where the ceiling had partly fallen. Old brickwork sagged, and rubble choked the passage. It was awkward rather than impassable. Kael was already working out the best route over when Sera reached for the Aether.

It was instinct. Here was a problem she could solve with a shove of force: clear the debris, open the path. She reached as she always did, not with her hands or with words but with the part of her that had been touching the current since before she had a name for it. The river. The vast invisible thing that ran beneath the world like blood beneath skin. You didn't command it. You opened yourself to it and shaped what came through.

Nothing came through.

She pulled. The Aether barely answered, a trickle where she'd expected a stream. She pushed the trickle at the rubble and felt it arrive like a breath against a boulder. The debris shifted. Dust fell. One brick rocked three inches to the left and settled.

That was all. A cough where she'd reached for a shout.

She was standing in a Galvanic pocket. The staircase gradient had dipped without warning, the zone shifting mid-tunnel, and she had reached for magic in air that was pressing it flat. What she'd summoned was hardly worth the effort, a weak shove that achieved nothing, like striking a match in a rainstorm.

Kael glanced back at her without comment or judgment. He climbed over the rubble by hand, finding footholds in the broken brick with the practised economy of a man who'd spent years getting through broken things. Mira followed. Aldric ducked through a gap, and his satchel caught on a jutting brick. Sera went last, hauling herself over the debris with her arms. The effort reminded her that she was a slight woman with a belt knife and not much strength in her arms, and that magic wasn't always there to make up the difference.

She swallowed the frustration. It tasted like the Galvanic hum, metallic and flat.

Round the next bend the Aether flooded back. It was like stepping from a cold room into sunlight: a rush of warmth, the copper taste filling her mouth, her compass spinning so fast she could hear brass ticking against its housing. Her skin prickled. The crystals on the walls blazed blue-white, and for a moment the tunnel was so bright the lanterns were pointless.

"Here," she said. She needed to stop, just briefly. "Give me a moment."

The others waited. Kael watched the tunnel ahead. Mira watched Sera. Aldric watched his instruments, and whatever they told him made his eyebrows climb.

Sera closed her eyes and let the Aether wash through her.

Nobody else understood this part. Aldric studied the Aether from outside, cataloguing its effects with the patient precision of a man describing a river he'd never swum in. Kael saw it as a tool at best and a danger at worst. Mira saw a resource to be managed. They knew what it did. They didn't know what it felt like.

It felt like standing in a river you couldn't see. The current pushed against you, warm and vast and indifferent to your plans. You could reach into it and pull, and something always came. But the river didn't care what you wanted. It gave what it gave, sometimes a trickle, sometimes a flood. The skill lay in shaping what arrived, not in commanding it.

She'd been doing this since childhood, before she had words for it, before anyone told her it was called the Aether, before the world decided it was dangerous and wonderful and worth fighting over. When she was small, things near her drifted when she was angry. Her mother's teacups slid across the table. A door slammed when nobody touched it. She thought she was haunted. Then she thought she was broken. Then Old Ren in the Quarter found her and told her what she was, and the world rearranged itself round a new truth. The river was always there. She'd simply been born knowing how to swim.

She opened her eyes. Ahead the tunnel shone with crystal light, the blue-white glow pulsing in a rhythm that was almost regular. Like breathing, if the thing breathing were the size of a building.

"Moving," Kael said. They moved.

---

The collapse announced itself by sound before sight: a deep structural groan from the ceiling, then a crack that ran through the brickwork like a fault opening. The crystals along the walls flared. The current surged so sharply that Sera gasped. The pocket they'd entered was suddenly, violently alive, its power leaping from strong to something she had no word for.

"Back up!" Kael was already moving towards them, putting himself between the team and a ceiling shedding brick in chunks. It wasn't a true collapse. The tunnel was moulting, its old skin shearing away from the crystal underlayer replacing it. Debris rained down. A brick struck Aldric's shoulder, and he stumbled sideways and caught himself against the wall.

Sera reached.

The Aether was right there, no thin trickle this time but a flood. She pulled for a barrier, a shield across the top of the passage that would deflect the falling stone long enough for them to get clear.

What came was not what she'd reached for.

The power rushed through her faster than she could shape it. It was like cupping water from a fire hose: you got what you wanted and ten times more. The magic answered as it always did, on its own terms, and today its terms were *everything*. She felt the barrier forming and tried to hold it to the shape she meant, a flat shield, ceiling-wide, temporary. But the current was too strong and her grip too small. The shape escaped her and grew into something else.

The barrier erupted outward. Instead of a shield across the ceiling, a dome of rippling force sealed the whole section of tunnel round them, floor to ceiling, wall to wall. It didn't just stop things. It pushed. The shearing ceiling was slammed upward mid-collapse and held there, the falling brick pinned against a surface that met its weight with an equal force. Where the dome met the walls they bulged outward. The crystal skin strained, and the old brickwork cracked visibly into a wider cavity than it had filled a second before. The blue-white energy was visible and humming, and its light made the crystals look dull. Inside the dome the air was still and warm and faintly electric, the moment before a thunderstorm that would never arrive.

They were safe, completely and dramatically safe. The dome was beautiful.

The cost hit a second later.

Sera's knees buckled. This wasn't a stumble. It was a surrender; her legs simply declined to hold her. Exhaustion poured through her veins like ice water, a draining so sudden and deep that for a moment she couldn't tell whether she was standing or falling. She was falling. She caught herself on one knee and one palm against the tunnel floor, arms shaking. It wasn't fear. Her body was telling her mind that it had just written a cheque on reserves it didn't have.

It felt specific: a hollowing, as though the spell had reached in and taken everything from the top drawer and most of the second. It was less pain than absence. Where her energy had been there was emptiness now, and the emptiness had weight.

Then came the burn.

A flash of heat behind her eyes, sharp enough to make her gasp. Her vision whited out for half a second. It wasn't blindness but overexposure, as if something too bright had gone off inside her skull. When it cleared, Kael was staring at her.

His face told her what her eyes were doing before she could feel it. It wasn't the concern she was used to. It was older and more complicated: a man watching something he loved do something he couldn't protect it from.

"Your eyes," he said.

She knew. She could feel it now, a heat behind the irises and a brightness she couldn't turn off. This wasn't the faint ring from the Wilds, the blue-white discolouration that had become her new normal and that she'd been pretending was cosmetic. This was brighter, a glow visible in the tunnel's ambient light that would draw eyes from across a room. Light through stained glass, if the glass were her.

It would fade over minutes, maybe hours, receding by degrees like cooling embers, too slowly to watch. The ringing in her ears settled into a high thin tone nobody else could hear, and her hands wouldn't keep still.

"I'm fine," she said, because that was what you said.

Aldric was watching her with the expression she'd learned to read as professional concern hiding personal worry: eyebrows level, mouth set, eyes cataloguing. She knew what he was doing. He was measuring her against whatever baseline he kept in his head and adding this to the running tally he thought she didn't know about. She'd rather he shouted at her. Shouting she could argue with. Silent observation made her feel like a specimen.

The dome held. She could feel it drawing on her, a shallow but constant drain, like a tap left running. Through the drain she could also feel what the dome was doing to the tunnel: pushing outward against brick and crystal, holding a whole collapse in suspension. Ceiling, debris, the strained shape of the walls, all of it penned behind the force.

"I can't just let it go," she said thinly. "When I drop this, everything it's holding comes down."

Kael understood faster than she expected. He was already looking past her with his lantern raised, studying the tunnel beyond the dome's edge, past the bulged section and the shearing ceiling to where the walls still ran clean.

"Twenty feet," he said. "Past the bend. The stone there hasn't moved."

"We go," Mira said. "Sera holds. She drops it when we're clear."

They moved. Kael slipped past the curve of the dome along the narrow margin where the force thinned against the wall. Mira went second with a hand on Aldric's shoulder, steering him through. Aldric clutched the satchel to his chest, his eyes on the strained ceiling with the horrified professional interest of a man calculating loads in a structure about to stop existing.

Sera went last. As she passed the dome's edge she let it thin behind her, the outer rim dissolving in her wake so she could step through without bringing the whole thing down. She couldn't have described how. The same instinct that had always told her the shape of a spell now told her where the shape could give and where it couldn't. The partial release brought its own exhaustion, finer than the first, in a muscle she hadn't known she was using.

They reached stable ground. Kael's lantern lit a stretch of tunnel the pressure hadn't touched: solid floor, walls meeting the ceiling at honest angles. Twenty feet back, the dome still glowed, a blister of light in the dark.

"Now," Kael said.

She let it go.

The sound was less a crash than a long, tearing sigh as the held collapse relaxed into itself. The bulged walls breathed inward. The suspended ceiling resumed its interrupted fall. Brick rained down. Crystal shattered in bright arcs that flared and died. A gust of displaced air rolled past them, smelling of dust and the electric copper of spent magic. When the dust plume reached the lantern, the light went small and orange.

When it cleared, the tunnel behind them was sealed with brick and crystal rubble, filling the space the dome had held open. They weren't going back that way.

"Forward," Kael said. He wasn't suggesting it. He was acknowledging it.

Sera got to her feet. The world tilted, then steadied. Everything felt harder, the air heavier, her thoughts wading through something thick. Next time she reached for the Aether it would be through fog. She knew it as you know your legs are heavy after running, as a fact rather than a decision.

"I'm walking," she said.

Kael held her gaze a moment longer. Then he turned, knife in hand, and led them deeper.

---

The tunnels went on. The crystals thickened and the light brightened until Kael capped the lanterns and they walked by the tunnel's own glow, blue-white, pulsing, alive. In the upper tunnels the pale material had filled the gaps between bricks. Down here it had replaced the brickwork entirely, seamless and warm. The drainage works were disappearing, and in their place were corridors: architecture built by no one for a purpose no one understood.

Sera navigated by feel. Pathfinding didn't cost her as casting did. It was a sensitivity, not an expense. She could feel the tunnels ahead as you feel a draught from an open window: direction, distance, a sense of what was open and what was closed. The Aether ran through the passages like water through a river system, and she read its grain as a riverman reads current against rock.

The compass at her throat spun in lazy, insistent circles, ticking against its housing. It wasn't guiding her. It was a barometer, telling her what the air already had: that the Aether here was strong and raw and running thick. She ignored it and followed the current.

That she could do this here, underground, beneath a Galvanic factory, in territory that should have suppressed everything she was, frightened her without surprising her. The rules down here weren't the rules above. The staircase gradient that had frustrated her in the upper tunnels had given way to something deeper and steadier. The Aether here was thick and strange, neither the wild vastness of the forest nor the moderate hum of the Quarter. It was structured and purposeful, a current that knew exactly where it was going.

She kept her hand off the compass and followed the feel, and tried not to think about what it meant that the Aether down here was less like a river than a road.

A crystal formation on the wall shifted as they passed. It was barely perceptible, a slow adjustment like a sleeper turning over. The tunnel was still growing.

Sera watched it and felt its pulse quicken, as if it had noticed them noticing.

They walked on into the deep.
