# Chapter 1

Corrigan Fels walked into the Wet Ember like a man who owned the place and was considering selling.

Kael saw him in the mirror behind the bar. In his first week as a regular he'd worked out the angles: with Hesper's bottles arranged just so, the mirror gave him the door without turning his head. Fels came through it in a good coat with an expression of mild interest, as if he'd wandered in by accident and was pleasantly surprised by what he'd found. He ordered a drink from Hesper with a smile she did not return. He paid with a coin too large for the tab and didn't ask for change.

Kael's hand moved under the table to the revolver on his thigh and rested there, not drawing.

"Don't," Mira said quietly, without looking up from her drink. She'd seen it too, without needing the mirror. Mira read rooms as weather-watchers read clouds, and the pressure had just dropped.

Fels collected his drink and walked past their table without a glance. He took the booth behind them and settled into the corner seat with his back to the wall. That was Kael's preferred position, which meant Fels had either cased the place in advance or shared the same instincts. Neither was comforting.

The Wet Ember went on round them. Near the door two factory workers from the Galvanic district were arguing about last season's boxing, one of them gesturing with a beer that slopped onto the table at every emphatic point. A woman in a frayed shawl was reading a broadsheet by the coal fire, her lips moving silently. Hesper polished a glass with her one arm and watched the room with the flat attention of a woman who'd seen everything at least twice.

It was a normal evening, except for the man in the booth behind them. They had torn his smuggling operation apart during the Ashwick Job, and three months later he had apparently decided to discuss it in person at their local.

"I think," Fels said to the air behind their heads, "that we should have a conversation."

Kael didn't turn. Mira did, slowly, one arm draped over the back of her chair, her posture suggesting she'd been expecting this. She hadn't. But Mira's talent was making improvisation look like preparation.

"Mr. Fels." Her voice was pleasant and measured and gave away nothing. "You're a long way from the docks."

"I'm a long way from a lot of things." Fels sipped his drink. "The job your people did for the Greycoats four months ago. The missing researcher, the foundry tunnels. You disrupted a shipping route I spent three years building. It cost me product, money, and a relationship with a supplier who doesn't extend second chances." He smiled, and the smile stopped at his mouth. "I've been thinking about that."

"We were under contract," Kael said, still facing forward. "The job crossed your operation by coincidence."

"Coincidence." Fels tasted the word as if testing a vintage. "That's one interpretation. The other is that a four-person Society with more ambition than sense blundered into a carefully managed enterprise and set fire to it on the way out. The interpretation doesn't much matter. The cost is the same."

The room noise went on. The boxing argument reached a point of shouted agreement. Hesper set down one glass and picked up another. Nobody was paying attention to the quiet conversation at the back of the room, which was how conversations like this were meant to go.

Fels took a clove cigarette from a slim silver case and lit it with a match struck on the underside of the table. The smell reached Kael a moment later, clove and something darker, imported. "I know about the mill," Fels said. "I know you went into the Wilds on a Covenant commission and came back with information several factions would pay handsomely for. I know Inspector Laine has been your contact at the Greycoat Authority, and I know what you told her about the foundry tunnels doesn't match what you actually found down there." He drew on the cigarette and breathed a thin stream of smoke at the ceiling. "I know the Ashworth Collective tried to kill you in the forest, and I know you didn't report it to anyone who could act on it."

Kael felt the temperature of the conversation change. It wasn't a threat yet. It was the inventory before the invoice.

"I'm not here to collect on a debt," Fels went on. "You don't owe me anything, in any traditional sense. But I have information about your operations that would interest people you can't afford to have interested. The Ashworth Collective. Ironwork Solutions. Others." He tapped ash into the tray on his table. "I'm here to offer you a job. The compensation is that I don't sell what I know to the people who would use it worst."

Mira's expression hadn't changed. Kael could see enough of her profile to read the calculation behind it. She wasn't wondering whether Fels was bluffing; he wasn't. She was weighing which responses left the Society standing when the dust settled.

"What's the job?" she asked.

"Something went missing in the tunnels under the Ashworth Foundry. The same tunnels your people walked through during the Harker business. A transport case, sealed and marked, part of a shipment we were moving through the old drainage route. Three weeks ago the route became impassable. Conditions down there have intensified past anything our runners can handle. Passages that were open have closed. The environment is..." He paused to choose the word with the care of a man who knew words were currency. "Hostile. We sent people down. They came back with headaches and stories I don't pay them to tell me."

"And you want us to go and get it," Kael said.

"You have two people who can handle what's down there. We don't. It's a straightforward retrieval: go in, find the case, bring it back. I'll provide the access point and what intelligence we have on current conditions." Fels stubbed out the cigarette half smoked. "When the case is delivered, I'll share something you need. Intelligence on the Ashworth Collective's plans for the mill site. They're not done with you, and what they're planning next is considerably more ambitious than last time."

He had laid the carrot and the stick on the table together. It was professional and clean, the kind of offer that only sounded reasonable because the alternative was worse.

Kael looked in the mirror behind the bar. Fels's reflection looked back at him: a pleasant face, a good coat, the calm of a man who had come to deliver terms, not to negotiate them.

"I'll tell you something for free," Fels said, standing. He left his glass half finished, a man who'd said what he came to say and didn't linger. "I don't make threats I can't carry out, and I don't make offers I can't fulfil. You have until tomorrow evening. Send word to the usual channels."

He walked past their table again without looking at them. The door opened, the factory noise from the street came in, and it left with him.

The booth behind them was empty. The thin smell of clove smoke lingered.

Kael looked at Mira. She was already three moves ahead. He could see it in the set of her jaw and in her fingers turning her glass on the table in slow quarter-turns. Sera and Aldric weren't here tonight. Sera was at the flat working on her journal, and Aldric was at the public library chasing a citation he'd been muttering about for a week. They would need to be told, and to decide.

But the decision was already narrowing. Kael could feel it, as a room narrows when someone bars the exits. Fels hadn't asked. He'd informed. And what he knew about the Society's operations was a loaded weapon pointed at everything they'd built.

"He's not bluffing," Kael said.

"No," Mira agreed. "He's not."

The coal fire cracked. Hesper served the woman with the broadsheet. The boxing argument wound down into companionable disagreement. The Wet Ember went on, as taverns do, indifferent to the small catastrophes at the tables in the back.

Kael picked up his drink and found it had gone warm. He drank it anyway. The evening was going to be long, and the taste didn't matter.
