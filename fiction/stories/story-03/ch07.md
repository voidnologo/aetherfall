# Chapter 7

Mira chose the coffee house. The Wet Ember was theirs, and she didn't want Fels in it again. She picked a place in Central instead, where the Greycoat Authority's presence kept the streets civil and businessmen met to discuss arrangements that needed witnesses but not listeners. It had a tiled floor and a zinc counter, and smelled of roasted beans, tobacco smoke, and the faint mineral sharpness of the city water they used in the press. It was normal and bright, the kind of place where the worst that happened was an overcharge on the bill.

Fels was there when they arrived. Only Mira and Kael had come. Aldric and Sera stayed at the flat. Aldric was writing, his research journal open on the kitchen table, his pen moving with the intensity of a man assembling a puzzle from pieces that didn't quite fit. Sera was asleep. She'd been asleep for fourteen hours when Kael left, and Aldric had checked on her twice: pulse, pupils, the signs he knew to look for when a caster had spent too much. Not in danger, he said. Just empty, in a way that sleep refilled slowly, like a well that needed time and stillness to rise back to its level.

Fourteen hours. That was the cost of the barrier, the Kinesis casts, the artifact's touch and the zone change on the way out. Kael had watched her walk home from the foundry as carefully as someone crossing ice. Every step was considered and every movement managed; her reserves were so low that even walking needed conscious supervision. She'd made it to her bed and been asleep in minutes. It was the deepest sleep he'd seen in her, heavy rather than peaceful, the sleep of a body rebuilding what the magic had taken.

At the coffee-house door Kael checked his revolver. It was habit: reach, feel, assess. This time the result was different from the tunnels. The mechanism snapped with its usual precision. The cylinder spun clean and the action was crisp, the weapon answering his hand as it always had.

He stood in the doorway a moment, thinking it through. Down in the tunnels the revolver hadn't worked properly. It hadn't jammed or broken, but it had been sluggish and uncertain, damped by the same force that fed Sera's magic and pushed against Aldric's control. Up here, in the Galvanic-leaning air of Central, it was itself again. The engineering the city ran on, the arc lamps and trams and factories, fed the same physics his revolver depended on.

He'd always known in the abstract that magic and machinery didn't sit comfortably together. But knowing it from a briefing was different from feeling your weapon turn reluctant in your hand, trusting a thing you'd trusted for seven years and having that trust answered with hesitation. His revolver depended on its environment, just as Sera's casting did. The difference was that Sera knew it. She lived with it, worked round it, and accepted it as the price of her gift. Kael was only now understanding that the price ran both ways.

He walked in. The revolver sat on his hip, working and solid and his. He kept his hand near it anyway.

---

Fels sat at a corner table with a clove cigarette and a cup of black coffee, looking like any prosperous tradesman meeting associates for a morning appointment. His coat was good and his collar clean. He rose half an inch as Mira approached, a courtesy so slight it might have been an accident, except that nothing Fels did was accidental.

"Ms. Cade. Mr. Dunn." He gestured to the two chairs opposite. The cigarette drew a small circle in the air, an invitation that was also choreography. Fels managed physical space as Mira managed information, every gesture calibrated and every angle chosen.

They sat. Mira ordered coffee. Kael ordered nothing.

"The case was delivered," Fels said. He already knew; his crew would have reported within the hour. "My people confirm the seals were intact. I'm satisfied."

He didn't ask what was inside, or what they'd seen in the tunnels. Kael heard the omissions clearly. Fels already knew what the case held, since he'd been moving it through those tunnels on purpose. And he didn't want to know what the Society had learned, because knowledge shared was leverage lost. What the Society carried in their heads was their problem. Fels had got what he paid for.

"The intelligence," Mira said. "As agreed."

Fels tapped ash from his cigarette. "The Ashworth Collective is assembling a second expedition to the mill site. Not Ironwork alone. They've contracted two more Societies, freelance outfits with no patron ties. Hired muscle. The expedition is planned for within the month." He said it as he said everything, pleasantly and precisely, as if discussing commodity prices. "They mean to breach the mill's defences with industrial-grade equipment. Field dampeners. Resonance disruptors. Portable dead-zone generators. Heavy tools built to suppress Aetheric activity by force." He drew on the cigarette. "The Collective's working theory is that the artifact inside the mill is of Engine origin. A wild Galvanic manifestation, not Aetheric. Their research division wants it badly enough to militarize the retrieval."

Mira took this in without visible reaction, which meant she was reacting a great deal behind the mask. Kael watched Fels.

"The artifact in the case," Kael said. "The one you were moving through the tunnels. What is it?"

Fels's smile didn't change. It was a good smile, warm and collegial, the kind that worked at dinner parties and in business negotiations and across the table from people you were methodically manipulating. "You completed the job. I've paid the agreed price. Everything else is above your commission." He tapped ash again, precisely enough to punctuate. "I'll tell you this for free, because I like you. Stop asking about the tunnels. What's down there isn't something you want to be curious about. Not because it's dangerous. Because the people who are already curious about it don't share well."

The warning was genuine. Kael recognized it as he recognized all genuine warnings, by the moment the performance dropped. Fels's smile thinned. His eyes, which had been warm and collegial since they sat down, went flat for half a second. The real man showed through the surface like a rock in shallow water, and the real man was not smiling.

He wasn't protecting the Syndicate's interests. He was protecting the Society from them. The distinction mattered. Kael filed it where he kept the small, important things people revealed about themselves without meaning to.

Fels finished his coffee and stood. He left money on the table, enough for their drinks as well, a courtesy that was also a statement about who held the power. "A pleasure," he said, and meant it as men like Fels mean things: partly, strategically, and aware that the next meeting might go differently.

He left. The clove smell lingered. The tiled floor shone clean in the morning light from the windows.

---

Kael drank the coffee Fels had paid for. It was good coffee. Central had good everything: the Galvanic infrastructure kept the district running smoothly, the businesses prosperous and the streets well lit. The hum was constant here, a background frequency most people never noticed and Kael had learned to read like a room. It said: *this is where the power is. This is where the machinery works, and the world runs on rules you can trust.*

He'd done the job. He'd worked for the Syndicate. The line he'd drawn when he left the Greycoats, between what he would do for pay and what he wouldn't, had bent. It hadn't broken. And the bending bothered him more than breaking would have, because a man who broke knew where he stood. A man who bent had to wonder how far he'd go before he straightened, and how many more times the line would flex before it stopped being a line and became a suggestion.

The coffee tasted like coffee. The world didn't end when you compromised, and that was the worst of it: the ordinariness. The morning went on. The trams ran, merchants opened their shops, Greycoats walked their beats, and nothing anywhere acknowledged that Kael Dunn had crossed a line he'd sworn not to cross, for defensible reasons, for people he cared about. The universe didn't care about your moral architecture. It only asked what you'd do next.

Mira was watching him over the rim of her cup, waiting. She knew what was coming. She always knew, which was part of the problem.

"You edited the briefing," Kael said. "Yesterday morning. You left out the part where he threatened us."

She didn't flinch or look away. Mira met confrontation as she met everything, directly, with the composure of a woman who had rehearsed every possible version of every conversation and was now performing the one she'd prepared for.

"I managed it," she said.

"You filtered it."

"That's the same thing."

"No," Kael said. "It isn't."

The silence between them had a shape. It was the shape of every conversation they hadn't had since the Wet Ember. He had watched Mira smooth the edges off hard truths, and noticed the gap between what happened and what she reported. Her gift for managing information had slid from asset to liability and back so quickly that calling it out would have seemed petty. It didn't now. It was a pattern, and patterns had consequences. The consequence of this one was that the team was working from a version of reality Mira had curated for them without their knowledge or consent.

He wasn't angry. That was the complicated part. He understood why she'd done it. The team performed better believing they had a choice, and morale mattered. Mira's instinct to protect the people round her by controlling what they knew wasn't malice. It was care, expressed through the only method she trusted. She was good at it, and she'd been doing it their whole partnership.

He just needed her to know that he saw it.

Mira set down her cup. "The team needed to function. They needed to feel this was a decision, not a corner. I made a judgment about what information served that."

"And you didn't think I'd notice."

"I knew you'd notice." She paused, measured as all her pauses were. "I also knew you'd understand why."

"Understanding isn't agreeing."

She looked at him a long moment. The noise of the coffee house filled the space between them: the clink of cups, the murmur of conversations that weren't about trust, the hiss of the espresso machine. They were ordinary sounds in an ordinary place, where two people were establishing for the first time exactly where a crack ran, one that had been forming since before either of them had a name for it.

"I'll keep doing it," Mira said. It wasn't a challenge. It was a statement of fact, delivered with an honesty that was its own kind of cruelty. "Not because I don't respect you. Because I think it works."

"I know," Kael said. "That's the part that worries me."

Neither of them was wrong. The crack wasn't a disagreement about facts. It was about methods, territory where reasonable people could reach different conclusions from the same evidence and both be justified. Mira managed information because that was how she kept people alive. Kael demanded openness because that was how he kept himself honest. The two positions could coexist, but not comfortably, and the discomfort would grow.

Kael drained his cup. Mira left hers half full. Fels's money was still on the table, held down by the rim of the saucer, enough for their drinks and a generous tip, an account that needed nothing more from them. They stood and walked out into the Central morning, back towards Harrier Street side by side. Their trust hadn't been broken. It had been named, and naming a thing, Kael knew, was the first step in deciding what to do with it.

The Galvanic hum came from the west and the Aetheric sweetness from the east, with the gradient running between them under their feet. Beneath that, deeper still, in tunnels that had been tunnels and were becoming something else, the glowing veins pulsed in a rhythm nobody above ground was counting.
